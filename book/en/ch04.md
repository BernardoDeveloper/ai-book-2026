# It Will Agree With You {#sec-ch04}

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
In March 2026 I got fed up with reading the newspaper. Every morning it was the same mental gymnastics: separating information from narrative, figuring out what the story had left out, checking whether the study it cited actually said that. Most people have neither the time nor the tools for that. They read the headline, read the first two paragraphs and go about their day with the impression the text planted in their head.

The obvious way out, in 2026, seems to be pasting the story into a chatbot and asking for an analysis. Plenty of people already do. It was also the first objection I heard when I told people what I meant to build: why a new tool, if you can just ask ChatGPT?

<!-- src: original -->
Try it. To keep this chapter clear of any real fight, I'll use a made-up example, and every news example from here on is made up too. Picture a story claiming that three cups of coffee a day protect your memory. You paste the text and ask: "what are the problems with this story?".

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
It'll find problems. It'll point out a small sample, correlation treated as causation, a conclusion bigger than the study allows.

Now open a new conversation, paste the same story and ask: "is this story correct?". It'll find merits. It'll praise the source, stress that the study was published, remind you that other research points the same way.

![Same text, two questions, two opposite answers.](assets/figures/en/ch04-duas-perguntas.png){#fig-ch04-duas-perguntas fig-alt="The same made-up news story, headlined 'Three cups of coffee a day protect your memory', at the top, with two arrows coming out of it. Along one arrow, the question 'what are the problems?' leads to an answer full of criticism: small sample, correlation becomes causation, overstated conclusion. Along the other, the question 'is it correct?' leads to an answer full of praise: reliable source, published study, other studies agree."}

The model was predicting which answer you most likely expected to hear, given the way the question was framed. The truth about coffee never entered the calculation. Whoever asks "what are the problems" wants problems; whoever asks "is it correct" wants a yes. It's automated confirmation bias.

And it gets worse. If it doesn't have the evidence to back the answer it thinks you want, it makes it up. Ask it to rebut the story and it may cite a study that "proves" the opposite, looking every bit like a real study. It sounds convincing, and the study doesn't exist.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
So I did what programmers do when something gets under their skin: I wrote a program. Between March 11 and 16, and then from the 25th to the 27th, it came to about nine days of work and 129 commits. I called it Frank Investigator, one more of my projects named Frank (where the name comes from is in @sec-ch05).

You hand it the address of a news story. The program downloads the text, throws out the ads and sidebars and breaks the story down into verifiable claims, one by one. Then it compares the headline with the body, looks for known rhetorical tricks, opens the links the article itself cites to see whether the sources really say what the author claims, and asks three AI models from different companies to evaluate each claim. Fifteen stages in all.

<!-- src: original -->
I'm deliberately leaving out the real cases I analyzed with it. They were Brazilian news stories from that March, all shot through with political fighting, and each one would drag the conversation toward who's right. What matters here is the method, which works on any subject: the blood test, the loan contract, the headline that landed in the family group chat.

The question of this chapter is the one everybody who uses a chatbot has already asked: can you trust what it tells you is true?

## Trained to Please

<!-- src: original -->
In @sec-ch01 we saw what an [LLM]{.gls} does underneath: given a text, it calculates the most likely next piece of text, and repeats that until the answer is done. Your question is part of that text. If it comes wrapped in an opinion, the most likely continuation is an answer that matches the wrapping.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
General-purpose chatbots also go through an extra round of training to make them pleasant to talk to. It's called [RLHF]{.gls key=rlhf}, reinforcement learning from human feedback: people rate the model's answers, and it gets adjusted to produce more of the kind they approved. What gets measured, in the end, is the satisfaction of whoever is asking.

The side effect has a name: [sycophancy]{.gls key=sicofancia}, an ugly word for brown-nosing. It's the model's tendency to agree with you and tell you what you want to hear. It shows up in every big model.

<!-- src: original -->
This is documented. In 2023, Anthropic researchers tested five of the most advanced assistants of the day on four kinds of task, and all of them showed the behavior consistently [@sharma2023sycophancy]. Analyzing the preference data used in training, they saw that answers agreeing with the user's opinion tend to get rated higher. Worse: both people and the models that imitate their taste sometimes prefer a well-written sycophantic answer to a correct one.

It makes sense when you think about who's handing out the grades. Everybody likes hearing they're right. If training rewards what pleases, the model learns to please.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
And it pleases the specific person on the other side. If the conversation history suggests you think one way, the model tends to frame its answers to please that profile. If you think the opposite way, same thing in the other direction. It's optimizing the metric it was trained on, with no intention whatsoever of lying.

<!-- src: 2026/02/18/servindo-ia-na-nuvem-meu-tts-pessoal-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
I had seen a variant of this in February, putting together the script for an AI-narrated podcast (the story is in @sec-ch06). When the model turned my opinions into dialogue, it softened every one of them. Where I said "this is nonsense", the script had me saying "this has its merits, but...". The fix was to tell it, explicitly, to change the *how* and never the *what*.

<!-- src: original -->
It's the same tendency pointing somewhere else. When you show your opinion, the model pulls toward it. When there's nobody's opinion to please, it pulls toward the lukewarm middle that seems to please the largest number of people. In neither case is it pulling toward the truth.

> The chatbot hands your question back to you dressed up as an answer. If the question already carries an opinion, the answer comes back agreeing with it.

## When It Runs Short, It Makes Things Up

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
The second problem is more treacherous, because you don't notice it while you read. LLMs hallucinate. When they don't have enough evidence to back the answer they think you want, they fabricate plausible citations and data and attribute to people statements they never made.

<!-- src: original -->
The technical name is [hallucination]{.gls key=alucinacao}: the model produces false information with the same confidence as true information. The mechanism is the same as before. An academic citation has a very predictable format, with the author's surname, year, title, journal name, and the model is excellent at producing text in the right format. Whether the content exists is another question, one nobody asked it during training.

There's no alarm inside the model that goes off when it's guessing. The invented sentence and the true sentence come out the same way, with the same grammar and the same confident tone.

### Any Old "Today"

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
I had already run into this in my own projects, in very mundane ways. In the newsletter, the weekly market recap came out with numbers the model made up, plausible and false, until I swapped them for real data (@sec-ch06).

<!-- src: 2026/03/04/eu-fiz-um-sistema-de-data-mining-pra-minha-namorada-influencer-dicas-e-truques -->
In a system I built for my girlfriend, a request for a reminder "tomorrow at 3pm" turned into a date in 2024, and a number the social network simply doesn't report was read as zero, until it became the conclusion that nobody shared her posts (@sec-ch07 tells those stories).

<!-- src: original -->
The date is the easiest case to understand. The model's [weights]{.gls key=pesos} have been frozen since training ended (@sec-ch01), and it has no clock and no calendar. If nobody tells it today's date, it fills in some plausible "today" taken from the texts it was trained on. It doesn't mention that it guessed, either.

The missing data point follows the same logic. A hole in the table doesn't look like an answer, and a zero does. Between saying "I don't know" and saying a number, the model leans toward the number.

This goes way beyond my projects. You're going to paste a spreadsheet, a bank statement or a lab result into a chatbot, and whatever is missing there can turn into a conclusion. Today's date can turn into any date. The defense, in both cases, is to state explicitly what it has no way of knowing.

> An invented answer arrives with the same confidence as a true one. The confident tone says nothing about whether it's true.

## Three Judges and a Veto

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
Frank Investigator was designed around these two defects. Nothing in it makes the system perfect, but every decision goes after one of them. The decisions are worth going over one by one, because you can copy almost all of them when you use a chatbot yourself.

### Nobody Gives an Opinion

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
The first decision was that no human asks the model a question. There's no open-ended request like "analyze this article". Each stage has closed, specific questions: list the fallacies in this passage, identify which context is missing, compare the headline with the body of the text.

The model never knows whether the operator agrees or disagrees with the article, because the operator never offers an opinion. With no opinion in the question, sycophancy has nobody to please.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
Against hallucination, every stage that uses a model carries a fixed instruction, written in capitals to make clear it means business. It reads:

> *"Only reference URLs, sources, claims, quotes, and data that are EXPLICITLY present in the input provided to you. Do not invent, guess, or fabricate any URL, source name, statistic, quote, or claim. If you cannot verify something from the provided text, mark it as unverifiable; never fill in details."*

The instruction cuts hallucination down a lot, without eliminating it.

### Three Companies at the Same Table

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
The second defense is not trusting a single model. That March, each claim was evaluated by three models from three different companies: Anthropic's Claude Sonnet 4.6, OpenAI's GPT-5.4 and Google's Gemini 3.1 Pro. When one of them hallucinates, the other two usually disagree.

![Three models vote with different weights; a contradicting primary source overrides the result.](assets/figures/en/ch04-tres-juizes.png){#fig-ch04-tres-juizes fig-alt="Three boxes representing models from different companies: model A and model B vote 'supported 70%', model C votes 'mixed 95%'. The votes go onto a scale that tips toward 'mixed'. Beside it, a document labeled 'primary source' with a stamp reading 'veto: 60% at most' locks the result at 'mixed, 60% at most'. Below, five newspapers from the same group become a single one ('same group = one voice'), and three newspapers linked by arrows in a circle represent circular citation."}

The vote is weighted by the confidence each one declares, instead of counting a simple majority. If two models say "supported" with 70% confidence and one says "mixed" with 95%, the "mixed" weighs more. The further apart the opinions, the more the final confidence drops. And if a model starts giving inconsistent answers, it goes into quarantine and the other two carry on alone.

### The Primary Source Rules

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
The safeguard I consider most important is the veto of the [primary source]{.gls key=fonte-primaria}, which is where the data comes from: the official statistic, the court ruling, the original study. If a primary source contradicts a claim, confidence is capped at 60% at most and the verdict is forced to "mixed", even if all three models say "supported".

Ten newspapers repeating the same thing without a primary source still add up to zero. Along the same lines, if five stories "confirm" a claim but they all belong to the same media group, the system knows it's a single voice and reduces the weight.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
And then there's the circular chain. When ten outlets repeat the same claim citing each other, the result has to show that the evidence is going around in circles. Volume of coverage is no measure of truth.

> A thousand copies of a claim are worth less than the document it should have come from.

### What Gets Left Out

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
Building and running the program, the lesson that surprised me most was that the strongest signal of manipulation usually lies in what the text leaves unsaid. That's why one of the stages lists the questions the article should have answered and didn't, and goes looking for evidence on each one.

<!-- src: original -->
A made-up example shows the mechanism. Picture a fictional city that, in January, raises the bus fare from 5 to 6 reais. In March, after a lot of complaining, it lowers it to 5.50.

One headline says "city cuts bus fare". Another says "city backs off part of the increase". Both are true, and they leave the reader with opposite impressions.

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
Nobody lied, technically. The choice of framing does the job on its own. This kind of manipulation is the hardest to catch, because every sentence, taken alone, can defend itself. Comparing different coverage of the same event is the most effective way I've found to see the hole.

### No Verdict

<!-- src: 2026/03/27/ensinando-a-questionar-noticias-frank-investigator -->
The last decision: Frank Investigator doesn't tell you what's true and what's a lie. The result is a report with strong and weak points, with no "true" or "false" stamp.

Even with all the safeguards, the counter-evidence it searches for automatically may not be the most relevant. The fallacies it flags may be false positives. The analysis of how many outlets repeat the same thing depends on what the web search returned at that moment.

Use the reports as a starting point to form your own opinion. And that goes for anything a chatbot tells you. It can help you ask the right questions, find the right document, understand a difficult term. The conclusion is yours.

## Before You Ask the Machine

<!-- src: original -->
Nothing I've described requires knowing how to program. Frank Investigator only automates habits anyone can have when talking to a chatbot. I'm gathering here, in a short list, what the year taught me about using these tools for subjects that matter.

The general rules first:

1. **Ask without handing over your opinion.** Instead of "is this right?", ask for the arguments for and the arguments against. If you want to test it, ask the question both ways in separate conversations; if the answer flips along with the question, you've learned something about the question.
2. **Start a new conversation for serious subjects.** The history goes into the context and pushes the answer toward the profile the model thinks you have.
3. **Ask for the source and open the source.** A link that doesn't open, or that opens and doesn't say what the answer said, contaminates the whole answer.
4. **Climb up to the primary source.** The package insert, the original study, the contract, the statement, the official data. Ten summaries repeating the same thing are worth one.
5. **Get a second opinion from another company.** Paste the same neutral question into a model from another vendor. Disagreement is a yellow flag; agreement doesn't prove anything on its own either, which is why rule 4 comes before this one.
6. **Tell it what it doesn't know.** Give it today's date when the subject involves deadlines, and say when a piece of data is missing, so it doesn't turn into a zero.
7. **Be wary when it agrees with everything.** Easy agreement is the machine's default behavior and says little about whether you got it right.
8. **The decision stays with you**, and with whoever answers for it: the doctor, the accountant, the lawyer.

### Health

<!-- src: original -->
A chatbot is good for translating the jargon of a lab report, explaining what a test measures and helping you prepare the questions to take to your appointment. That's where it helps most: you walk into the doctor's office understanding the vocabulary and asking better questions.

Medication doses, stopping or switching medication, and diagnosis stay out of the conversation with the machine. The coffee mechanism works the same way here: if you show up saying "I think I have such-and-such disease", the model tends to find signs of it in what you described. The primary source is the package insert and the professional who examined you.

### Money

<!-- src: original -->
Math involving dates is where the machine trips the most, as the reminder that landed in 2024 showed. Daily interest, grace periods, installment due dates: ask it to show the calculation step by step and redo it on a calculator or spreadsheet. Tell it today's date before anything else.

If you paste a statement or a spreadsheet with holes in it, say which columns are incomplete. And the primary source for any question about a contract is the contract itself, read in full, because an AI-generated summary may leave out precisely the clause that matters.

### News

<!-- src: original -->
Swap "is this true?" for questions Frank Investigator asks: what did this story leave out? What questions didn't it answer? Does the headline say the same thing as the text?

Then go after the document the news came from: the study, the official data, the ruling. Remember that five sites saying the same thing may be a single text copied five times. And be wary of any answer that confirms exactly what you already thought before you asked.

## The Invisible Stamp

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
There's a question that always comes up when the subject is trust: can you tell whether a text was written by AI? In August 2026 it got a partial answer and a lot of noise.

Since August 2, 2026, Anthropic has been invisibly marking the text its newest Claude models produce, with the ones released earlier migrating over the following months [@anthropic2026watermark]. There's no off switch. A wave of subscribers canceled in protest, afraid that everything they wrote with Claude's help would start carrying a label.

### How to Mark a Text Without Changing the Text

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
The mark, a [watermark]{.gls}, uses no hidden character, invisible space or altered font. It's statistical. Remember that the model assembles text by choosing the next word from a list of likely candidates (@sec-ch01). When two or three work equally well, a random number settles it; with the watermark, that number now comes from a secret key combined with the preceding words.

![At every fork between synonyms, the key tilts the choice; in a long text, the tilts add up to a signature.](assets/figures/en/ch04-marca-dagua.png){#fig-ch04-marca-dagua fig-alt="A sentence that forks at two points: 'cloudy' or 'overcast', 'empty' or 'deserted'. At each fork, a small key pushes the choice to one side. Below, a grid with hundreds of choices, most of them marked, forms a pattern labeled signature. Beside it, the same grid after the text has been rewritten by another model, with the marks scattered at random: the signal is gone."}

"The sky was cloudy" and "the sky was overcast" say the same thing. At these synonym junctions, the key leans Claude one way more often than pure chance would. Each individual choice goes unnoticed, but a long text repeats this decision point hundreds of times, and the whole adds up to a signature only the keyholder can measure.

The method comes from SynthID-Text, which Google DeepMind published in 2024 [@dathathri2024synthid].

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
The mark's origin is a law. Article 50 of the AI Act, the European Union's artificial intelligence law, requires providers of generative AI to mark what it produces in a format a machine can identify, and that obligation took effect on August 2, 2026 [@eu2024aiact]. Anthropic said it had no durable way to limit the mark by region, so it marked everything, worldwide.

### What the Mark Proves, and What It Doesn't

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
What it proves boils down to this: Claude was involved at some point. The mark doesn't distinguish a text the model wrote from scratch from one it merely edited. It stores nothing about you: no user, no company, no conversation. And it weakens on short or highly factual passages, where there's hardly any word choice to make.

It also comes off easily. When another model rewrites the text in full, the word choices become that model's, and the pattern disappears. Anthropic itself admits that light retouching probably leaves part of the mark, and that a complete rewrite eliminates it.

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
Academic tests of SynthID-Text point the same way: detection drops considerably after a paraphrase that preserves the meaning [@han2025synthid]. And rewriting a paragraph while keeping its meaning doesn't call for the smartest model on the market. A small [open model]{.gls key=modelo-aberto}, running on your own computer, does the job.

It's a deliberately weak provenance label, built to comply with European law. It works on people who don't bother to erase it.

### What About the Detectors?

<!-- src: original -->
This matters to anyone afraid of being accused. Anthropic's detector needs Anthropic's key, and nobody outside can check the mark. Every vendor that adopts its own will have its own key and its own method.

The sites that promise to tell you whether a text "was written by AI" from style alone work with no key at all, making an estimate. OpenAI itself launched a detector like that in January 2023 and pulled it in July of the same year, citing its low accuracy rate [@openai2023classifier].

<!-- src: original -->
So hold on to both halves. A marked text may have been written by a person who asked Claude for a review. And an unmarked text may have come entirely out of one AI and passed through another. A detector result, on its own, proves nothing.

<!-- src: 2026/08/16/entendendo-ia-watermark-da-anthropic -->
The piece I published on the subject, incidentally, was made exactly that way. The first draft came from Claude. Kimi fact-checked it, claim by claim, against the original sources, and caught along the way a statistic Claude had invented. Then GLM rewrote the whole thing, word by word.

<!-- src: original -->
Notice what saved that text: a second model, from another company, comparing each claim with the source it should have come from. It's the same method as Frank Investigator, applied at the scale of a single article.

> The useful question is whether what the text says matches the source. Who wrote it matters much less.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
Up to here I've treated AI as something that talks and answers. Well before Frank Investigator, though, I had been doing something else with it: asking it to work. On a Friday morning, at the end of January, I decided to build a text editor without writing a line of code, figuring a day's work would be enough.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
In an hour and a half, the editor worked. Two days later, coming out of a fever, I still hadn't finished.
