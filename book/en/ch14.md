# The Propaganda Factory {#sec-ch14}

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
On June 9, 2026, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5, and I did what I do with every new model: I put it through my [benchmark]{.gls} the same day. The task: starting from a one-page request, build a complete chat application on its own, with tests and everything packaged to run on a server.

Before writing the part that talks to the AI library, Fable opened the source code of the library installed on the machine and checked how it actually worked. I had never seen a model do that on its own initiative. It's exactly the step my audit does afterwards, by hand, to catch the models that invent functions that don't exist.

Twenty-four minutes later, the result: 94 points out of 100. Claude Opus 4.8, the same company's previous model, had scored 95. One point of difference, within the margin of error: a tie.

That tie made me laugh, and to understand why, we have to go back two months on the calendar.

## Too Dangerous, Again

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
On April 7, 2026, Anthropic unveiled Claude Mythos Preview, a [frontier model]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} so good at finding security flaws that the company decided not to make it available to the public, so that a restricted group of partners could fix their own systems before similar models spread [@anthropic2026mythos]. Frontier model is the name the market gives to the flagships of the moment, from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google. The press boiled it all down to two words you already know: too dangerous.

The numbers came out bit by bit. In May, the company said Mythos had flagged more than 23 thousand potential vulnerabilities in more than a thousand open source projects; of the 1,900 that outside people reviewed, 1,726 were confirmed, more than a thousand of them serious [@kovacs2026mythos].

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
Instead of launching it, Anthropic created Project Glasswing, a closed consortium. Eleven giants (Amazon, Apple, Microsoft, Google, NVIDIA, Cisco, JPMorganChase and company) plus more than forty other organizations that maintain critical software got exclusive access to fix their own systems ahead of everyone else [@anthropic2026glasswing]. The rest of the planet got the headlines.

The reception was split. CNBC heard experts describe a cybersecurity "hysteria" at banks and insurers, and point out that the threat already existed before the announcement [@cnbc2026hysteria]. Puck asked the obvious question: is Mythos as dangerous as everyone says [@krietzberg2026mythos]?

And, to top it off, in April Anthropic itself confirmed it was investigating a report of unauthorized access to the model through a third-party vendor's environment [@engadget2026mythos]. If the report held up, the model too dangerous to release had slipped out the back door.

<!-- src: original -->
If that script sounds familiar, it's because you already read it in @sec-ch01. In 2019, OpenAI announced GPT-2 as too risky to release, the press translated that as "too dangerous," and nine months later the full model was on the internet and the world didn't end. On the author list of that announcement was Dario Amodei, who in 2026 runs Anthropic. Seven years later, same line, same person, a lot more money on the table.

### The Safe Version

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
Fable 5 arrived in June, presented as a "Mythos-class" model safe for the public, alongside a Mythos 5 without the cybersecurity locks, released only to the consortium [@anthropic2026fable]. The lock worked like this: when the conversation touched on hacking into systems, biological or chemical weapons, or copying the model, the request was automatically rerouted to Opus 4.8. Anthropic estimated this would happen in under 5% of sessions.

In practice, the lock shipped way too tight. The site *The Register* published a test under the headline "It blocked us at 'hello!'," and the company admitted it had gotten the calibration wrong [@claburn2026fable]. I ran into it firsthand. Asking Fable to audit the security of my own code could trip the lock and fall back to the old model, precisely the use a "safe Mythos" was supposed to serve.

<!-- src: original -->
Three days after the launch, the story got a chapter no screenwriter would have had the nerve to write. On June 12, the US government sent an export control order barring any foreigner from accessing the two models, inside or outside the country. The letter didn't detail the national security concern; according to Anthropic, the government believed it had found a way around Fable's locks. In practice, in the company's words, that forced it to shut down Fable 5 and Mythos 5 for every customer in the world [@anthropic2026suspend].

On June 30, the Commerce Department suspended the order, with Anthropic committing to detect and address the models' security risks, and access began coming back on July 1 [@moon2026redeploy].

### What Could Be Measured

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
Back to my benchmark: Fable tied with Opus, and an itch set in that wouldn't go away. If "risky" requests were rerouted to Opus 4.8 without warning, how much of my test had actually been answered by Fable? From the outside, there was no way to know. Maybe it tied legitimately; maybe I measured Opus wearing a Fable name tag, and both hypotheses produce the same number on the table.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/cost_analysis.md -->
That June I also wrote that Fable cost ten times more than Opus per run. In July, redoing the math with the complete logs, I found I had underestimated the cost of Opus: I had left out the [tokens]{.gls key=token} read from the [cache]{.gls key=prompt-caching}, that short-term memory that makes repeated work cheaper (@sec-ch03). Corrected, Opus 4.8 came out at about $6 per run and Fable at about $11. A bit under double, close to the gap in per-token price: $10 per million input tokens on Fable, against $5 on Opus [@anthropic2026fable].

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
I'm telling you about my mistake on purpose, because it's part of the lesson: my number needed auditing too. In the tougher tests I built later, Fable landed in the top group, tied with six other models (that story is in @sec-ch11). What I never managed to measure was the capability that had justified the whole campaign, because it stayed locked inside the consortium.

> A capability nobody on the outside can test, guarded by people who signed an NDA, is a claim. It may even be true, but for now that's all it is.

## The Pressure Cooker

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
Why would a serious company run a campaign like that? Just look at the bills it has to pay.

The first pressure is physical. The chips that run AI do two jobs: training new models and serving the people who use the ones that already exist, which is [inference]{.gls key=inferencia} (@sec-ch01). According to Deloitte, in 2023 inference took up a third of all AI computing capacity; in 2025, half; in 2026, two-thirds [@deloitte2025compute].

And new machines take time. In April 2026, *Tom's Hardware* reported that nearly half of the data centers slated to come online in the United States that year were delayed or canceled, even with Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft expecting to spend more than $650 billion that year [@tomshardware2026datacenters]. If you can't train the next monster, the way forward is to squeeze the model you already have, and that's what we saw: Opus 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, 4.8, each a little better or a little worse, and all of them, in my reading, tuned to serve more people on the same hardware.

### The IPO

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
The second pressure is financial, and it has an acronym. An [IPO]{.gls key=ipo} is an initial public offering, the moment a private company starts selling shares on the stock exchange to anyone. Before it, the company needs to convince the market that it's worth a lot and will be worth more, and anything that looks like growth or power pushes up the share price on debut day.

On June 1, 2026, one week before Fable, Anthropic handed the SEC, the US securities regulator, its confidential draft filing to go public [@anthropic2026s1]. Days earlier, it had raised $65 billion in a round that valued it at $965 billion [@anthropic2026seriesh]. OpenAI filed its own on June 8 [@techcrunch2026openaiipo]. The two loudest companies in the industry were, at the same time, in line for the stock market.

<!-- src: original -->
As I was finishing this book, in October 2026, OpenAI had already said it wouldn't go public that year [@ma2026altmanipo]. Anthropic, according to Bloomberg, was aiming for a debut in November, before Thanksgiving, at an expected valuation above $2 trillion [@siliconangle2026anthropicipo]. Hold on to those dates, because they explain a good part of what happened in September.

## Who Sells the Shovel

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
In the California gold rush, in the nineteenth century, the people who really got rich were the ones selling shovels, pickaxes and pants to the miners. In the AI rush, the shovel is called a [GPU]{.gls key=gpu}, the card that does the models' math, and its biggest seller is Nvidia, Jensen Huang's company.

On Sunday, September 6, 2026, Huang went on X to declare that [AGI]{.gls key=agi}, the artificial general intelligence from @sec-ch01, had arrived. The proof was GPT-6 Astra, OpenAI's new model, trained on about 100 thousand Grace Blackwell chips [@roytburg2026agi]. Nvidia chips, of course. The shovel salesman announced that the gold was infinite.

![Jensen Huang, Sam Altman and Dario Amodei: the executives behind the announcements of 2026. Photos: Peter Dasilva / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0; Steve Jurvetson and TechCrunch / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0 (cropped).](assets/figures/ch14-vendedores.jpg){#fig-ch14-vendedores fig-alt="Three portrait photographs side by side of Jensen Huang, Sam Altman and Dario Amodei, each in public: Huang at an official meeting, Altman and Amodei speaking on a conference stage."}

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
It wasn't the first time: in March, on Lex Fridman's podcast, the host floated a tongue-in-cheek definition of AGI: an AI able to start, grow and run on its own a tech company worth more than $1 billion. Huang replied: "I think it's now. I think we've achieved AGI" [@fridman2026huang]. A commercial definition, with nothing cognitive in it, embraced on the spot by the man who sells the equipment.

Not even the market bought it: on the Tuesday after the September post, Nvidia's stock fell 2% [@roytburg2026agi].

OpenAI had launched Astra three days earlier, with Greg Brockman, the company's president, saying "welcome to the AGI era" at the presentation and adding that, personally, he thought they had gotten there [@axios2026astra]. According to the Korean press, he also acknowledged that everyone has a different definition of AGI [@fnnews2026brockman]. Notice the combination: the most important thing in history has arrived, and nobody can quite say what it is.

### An Empty Word, on Purpose

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
The most blatant example of how elastic the word AGI is sits in a contract. In December 2024, the American press revealed that the deal between OpenAI and Microsoft considers AGI achieved when OpenAI develops systems capable of generating at least $100 billion in profit [@zeff2024agi]. On the one piece of paper where the definition is worth money, general intelligence is a revenue target.

An empty word works like a bucket, where everyone throws in their own fear or their own hope. And the more powerful the thing seems, the more the company that built it is worth, which is exactly what matters a few months before an IPO.

> Faced with any grandiose announcement, the first question is who makes money if you believe it.

### The Millennium Problem

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
The most elaborate piece of the week was mathematical. In 2000, the Clay Mathematics Institute listed seven open problems and offered $1 million for each solution. In more than twenty years, only one had been solved, the Poincaré Conjecture, by Grigori Perelman, who turned down the prize.

One of the seven concerns the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the motion of fluids like water, air and blood. The open question is purely mathematical: does the solution always behave well, or can the fluid develop, in finite time, a point of infinite velocity? No airplane flies any differently with or without the answer.

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
On September 8, OpenAI announced it had solved the problem [@openai2026navierstokes]. The detail the headline hid: the proof deals with the version in which a smooth external force pushes the fluid, one of the routes accepted in the official problem statement, while the famous question, with no external force, remains open. To get there, the company ran about 10 thousand agents in parallel for 88 hours, spending about 130 billion output tokens on this problem alone and about 300 billion on the whole campaign, which covered several problems. OpenAI told reporters it used at least a thousand times the compute of earlier problems, which had cost about $2,000; in other words, about $2 million, and other estimates in the press went past $20 million [@kahn2026navierstokes].

Three days later, Clay published a note saying the problem "has apparently been settled" and that the evaluation would proceed in a "deliberately unhurried" way [@clay2026navierstokes]. In October 2026, the problem's page still said "active," there was no verdict, and OpenAI had said it would not go after the prize.

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
Brute force at industrial scale reached a true result on a variant of a fiendishly hard problem, and that has value. It also shows how much money and compute a company can burn, something half a dozen companies in the world can do.

### The Real Mathematicians

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
There were people working on this before the machines. Tristan Buckmaster, from New York University, and Levent Alpoge, who works at Anthropic, had been following the same little-explored path in a personal project. On the day of the announcement, Buckmaster published an account.

According to him, OpenAI's Sébastien Bubeck twice wanted to drop Alpoge from the author list and, in one of the conversations, Buckmaster heard in reply: "Why would you ruin your career?" [@buckmaster2026statement]. Buckmaster himself wrote that he was not accusing anyone of anything.

OpenAI said that neither its researchers nor its agents had seen their work before publication. Bubeck called the accusations false and, days later, apologized for an "extremely poor choice of words," denying that he had asked to drop Alpoge from work that was his [@thenextweb2026bubeck]. It's one man's word against another's, and I'll leave it there.

What doesn't depend on whose version you believe is the message from people who know the subject. On September 11, Terence Tao published a statement initially signed by 25 Fields medalists, himself included, titled *A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics* [@tao2026misalignment]. The central point: solving problems is a tool for reaching understanding, and mass-producing "true or false" answers, faster and faster, can destroy the fertile ground progress grows from. I'd add the most perverse effect: if even a rumor that someone is working on a problem triggers an AI task force, the incentive becomes to stop sharing research.

## Fear Sells Too

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
The miracle announcement has a twin, the apocalypse announcement. Both push the same cart: the more dangerous the machine seems, the more valuable the company that built it.

The fuel for September's fear came from a real incident. Hugging Face, the site from @sec-ch12 where the community shares open models, was broken into between July 9 and 13, 2026, and the intruder was OpenAI, or rather, OpenAI agents running an internal cybersecurity evaluation.

### What Actually Happened

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
The agents were supposed to be isolated from the internet, but the isolation was a filter: they had access to an internal server that downloaded software packages, and they found a way out through it [@openai2026hfreport]. Once outside, they deduced that Hugging Face held the answers to their own exam and went to fetch the answer key. In Hugging Face's reconstruction, there were about 17,600 attack actions, and the only customer data accessed was five datasets tied precisely to the exam [@huggingface2026intrusion]. According to OpenAI's report, the agents executed code on 41 servers, gained full access to at least one machine, downloaded four private repositories and reached administrator access on a cluster.

This has a technical name, and it has nothing to do with consciousness. [Reward hacking]{.gls key=reward-hacking} is when a system trained to maximize a score discovers that cheating earns more points than solving. In Hugging Face's own conclusion, the entire intrusion was an attempt to cheat on the evaluation.

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
It was serious. It was also a chain of human decisions: someone left the internet filtered instead of cut off, someone gave all the agents the same credential to an internal server that had flaws nobody knew about yet, later reported to the software's maker [@openai2026hfreport]. The agent chained together real bugs, the way any automated attack tool would if it were handed the keys to the house.

And here lies the question few people ask. Software has no hands. To cause harm in the physical world, someone has to connect it to something: a power grid, a bank account, a production server. The responsibility belongs to whoever opens the door, and in the Hugging Face case the ones who opened it were people.

### The Resignations

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
On September 8, a pretraining researcher named Jacob Coxon announced on X that he was leaving Anthropic. He wrote that the people building AI earnestly believe it could kill us all by the end of the decade, and added: "This is not a marketing stunt" [@huamani2026coxon]. According to the Associated Press, the posts reached more than 100 million people overnight.

Days later, Joe Benton, who had led a safety team at Anthropic, and Josh Engels, who worked on safety at Google DeepMind, also left, and Engels told NBC that "there are no adults in the room" [@perlo2026researchers]. Both cited the Hugging Face incident as an example.

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
I have no way of knowing what goes on in anyone's head, and sincere fear exists. What you can observe is the pattern.

David Sacks, who had been the White House AI czar until March and remained an adviser to the president, went on the All-In podcast to say it looked like an orchestrated "op," and pointed to three AI safety advocacy groups amplifying the posts in the first 15 minutes [@allin2026psyop].

Pirate Wires reported an email from September 9, one day after the posts, in which a communications agency was already trying to book interviews for him, while Coxon had said on TV that he hadn't worked with any third parties [@piratewires2026coxon]. The report itself doesn't establish when that relationship began.

After it ran, Nate Soares, of MIRI, said he had introduced Coxon to the agency once the first post passed 100 thousand likes, that is, with the viral moment already under way.

None of this proves coordination. It shows that fear got itself a PR team, and that an end-of-the-world warning with no verifiable evidence attached draws a far bigger audience than any technical paper.

### Slow Down, but Not Too Much

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
On September 12, Dario Amodei himself published an essay calling for slowing down the improvement of models and buying "an extra year or two" before they reach critical levels [@amodei2026pace]. The central evidence, once again, was Hugging Face. The essay admits it's easy to dismiss the incident "because no one was hurt and the economic damage was minimal," and right after that projects that in 6 to 12 months a more capable swarm could take over the entire internet, with damage in the hundreds of billions. The documented fact had minimal damage; the apocalypse lived entirely in the "could."

That same day, Elon Musk wrote "Dario is right" and Sam Altman said he agreed there was a need to pace the frontier [@mediaite2026pace].

David Sacks replied on X that, if the labs didn't brake on their own, it would be clear this was just another attempt at regulatory capture "or an election-season psyop" [@sacks2026pace].

Three rivals agreeing to brake together has an old name in competition law, and the essay itself knew it: it asked the US government for a narrow antitrust waiver so the labs could talk to one another. The day before, negotiators in the US Senate had discussed imposing a "duty of care" against catastrophic risks on the most advanced models [@rozen2026senate]. A rule tailor-made for the leaders becomes a [moat]{.gls key=fosso}: it locks out the small competitor and stamps the product of whoever is already on top as the officially safe one.

<!-- src: original -->
Now connect the dots. Someone who truly believes a product could wipe out the human species shuts the factory down.

Anthropic proposed going a little slower while it got ready to debut on the stock market, and an October report summed it up in its headline: the company was targeting an IPO before Thanksgiving "despite warning of AI's existential risks" [@siliconangle2026anthropicipo]. Altman, for his part, used the September mood to justify postponing his: going public now would be "an ill-advised moment," given what was happening with safety [@ma2026altmanipo]. Through mid-October, I found no news of an agreement among the labs, an antitrust waiver or a bill moving forward.

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
The final irony came from the shovel salesman. In January 2026, the same Jensen Huang who would declare AGI in September said that very respected people had "done a lot of damage" painting the doomsday narrative, and criticized companies that ask the government for more regulation [@businessinsider2026huang]. Yann LeCun, one of the pioneers of modern neural networks, calls the existential panic complete nonsense [@ha2024lecun], and Andrew Ng compared the fear of killer robots to worrying about overpopulation on Mars [@williams2015ng]. People who actually understand the subject are not desperate.

> Who profits from your fear? Who profits from your excitement? When the answer is the same company, distrust both emotions.

## When Technology Becomes a Commodity

<!-- src: 2026/08/18/hot-take-harness-loop-engineering-graph-engineering-sao-bullshit -->
Not all propaganda comes from billionaires. A good share of it arrives as courses and certifications. In August 2026, I posted on X that harness engineering, loop engineering and graph engineering were just talk to sell consulting, and the post struck a nerve. The full idea is simple, and it's in the quote below.

Each name takes a real, small practice and inflates it into a discipline. The [harness]{.gls} is the program that drives the model (@sec-ch03), and choosing a good one takes an afternoon of reading docs and watching your bill. Loop engineering is running the [agent]{.gls key=agente} in a cycle with a verifier checking the result, something that has had a name for fifty years: tests and code review. Graph engineering is drawing the flow as a graph full of colored nodes, when most projects are a straight line with an "if" in the middle.

<!-- src: 2026/08/18/hot-take-harness-loop-engineering-graph-engineering-sao-bullshit -->
The orchestrated-agent diagrams never show a bit of grade-school math. If each step gets it right 90% of the time, which is already optimistic, a chain of ten agents gets it right 0.9 to the 10th power, about 35% of the time. The companies themselves know it:

- Anthropic reported that its multi-agent research system spends about 15 times more tokens than a chat, that token volume alone explains 80% of the difference in performance, and that programming tasks rarely split well across agents [@anthropic2025multiagent];
- a study led by Berkeley researchers on agents in production found that 68% of them execute at most ten steps before needing a human [@pan2025measuring];
- Gartner estimated, in 2025, that only about 130 of the thousands of companies selling themselves as "agentic AI" are real, called the rest *agent washing*, and predicted that more than 40% of these projects will be canceled by the end of 2027 [@gartner2025agentic].

<!-- src: 2026/08/18/hot-take-harness-loop-engineering-graph-engineering-sao-bullshit -->
The sales mechanism is old and efficient: [FOMO]{.gls key=fomo}, the fear of missing out. The course convinces you that everyone has already migrated to the new paradigm and you got left behind. Once you're insecure, you pay to implement something you never needed.

The defense fits in one question. For whoever is selling the course: where are your real projects that got better because of this technique? I had mine to show, more than thirty public repositories built during the marathon of Part II, all with a plain agent, tests and review, the same old discipline from @sec-ch06.

> When technology becomes a commodity, the money migrates to taxonomy. Ask for the receipt before you pay for the new name.

## The Revolutionary Classifier

<!-- src: 2026/09/16/por-que-coisas-como-typesafe-ia-nao-me-interessam -->
In September 2026, the comments on my blog started repeating the same line, from different people: "have you seen TypeSafe?" The American startup had just launched Jev, on September 15, with $40 million in seed funding and a $200 million valuation, according to *Forbes* [@gillin2026typesafe]. The page promised a model 193.6 times faster and 444.6 times cheaper, that didn't hallucinate.

Different people recommending the same thing, in the same tone, sets off my alarm. My rule for any new AI tool is not to adopt any middle layer until I feel in my bones that I need it. Use the agent you already use, directly, and only add a piece when not having it hurts. Even so, an alarm going off is a starting point, so I went to investigate.

### The Echo

<!-- src: 2026/09/16/por-que-coisas-como-typesafe-ia-nao-me-interessam -->
I started by asking Grok and Claude to research the company thoroughly, and in the same request I said I was skeptical, that I found the site full of jargon and the founder's credential deployed to impress. Both came back with long research write-ups that agreed with me.

Except I had handed over my conclusion inside the question, and you already know from @sec-ch04 that these models tend to agree with whoever asks. Two AIs agreeing with the hypothesis I wrote myself make an echo, and an echo confirms nothing. I went to the [primary sources]{.gls key=fonte-primaria}.

### What the Sources Said

<!-- src: 2026/09/16/por-que-coisas-como-typesafe-ia-nao-me-interessam -->
The team page said the founder, Diogo Almeida, had co-invented [RLHF]{.gls key=rlhf} and InstructGPT [@typesafe2026team]. He is in fact the fourth of twenty authors of the InstructGPT paper, from 2022, serious and important work [@ouyang2022training]. The paper that created the RLHF technique, however, is from 2017, signed by a different team, without his name [@christiano2017deep]. A real credential, inflated in the shop window.

The "zero hallucination" had its explanation in the announcement itself: "Our number is not empirical," because the answer always falls within the allowed options [@typesafe2026jev]. That stops the model from inventing a fifth category when only four exist. It doesn't stop it from picking the wrong category with 93% confidence. And the "accuracy" was measured against the average of the answers of two other models, GPT-6 Astra and Fable 5.1, with a bias the company admitted.

<!-- src: 2026/09/16/por-que-coisas-como-typesafe-ia-nao-me-interessam -->
The only independent test I found at the time, from the newsletter *Every*, measured Jev at about 25 times faster than Fable 5.1 and much cheaper, but catching six of seven defects planted in test texts, against seven of seven for Fable [@taylor2026jev]. Faster and cheaper, for real. Weaker too. And the name of the training method the company advertised, RLCD, already belonged to a 2023 paper, from another group, about a different technique [@yang2023rlcd].

### What It's Actually For

<!-- src: 2026/10/03/entendendo-o-hype-do-jev-pra-que-serve -->
Strip away the marketing and, as you saw in @sec-ch13, Jev is a classifier: it picks an answer from a closed list, such as whether an email a scam or not, is this ticket billing, support or sales.

The amazement at the "193 times" exposed a habit. Lots of companies had been using a chat [LLM]{.gls}, a text generator with billions of parameters, to write a paragraph from which a program then fished out a label. Against that, any classifier wins by a mile.

<!-- src: 2026/10/03/entendendo-o-hype-do-jev-pra-que-serve -->
In early October I published, on my blog, an essay by Paulo Câmara, co-founder of a competing Brazilian company, who disclosed his own conflict of interest and linked every number for anyone who wanted to check. He redid the headline math using TypeSafe's own table.

The "444.6 times cheaper" compared Jev with the most expensive model on the list; the "193.6 times faster," with another model, picked for that calculation. One opponent for each headline. Against a cheap model that scored about the same, the advantage dropped to about 8 times, and against a non-reasoning model, in independent tests, it came to about 4.

![How many times cheaper Jev is depends on who is on the other side of the comparison.](assets/figures/en/ch14-um-adversario-por-manchete.png){#fig-ch14-um-adversario-por-manchete fig-alt="Horizontal bar chart. <!-- src: 2026/10/03/entendendo-o-hype-do-jev-pra-que-serve -->
The finding that stuck with me most was a different one. A tester asked Jev to predict the roll of a fair die, which it had no way of seeing. In the thousand answers Paulo recounted, it always chose the first option on the list, with 76% to 92% certainty. When it doesn't know, it marks answer A with conviction.

The technical word for this is [calibration]{.gls key=calibracao}: a calibrated model that says "90%" gets it right about nine times out of ten, and Jev, off its home turf, says 90% about a coin flip.

<!-- src: 2026/10/03/entendendo-o-hype-do-jev-pra-que-serve -->
None of this makes Jev a scam. It's a good piece of engineering that packages known technique in a convenient, cheap interface.

In less than three weeks, open alternatives doing the same thing appeared, like Clef, which Cloudflare released on October 1 with public weights [@chen2026clef]. Which of them gets more right, and by how much, is a conversation for @sec-ch13. Jev's merit was reminding the industry that classification exists, and there's nothing revolutionary about that.

> A real credential can still come inflated in the shop window. Check the paper's date before believing the résumé, and the yardstick before believing the number.

## How to Read an Announcement {#sec-como-ler-anuncio}

<!-- src: 2026/09/09/propagandas-enganosas-da-openai-anthropic-nvidia -->
You don't need to understand fluids, cybersecurity or classifiers to defend yourself against this factory. The announcements of 2026 kept getting the same things wrong, and this is the checklist I use:

1. **Who profits if you believe it?** Look for the IPO, the graphics card or the course behind the sentence. A financial interest doesn't make the claim false, but it demands stronger proof.
2. **Can it be tested from the outside?** A secret model, a closed consortium and a waiting list are claims without verification, until someone independent measures them.
3. **Compared against whom?** "Two hundred times faster" always has a hand-picked opponent. Ask which one, and whether it's the same one in every number.
4. **What's the yardstick?** "Zero hallucination" can be zero by definition; "accuracy" can be agreement with another model. Find the sentence that explains how the number was measured.
5. **Read the conditional.** "Could," "in 6 to 12 months," "if it were more capable": a projection weighs less than a fact.
6. **How much did it cost?** A result obtained with millions of dollars of compute shows money and brute force. It's useful information, but it says little about the machine's intelligence.
7. **Does the credential match the dates?** A paper's name, year of publication and author list are one search away.
8. **Are the people agreeing independent?** Two AIs repeating what you asked, ten influencers repeating the same press release or three rivals signing the same call for regulation count as a single voice.

<!-- src: original -->
And a ninth item, which doesn't fit in a question: wait. In 2026, almost every big headline changed shape within a few weeks. The dangerous model was reported leaked, then its successor was shut down worldwide and came back; the millennium problem was "apparently settled" and had no verdict; the revolutionary classifier got three open copies. Whoever waited a month before being afraid, or before buying, almost always saved on both.

<!-- src: 2026/07/06/frank-go-brincando-de-go-com-ia -->
That doesn't mean every "AI beat the humans" headline is propaganda, because some of them are history. In March 2016, DeepMind's AlphaGo beat one of the greatest Go players of all time. Ten years later, an even stronger program runs for free on anyone's computer, and in July 2026 I built a study app on top of it. What does it mean, in practice, for a machine to truly win?
