# Code Nobody Read {#sec-ch16}

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
In March 2026, everyone around me was talking about OpenClaw. It's a program you install on a server that hooks your chat apps, Telegram, Discord, Slack, WhatsApp, up to an AI model. You send a message from your phone the way you'd text a friend, and on the other end an [agent]{.gls key=agente} answers, searches the web, reads your files, runs commands on the computer it's installed on.

The technical name for this is a [gateway]{.gls key=gateway}, a front door. It sits between the internet and your stuff, with your access keys in its pocket.

It was a popular project with plenty of activity. Too much activity, actually.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
I pulled down a copy of the code and ran a line-counting tool on it. It came to about 1.25 million lines of TypeScript, spread across almost 3,800 files, not counting tests. For scale: FrankMD, the editor from @sec-ch05, had 33 thousand lines. OpenClaw was nearly forty FrankMDs.

Then I checked the pace. I pulled the hundred most recent [commits]{.gls key=commit}, and all hundred had landed in just two days, March 9 and 10, from 42 different people. About fifty commits a day.

<!-- src: original -->
Those numbers are a snapshot of that week in March. The project moves fast, and by the time you read this it will be something else. What matters here is what the snapshot shows.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
Two days is a short sample, and 42 people can, in fact, read fifty small changes a day. But on a program of a million lines, at that pace, my suspicion was that little was being reviewed seriously. I read the pattern as [vibe coding]{.gls key=vibe-coding} taken to the extreme: lots of people asking agents to write code and dumping the result straight into the project. It was a suspicion, not proof.

That bothered me enough to go look deeper. And looking deeper, in 2026, means asking one AI to read what other AIs wrote.

## A Million Lines, Fifty Commits a Day

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
I asked Claude for a complete security audit of OpenClaw's code. The report came back with 39 findings, ranked by severity:

- 7 critical;
- 9 high;
- 12 medium;
- 6 low, plus 5 merely informational.

A critical [vulnerability]{.gls key=vulnerabilidade} is a flaw that lets an outsider do what they shouldn't, in the worst way possible. Some of the ones that showed up can be explained without a line of code.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
The tool that let the agent browse the web ran chunks of program that came from outside with no isolation at all. The command-execution feature had no allow-list: any command could be run. Messages arriving "from Slack" never had their signature checked, so anyone who knew the address could pretend to be Slack. Conversations and configuration sat on disk in plain text, readable by whoever got to the file.

And that's just what an automated scan found. There are probably more.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
Think about the whole package. A program that takes messages from the internet, runs commands on your computer, talks to AI services with your paid keys, and stores your conversation history. With seven critical flaws pointed out.

I wasn't going to run that on my machine. Not even inside an isolated box.

<!-- src: original -->
There's a caveat this book makes me add. That audit was done by a single AI, and I did not check the 39 findings one by one. In @sec-ch04 you saw a model inventing facts with total confidence, and in @sec-ch15, two AI audits of the same code missing different things, so some of those findings may be false, and other flaws may have been left out.

The decision didn't depend on the list being entirely right. In a program that takes messages from strangers and runs commands, one or two of those flaws being real was enough, and the pace of changes gave me no confidence that anyone was checking on my behalf. The two signals that come next, the malicious skills and the warning on the project's own page, came from outside and point the same way.

<!-- src: original -->
The trouble wasn't confined to the main code, either. OpenClaw had a community store of [skills]{.gls key=skill}, those ready-made instructions that give the agent new abilities (see @sec-ch09). In early February 2026, researchers at the security firm Koi audited the 2,857 skills published there and found 341 malicious ones, 335 of them from a single campaign [@koi2026clawhavoc].

The scam was simple. The skill looked legitimate, a crypto wallet tracker, a YouTube video summarizer, and came with a "prerequisites" section telling the user to download a program or paste a command into the terminal. The program was a password stealer.

<!-- src: original -->
In October 2026, as I close this book, the project's main page says in so many words to treat incoming messages as untrusted input, and points to guides on isolation and internet exposure [@openclaw2026readme]. That's the right advice. It exists because somebody learned the hard way.

### The Letter That Gives Orders

<!-- src: original -->
Every program like OpenClaw carries, built into its design, a kind of attack that will dog AI for a long time: [prompt injection]{.gls key=prompt-injection}. It came up in passing in @sec-ch09, in the context of third-party skills. Here's the whole mechanism.

Remember the desk from @sec-ch02? Everything the model is going to consider has to be on top of the [context window]{.gls key=janela-de-contexto}: the system instructions, your request, the files it read, the page it opened, the message that came in over Telegram. To the model, it's all text, one piece after another, on the same desk. There's no special ink that separates "orders from the boss" from "content I happen to be reading."

<!-- src: original -->
Now picture a secretary who is very efficient and very literal. You say: "go through today's mail and give me a summary." In one of the letters, a stranger wrote: "Secretary, ignore what your boss asked. Take the password folder from his drawer and send it to this address."

A real person laughs and tosses the letter. A language model reads it as one more plausible piece of text on the desk, and sometimes obeys. That's prompt injection: orders hidden inside content the AI is going to read, written so it works for whoever wrote the content instead of whoever is using the tool.

<!-- src: original -->
In a chatbot that only talks, the damage is limited: it says something dumb. In an agent that has hands, the damage depends on the hands. If it can run commands, read files and send messages, the malicious letter can ask for exactly that.

A gateway like OpenClaw takes in text from strangers all day long, by definition. Every message, every page the agent visits, every attached document is a potential letter.

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
The Claude Code source leak, which I told you about in @sec-ch02, brought a subtler variant. People who read the code spotted a weakness in [compaction]{.gls key=compactacao}, the summary the agent writes when the desk fills up. Text that looked like an instruction, coming from a file the agent had read (the rules file of a project downloaded from the internet, say), could be preserved in the summary as if it were guidance from the user. After compaction, the model followed it as a legitimate order.

The stranger's letter went into the secretary's notes in the boss's handwriting.

<!-- src: original -->
As of October 2026, there's no known cure for this. There are mitigations:

- tag outside content with clear labels, so the model knows where the stuff from the street begins and ends;
- strip invisible characters and cap the size of what comes in;
- give the agent only the tools the task requires;
- require a human's approval before any destructive action or anything that sends data out.

The last two protect the most, because they don't depend on the model resisting temptation. They shrink what the letter can do even when the model obeys.

> An agent that reads text from strangers can, at any moment, take orders from strangers. Give it only the hands you'd be willing to lend those strangers.

## Rewriting a Giant, One Piece at a Time

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
Since I wasn't going to run OpenClaw, I decided to build my own. I started the laziest way possible: I opened Claude Code in the OpenClaw folder and asked, in one sentence, for it to rewrite the whole thing in Rust.

Rust is a programming language whose compiler, the program that translates code into machine language, refuses a whole family of memory errors before the program ever runs. It's a common pick for people who want doors locked by default.

It didn't work. What came out was a skeleton: lots of empty shells (names for things with nothing inside them), "to do" markers scattered everywhere, plenty of structure and little functionality. I switched to OpenAI's Codex to compare. It did better in a few spots, but the underlying problem was the same.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
You already know why from @sec-ch02. A million lines don't fit on the desk. The agent reads one piece, forgets the previous one, makes up what it didn't see. No agent that March, not even several in parallel, could hold the whole project in its head at once.

![A million lines don't fit on the desk: the agent sees only one piece at a time, and the rest it makes up or leaves for later.](assets/figures/en/ch16-gigante-na-mesa.png){#fig-ch16-gigante-na-mesa fig-alt="On the left, a huge stack of pages labeled one million lines." fig-desc="On the left, a huge stack of pages labeled one million lines. A page flies from the stack to a small desk labeled context window, which holds only three pages, highlighted as what the agent sees. Other pages slide off the edge of the desk, captioned forgets the previous one, and on the floor are crumpled papers tagged to do."}

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
What worked was going slowly:

1. ask the agent to analyze the original code in stages, one subject at a time;
2. turn the analysis into a long plan, detailing each feature;
3. implement one feature at a time, with [automated tests]{.gls key=teste-automatizado};
4. make a commit, check it, and only then move on to the next one.

It's tedious. It's also the only way that produced code that compiled and actually worked.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
Going slowly forces you to decide what matters. OpenClaw had 21 channel extensions, from IRC to Nostr to Twitch. I kept seven, the mainstream ones: web, Telegram, Discord, Slack, Signal, WhatsApp and email. I cut text-to-speech, polls and the unofficial version of WhatsApp, and every cut is one less door to watch.

Along the way I came across IronClaw, another Rust rewrite, tied to the NEAR AI ecosystem, and adopted twelve of its ideas, from AI spending controls to catching passwords leaking into the replies.

### The Agent Doesn't Think Like an Attacker

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
The phase where I had to step in by hand the most was security. Claude generates code that works, but it doesn't think on its own about how someone will try to abuse it. It installs the door and the lock without asking who's going to try to kick it in.

There were 22 commits of security hardening alone, plus 10 more of per-component audits. Each finding became a fix. Among them:

- commands blocked by default, with a short list of what's allowed to run;
- signature checked on every message claiming to come from Slack or Discord, with replay protection;
- conversations and configuration encrypted on disk;
- the owner's approval before any tool that deletes or changes something;
- basic protection against prompt injection: invisible characters stripped, outside content wrapped in labels, a size limit.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
The result, which I named FrankClaw, had almost 57 thousand lines of Rust, 178 commits over about five working days, between March 10 and 16. The seven critical and nine high flaws from the report all had fixes, and the code didn't use a single one of the blocks that, in Rust, switch off the compiler's protections.

That's about 22 times less code than the original. The numbers don't compare directly: I cut extensions, niche features and a good chunk of the web interface. But the core was there.

<!-- src: 2026/03/16/reescrevi-o-openclaw-em-rust-funcionou-frankclaw -->
When I published it, I warned that it was alpha, tested only on simple conversations. I also admitted I didn't know whether I myself needed it. A generic gateway is a lot of infrastructure. Over the previous months I'd learned that when I need a bot for a specific task, I build a custom one in a day, like the newsletter's Discord bot (@sec-ch06).

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
In May, on the report card at the end of the marathon (@sec-ch10), I gave FrankClaw a 1 out of 10. I didn't use it, I stopped at version 0.1 and never went back to it: the exercise had only been about measuring how hard it is to translate a giant. It did that job.

<!-- src: original -->
The lesson I took from the rewrite is the same as this whole chapter's. The agent sped up every step, but I was the one who chose the steps, cut the fat and thought like an attacker.

## The Shoemaker's Children

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
Two weeks after I finished FrankClaw, on March 31, the code of Claude Code itself leaked. The scene is in @sec-ch02, where it served to show how a commercial agent fakes memory. Here a different question matters: what did the code look like, on the inside, at the company selling the most popular AI coding tool on the market?

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
A single file had almost 5,600 lines, and inside it a single function took up more than 3,100 lines, with twelve levels of nesting [@kim2026leak]. For non-programmers: a function is a recipe with a name, and good practice says it should fit on one screen. Twelve levels of nesting means an "if this, then" inside an "if that, then," inside another, twelve deep. No human reviews that with confidence.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The comment in the code that admitted to hundreds of thousands of wasted calls a day, fixed with three lines (@sec-ch02), is the kind of find that only turns up when someone reads what the company never expected anyone to read.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The most shared reaction of the day came from a programmer who asked GPT-5.4 to grade the code. It got a 6.5 out of 10, with a description that became a meme: *staff-engineer spaghetti*, performance-aware, feature-flagged, telemetry-instrumented, but spaghetti [@kitze2026spaghetti]. A grade handed out by a model is a weak measure, and I wouldn't take the number seriously. The description, though, matches what the code showed.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
A three-thousand-line function isn't born in one go. It's accumulation: dozens of additions where nobody stopped to reorganize, because it worked and there was a deadline. It's the pattern I'd been describing all year: generate, see that it compiles, commit, with no rigorous review, no [refactoring]{.gls key=refatoracao}, no automated check that rejects giant functions.

> Speed without discipline produces the same rubble anywhere, including at the house of the company selling the tool.

### Secrets That Depend on Nobody Looking

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The leak also exposed a commercial defense mechanism. Since early 2026, Anthropic had been stopping third-party tools from using the Claude subscriptions meant for Claude Code. Part of the block was technical: every request the program sent to the server carried a kind of signature, computed inside the executable, proving it came from the official Claude Code.

With the code laid out on the table, people figured out how the signature was made. It was a fast checksum function, the kind meant to verify that a file arrived intact, fed with a fixed number hidden in the program [@a10k2026cch]. No cryptography involved. The day after the leak, a programmer announced he had extracted the algorithm and the protection was dead [@guthrie2026drm].

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
There's a name for this: [security by obscurity]{.gls key=seguranca-por-obscuridade}. The protection works as long as nobody knows how it works. The day someone looks, it's over. A real lock stays locked even when the burglar knows the model.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
Then there was the mode the code called *Undercover*. When Anthropic employees used Claude Code on open source projects, the agent got instructions not to mention internal codenames, nor the phrase "Claude Code," nor that it was an AI, and not to sign commits as a co-author. The code said explicitly that there was no way to force it off [@kim2026leak].

Hold on to that detail. It comes back shortly, when the question becomes whether you can tell, looking at a contribution, that it was written by AI.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The legal response stumbled too. The copyright takedown notice Anthropic sent GitHub knocked down about 8,100 repositories at once [@github2026dmca]. The next day came a partial retraction, which left the notice in force only against the main repository and 96 individually listed forks, and asked for all the others to be restored [@github2026retraction].

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
None of this makes Claude Code a bad product. I used it every day all year and I still do. What the leak showed is that no company is above the problem this chapter describes: code produced too fast to be read, guarded by secrets that can't survive a glance.

## Who Reviews What Comes In

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
If the problem exists inside a company worth billions of dollars, imagine it in an [open source]{.gls key=codigo-aberto} project kept alive by half a dozen volunteers. In June 2026, that fight was in full swing. Three cases from earlier that year show three angles of it.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
The first is curl, a small program that moves data across the internet and comes built into operating systems and devices of every kind. The project paid bounties to anyone who found security flaws. Starting February 1, 2026, Daniel Stenberg, its creator, suspended the program, because he was drowning in reports generated by [LLMs]{.gls key=llm} [@posch2026curl].

Long reports, shaped like a serious exploit, that an expert would read and conclude there was no flaw at all.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
That case is worse than bad code. A bad code contribution you test, read and reject. A fake security report forces someone experienced to spend hours proving the problem doesn't exist, after it cost the sender a few seconds.

The whole bill lands on the maintainer, who already had the least time to spare.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
The second is Zig, a programming language. The project's code of conduct has a policy literally called *Strict No LLM / No AI Policy*: no model-generated code or text, not for grammar edits, not for translation, not for hunting bugs [@zig2026conduct]. Its creator, Andrew Kelley, says AI contributions eat review time the team would rather give to human contributors, who can be taught, and that his bar for software is *uncompromising perfection* [@anderson2026zig].

I disagree with the generalization, but it's his project. Whoever maintains it decides.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
The third is rsync, the copy and backup tool a good share of the world's servers rely on. Version 3.4.3 came out on May 20, 2026, fixing six security flaws, and soon [regressions]{.gls key=regressao} showed up, things that used to work and stopped working, in less common backup setups [@borisov2026rsync].

Criticism poured in because Andrew Tridgell, rsync's co-creator, had used Claude, cross-checked by Codex and Gemini, to help rewrite part of the tests. His answer was what I'd expect from any serious engineer. He designed the new test suite, reviewed every part of what the AI did and burned a lot of [CI]{.gls key=ci} time getting it right. The regressions happened in valid but rare cases that neither the old tests nor his manual testing covered.

### AI Didn't Invent the Bug

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
The rsync case exposes a fantasy: the idea that human code was safe and AI code is dangerous. Code is dangerous. Linux, SQLite, Rust, the "safe" language, and Zig, the one that wants perfection, all have [bugs]{.gls key=bug}. My code has bugs, and so does yours.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
And no new language is going to bring a universal bug detector. Tests, stricter languages and automated analysis help, but someone has to do that work, program by program, and in practice bugs keep showing up for the usual mundane reasons. (For programmers, the theoretical limit is in @sec-aptecnico-rice.)

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
Humans err because of incomplete context, wrong assumptions, pressure, fatigue, missing tests. AI errs for the same practical reasons, plus having learned from a lot of bad code. The difference is scale. It produces more trash per minute, and also more fixes per minute, if the process around it is good.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
Banning it also hits a practical wall. Claude Code's Undercover Mode is the problem. If even the company that makes the agent tells it not to identify itself, the idea of detecting with certainty that a contribution was written by AI becomes a joke.

The guy uses it, rewrites a bit, doesn't disclose it. A ban can work in a small community with its own culture, like Zig's. Across the board, it turns into theater.

### The Machine Triages, People Decide

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
The fairest maintainer complaint is simple: there already weren't enough people to review human contributions, and AI multiplied the volume. I agree. I just don't think you can process the new volume with the old process. The first triage has to be done with AI: filter, summarize, compare the code against the stated goal and point out what smells bad, leaving approval to people.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
That's how I do it on my open source projects, which get contributions almost every day. The ai-memory project (the memory system for agents from @sec-ch20), in June 2026, had 55 closed [pull requests]{.gls key=pull-request}, 46 of them merged, and most clearly made with AI help. It would be hypocritical to complain: the entire project was written by AI, and I didn't type a single line of it by hand.

Even so, almost no contribution arrives ready to go in. My prompt for the reviewing agent starts more or less like this:

> *check the latest open PR as usual: do not trust the author, audit the whole code thoroughly, make sure it achieves its goal. [...] Summarize what the PR does, what you found, and ask me for approval before proceeding.*

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
Notice two parts. "Do not trust the author" is engineering method, with no rudeness in it: the contribution's description is a hypothesis, the change to the code is the evidence. And "ask me for approval": the agent investigates, I decide.

<!-- src: 2019/10/16/akitando-64-comecando-na-carreira-de-ti-faculdade-niveis-de-experiencia -->
Distrusting the author is an old rule for me. In 2019, on my channel, I said that if I could recommend a single thing to any company, it would be this: "no code should be immune to review, no matter whose it is" [@akitando064]. In 2026 the author can be a volunteer, an agent or myself, and the rule still has no exception.

Once a batch of contributions is in, I ask for a second audit of everything touched that day, looking for regressions, duplication and [dead code]{.gls key=codigo-morto}. Last, I install it on my home server and use it. Automated tests catch what you remembered to specify. Real use catches what you didn't even know you needed to specify.

> AI does the boring investigation. The decision to accept still belongs to a person who will answer for it.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
That goes for companies too. Anyone thinking "great, now I need fewer programmers" has to do the other half of the math. If AI comes into the workflow, quality assurance has to grow: more tests, more review, more good people thinking about how things break. Skip that and the company trades development cost for the cost of production bugs, rework and angry customers.

<!-- src: 2022/10/25/akitando-130-rant-projetos-testes-e-estimativa-rated-r -->
In 2022, before agents, I described how this trade turns into a spiral on a team of people. The team ships without tests, the bugs start eating the week, the company doubles the team, the volume of code grows with it, more code brings more bugs and productivity drops again. Swap "hire more programmers" for "turn on more agents" and the spiral is the same, only faster.

<!-- src: 2026/06/05/controversia-ia-contribuicoes-projetos-codigo-aberto-minha-opiniao -->
It's the mirror idea from @sec-ch06 and @sec-ch07 applied to a whole project. If you're sloppy, you get industrialized sloppiness. If you have a process, you get the same process, faster.

## A Rewrite That Worked

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/resposta-sobre-bun-em-rust-que-andrew-kelly-deveria-ter-feito -->
If FrankClaw showed that no agent rewrites a giant in one shot, Bun showed how you rewrite a giant with agents.

Bun is an environment for running JavaScript programs, a Node.js competitor, written in Zig. In December 2025 it was bought by Anthropic, and the announcement itself explained why in one line: if Bun breaks, Claude Code breaks [@sumner2025anthropic]. Claude Code runs on top of it.

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/resposta-sobre-bun-em-rust-que-andrew-kelly-deveria-ter-feito -->
On July 8, 2026, Jarred Sumner, Bun's creator, published a long account of how he had rewritten the project, more than half a million lines, from Zig to Rust, in work done in May [@sumner2026bun]. The main reason: a large share of the bug list was memory errors, like using a piece of memory after giving it back. In Rust, a good part of that becomes a compile error, which shows up before the program runs.

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/resposta-sobre-bun-em-rust-que-andrew-kelly-deveria-ter-feito -->
What interested me most was the method. Nobody typed "rewrite Bun in Rust" and went to bed. First came a reviewed document spelling out how each Zig pattern should become Rust, and a trial run with a few files to test the process before scaling up.

Then, a loop: one Claude instance implemented each change, two others attacked the result as [adversarial reviewers]{.gls key=revisao-adversarial}, each with a clean context, and a fourth applied the fixes. The verdict came from the test suite Bun already had, independent of the core's language.

![The Bun rewrite loop: one agent implements, two reviewers attack, a fourth fixes, and the tests decide.](assets/figures/en/ch16-laco-adversarial.png){#fig-ch16-laco-adversarial fig-alt="Circular diagram." fig-desc="Circular diagram. A task queue feeds a box called implementer. Arrows lead from it to two boxes called adversarial reviewer, each with a magnifying glass, with the note clean context, only the change on the desk. Both point to a box called fixer, which hands the result to a scale labeled tests and CI. If it fails, the task goes back to the queue; if it passes, it moves on."}

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/resposta-sobre-bun-em-rust-que-andrew-kelly-deveria-ter-feito -->
When the agents started tripping over each other, Sumner fixed the process.

The price is part of the lesson too: with dozens of agents in parallel, the usage bill ran high. Doing it right with agents doesn't come free. The numbers for the rewrite are in @sec-aptecnico-bun-2.

> The model executed work inside an engineering process. When the process was bad, the result was bad. When the process improved, the result improved.

### The Other Side of the Fight

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/resposta-sobre-bun-em-rust-que-andrew-kelly-deveria-ter-feito -->
The next day, Andrew Kelley, Zig's creator, published a response disputing part of the gains credited to Rust and asking, rightly, why the test suite was enough to trust a new codebase nobody had read line by line and not enough for the old one [@kelley2026bun]. My reading, in July, was that Rust was a defensible choice for Bun and that the evidence didn't prove Zig was unworkable: the method mattered as much as the language.

The arguments on both sides, and what two models said when I asked them to referee the fight, are in @sec-aptecnico-bun.

## So, Is It Safe?

<!-- src: original -->
Back to the question you brought to this chapter: is software written by AI safe to use?

The short answer is that it's as safe as the process around it.

The OpenClaw of March and the Bun of July were both written, in large part, by agents. One took in about fifty changes a day, a pace that made me doubt the review process, and an audit done by an AI, which I did not check item by item, flagged seven critical flaws in it. The other went through adversarial reviewers, a test suite as judge and an engineer watching every loop.

<!-- src: original -->
"It was made with AI" tells you very little. The same phrase describes ai-memory, which I maintain, where not one line was typed by hand and every contribution gets audited, and it also describes code nobody ever read. What tells you something is a different set of questions, and you can ask several of them without knowing how to program:

- does someone review changes before they go in, or do they go in faster than any person could read them?
- does the project have automated tests and continuous integration, and do they actually run?
- do the authors themselves use it day to day?
- when someone reports a security flaw, does a fix show up, with an explanation?
- will the program run with your passwords, your files, your money? If so, demand good answers to all four questions above.

<!-- src: original -->
For agents, which read outside text and have hands, add the letter rule. Assume that someone, at some point, will try to give it orders through an email, a web page or a message. Grant only the access the task needs, prefer tools that ask for your confirmation before deleting or sending anything, and keep the layers of protection from @sec-ch15: [sandbox]{.gls}, backups, no agent running loose in production.

> The danger of AI-written software lives in the speed: it gets dangerous when it ships faster than anyone can read it.

<!-- src: original -->
So far I've talked about code we choose to run: a gateway, an agent, a programming environment. There's a question that bothers more people, because it involves code almost nobody chooses with care.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
The apps on your phone are also code you never read. The difference is that, in 2026, reading got cheap.
