# Why AI Might Fail at Your Company {#sec-ch19}

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
In May 2026, a piece of news made the rounds among everyone who works in tech. Uber had burned through its entire 2026 budget for AI coding tools in four months [@angelo2026uber]. According to the reports, an internal leaderboard ranking teams by how much they used the tools helped push adoption along.

The company's chief operating officer, Andrew Macdonald, said on a podcast that it was "very hard to draw a line" between the Claude Code usage stats and more useful features reaching customers. About that link, he put it plainly: "That link is not there yet."

Notice what the story says: nobody there resisted the tool. They used it so much the budget blew up, and nobody could point to what had come out different on the other end.

<!-- src: original -->
If you're a manager, you may be living a smaller version of that story. You bought licenses, told the team to use them, and three months later it feels like everything is the same, only more expensive. If you're an employee, you may be the one who got a license on a Monday with orders to "be more productive" and no other instruction.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
In June 2026 I wrote about this in a post called "Why Will LLMs Fail at Your Company?" and I opened by warning that it was more speculation than a finished thesis. This chapter's title swaps "will" for "might" for exactly that reason, and the warning applies here too, right at the start. What follows is my hypothesis, drawn from what I've seen inside companies and from a year of using agents, with no research to prove it. The question, though, is real: why do companies spend so much on AI and see so little result?

My short answer, as a hypothesis, points at whoever decides, almost never at the model.

## People Trained Not to Decide

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
By this point in the book you know that a one-line request solves nothing. The fantasy of typing "build me an Uber clone" and getting back a finished product died quickly (@sec-ch07). The market's response in 2026 was to run in the opposite direction, under the name [spec-driven development]{.gls key=spec-driven}: development guided by a specification.

Instead of asking for everything in one sentence, you write a document. Scope, use cases, acceptance criteria, desired architecture, constraints, sometimes an entire requirements document. Then you hand that to the [agent]{.gls key=agente} and expect it to implement with less ambiguity.

It's better than the one-line request, of course. Even so, I think it misses the target.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
I've been hammering one idea since long before [LLMs]{.gls key=llm} became fashionable, in two 2019 videos from my channel, Akitando: "Não terceirize suas decisões" (Don't outsource your decisions), which appeared in @sec-ch09, and "Esqueça metodologias ágeis" (Forget agile methodologies) [@akitando063; @akitando051]. The summary, as I see it from what I've watched, is this.

> A lot of companies trained their professionals not to decide.

I don't have a number for this; it's the observation of someone who spent a career inside and around software teams. Think about your own working day. In a lot of places, nobody does anything unless there's a ready-made task list waiting.

When the task arrives, they execute. When it goes wrong, the requirement was to blame. When the customer complains, the priority was to blame. When the product gets worse, the process was to blame.

In those places, few people really decide, stand by the decision to the end and suffer the consequence. And the fault usually lies more with the organization, which punishes whoever decides and gets it wrong and rewards whoever follows the list, than with the person who picks up the task. Without suffering the consequence, nobody learns how to decide: which choices lead to which results, how to prioritize, how to explain why something needs to exist.

<!-- src: 2019/06/18/akitando-51-esqueca-metodologias-ageis-rated-r -->
In the video on agile methodologies, I pointed to where this habit comes from: fear. Whoever outsources the decision to a consultancy, a coach or the fashionable method does it because, having followed what everybody says to do, nobody can claim they got it wrong.

### The Ceremony

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
The Agile Manifesto was written in February 2001 by seventeen people gathered at a ski resort in Snowbird, Utah [@agile2001history]. The principles were good. What a lot of companies call agile today is bureaucracy organized into shorter cycles.

You may have seen the classic drawing on an office whiteboard. A backlog, a meeting to plan the two-week cycle (the *sprint*), daily meetings, a review at the end, a retrospective, and around it goes again. In practice, a lot of teams turned it into theater.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
An entire day goes into debating what makes the list. And what makes it is rarely what needs to be done: it's the consensus of least resistance. Small tasks, small risks, nothing that stirs up too much conflict.

Then the team spends weeks implementing it. QA finds problems, and the fixes go to the next round. A month later, maybe two, the original decision finally shows up in the product the customer uses.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
Except the result is so far from the decision that almost nobody connects the two. The team doesn't feel the weight of what it decided, the mistake never becomes learning, and the continuous improvement the Japanese call *kaizen* never happens.

What everybody learns is to stay invisible. To please management instead of the product, to survive the meeting instead of delivering value. When results get worse, the response from upstairs is predictable: more control, more rules, more committees, more checklists.

### Then AI Arrives

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
Until recently, the slowest part of making software was writing and adjusting code. Coding took days or weeks. That's why so much effort went into planning: if the team is going to spend two weeks implementing, it makes sense to spend a day arguing about what goes in.

Coding agents moved that bottleneck. They do a good chunk of the grunt work: they set up the structure, write basic tests, reorganize code, adapt integrations, build screens. They don't do it perfectly, but they do it fast enough to change the economics of the process.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
It's the old story of the chain and its weakest link. You strengthen one link and another one starts limiting the system. Before, the weak link was the speed of writing code. Now, more and more, it's the human ability to decide, explain, test and review.

The mistake companies make is dropping AI into the same broken process and expecting a multiplied gain. You take people trained for years not to decide, hand them a tool that demands clear decisions, and act surprised when garbage comes out faster.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
An agent needs you to explain the goal, the context, the constraints, the priorities, what must not break and how to measure whether it worked. That's what @sec-ch09 showed from the side of whoever writes the request. Now run a test on your team and ask each person:

- what is the real goal of what you're doing?
- how does this improve the product?
- which number should change when this is done?
- what trade-off are you accepting to get there?
- what must not break?
- which error cases need to be tested?

A lot of people can't answer. They know which task they picked up, they know they have to close a few a week, they know there's a meeting tomorrow. They don't know why it matters.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
And then you hand that person an AI. You saw in @sec-ch06, @sec-ch07 and @sec-ch10 that the tool hands back, amplified, what it receives from whoever uses it. In a company, it amplifies the whole organization: bad code faster, bugs faster, angry customers faster, giant change packages waiting for review faster.

Spec-driven development doesn't save this. If the document is born from the same cowardly process, it only documents the cowardice, and the agent executes a bad decision better.

## A Speculation About Process

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
From here on, it's hypothesis. I don't have enough data to prove what I'm about to propose, and I said so in so many words when I published the idea in June. What I have is the comparison between two kinds of scene: the failures I see in companies and the successes I see in intense individual use, like mine during the marathon in Part II.

My bet is that the whole process has to be redesigned around the new constraint. And the first step would be dropping the sprint as the central unit of work.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
It makes no sense to keep days of planning, days of review and weeks of coding as if the bottleneck were still writing code. With AI, the cycle needs to get much shorter, ideally within the same day.

![On the left, the sprint cycle, where decision and result sit weeks apart. On the right, the short cycle, where deciding, implementing, testing and reviewing fit in the same day.](assets/figures/en/ch19-ciclos.png){#fig-ch19-ciclos fig-alt="Two cycles side by side." fig-desc="Two cycles side by side. On the left, under the title sprint, a circle with four stages: planning, two weeks of code, testing and review; from review a long dashed arrow runs to the result, labeled one to two months. On the right, marked as a hypothesis, a programmer with an agent at the center and three people taking turns around them: PO, QA and tech lead; a short arrow leads to the result, labeled same day."}

### Four Chairs at the Same Table

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
In my ideal setup, each programmer with the agent would sit for short blocks of work with three people, one at a time:

- the product owner (the [PO]{.gls key=po}), who brings the goal, the priority and the business context, and decides on the spot: cuts scope, adjusts, accepts;
- the quality person (the [QA]{.gls key=qa}), who joins the same cycle to turn expected behavior into tests while the subject is still hot, covering the error cases programmers tend to forget;
- the tech lead, who looks at what changed the same day and cuts unnecessary complexity, bad design, obvious security holes, before they get expensive.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
Build, check, adjust. Then the PO moves on to the next programmer, and so do the QA and the tech lead. Instead of sitting still, each one circulates among the programmers.

<!-- src: 2021/09/28/akitando-104-rant-empreendendo-com-software-do-jeito-errado -->
The PO's chair is the one that least accepts outsiders. I spent a good part of my career on the service-provider side, first integrating systems with the SAP installations of large companies, then in my own software company. What I learned in both places is that you don't outsource the core of the business, because no contractor has the owner's worries. I say this as someone who provides outsourced services, and the agent is the cheapest contractor that has ever existed.

This model demands more responsibility from everyone. The programmer stops being the person who only picks up tasks: they need to understand why they're coding and discuss trade-offs. The PO stops being a backlog factory and starts deciding while looking at the live product. QA stops receiving the package at the end just to send back defects, and the tech lead stops being the late rubber stamp.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
And that's why a lot of companies won't like it. This process exposes decisions.

In the current process, the decision dissolves: it was the backlog, it was refinement, it was the team, it was the customer who changed their mind. In a short cycle, there's nowhere to hide.

You said that was the priority? Then implement it now. You said that was the acceptance criterion? Then test it now, and if it's wrong, the consequence shows up the same day.

It's uncomfortable. And that's how you learn: with the consequence close by, the decision becomes training.

### Throwing Code Away

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
The next step is accepting something professional programmers usually hate: you can throw code away. In fact, you should throw it away much more often.

In the traditional process, code gets too emotionally expensive. If it took two weeks to produce, nobody wants to admit it was waste. The manager feels that deleting it proves the earlier decision was bad, and the programmer defends the implementation because they suffered to make it.

<!-- src: 2018/11/01/akitando-23-the-mm-m-o-melhor-livro-de-software -->
The idea is old. In 1975, Fred Brooks gave a chapter of *The Mythical Man-Month* the title "Plan to throw one away": the first system almost always comes out unusable and goes in the trash anyway. To me it's the best book ever written about software [@brooks1975mythical]. What Brooks lacked was a cheap way to build the second version.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
With AI, making mistakes, prototyping and trying two paths to throw one away all got cheap. You can ask the agent to rewrite an entire approach in a temporary copy of the project, compare, write down what you learned and discard it without drama. The marathon in Part II was exactly that on a personal scale (@sec-ch08 tells the story of the failure that taught me to drop an approach).

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
Just don't expect the team to get the licenses on a Monday and figure this out on their own. That's the fastest way to burn the budget and then conclude that AI doesn't work.

My suggestion, still within the speculation, is the opposite. Set aside one or two weeks with no planned delivery and say clearly that the goal is to train the new process. In that phase, the PO doesn't write a twenty-page specification: they sit with the programmer and build the requests to the agent in small steps, watching the result in real time. If it goes wrong, great, delete it and try again.

<!-- src: original -->
It's little time next to the cost of an annual budget burned in four months.

## The Premise Changed

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
Three months later, in September 2026, I fired off a string of short posts on a social network telling everyone to stop holding code back and put more things live. A lot of people read it as "this guy is saying to ship any garbage to production without testing." The argument was much narrower and more boring than that.

The sentence that sums it up is this: it's better to put things live often ([deploy]{.gls}, in the jargon), because fixing things afterward became fast and cheap.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
The old excuse for holding code back used to be reasonable. A defect that reached production sat there longer than was reasonable, because fixing it never became a priority in the next sprint. So it was worth spending days reviewing everything line by line beforehand.

With the agents of 2026, fixing a bug after it shows up became a matter of minutes. And nobody reviewing a large volume of changes line by line catches everything. Idle code doesn't improve on its own: only code that gets used shows what needs improving.

<!-- src: 2021/10/11/akitando-106-recomendacao-de-livros-introducao-a-design-emergente -->
It's not the first time the price of the fix has changed the method. In 2021, in an episode about books and software design [@akitando106], I gave my reading of the history: designing everything before writing the code was essential from the 1960s to the 1980s, when every test went through a slow shared computer, and it stopped being essential when the personal computer got powerful enough to write first and refactor afterward. Agents lowered that price once more.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
In September I also wrote that the [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} of that month made fewer mistakes than a lot of the developers I'd seen go through code review. That was my impression, from having read plenty of code on both sides, with no measurement behind it, and it holds for one kind of mistake: the kind that breaks, shows up in a test or in use, and gets fixed in minutes.

<!-- src: original -->
The quiet mistake, the one that is just wrong and makes no noise, is another conversation. That one, as @sec-ch11 showed, survives if nobody audits on their own, and @sec-ch16 shows the danger of shipping code faster than anyone can read it. So the argument for shipping often doesn't rest on the model making few mistakes. It rests on the safeguards right below, and review stays inside them: automated, suspicious, and with a separate check for what makes no noise.

### This Is Twenty-Five Years Old

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
None of this is new. Kent Beck's [XP]{.gls key=xp}, which you saw in @sec-ch06, was already preaching small-batch delivery and [continuous integration]{.gls key=ci} at the end of the 1990s, on the idea that small batches reduce risk.

DORA, a research group that has published annual reports on how software teams deliver for more than a decade, reached a conclusion that bothers anyone afraid of putting things live. Speed and stability go together. In their words, their research "has repeatedly demonstrated that speed and stability are not tradeoffs," and the top performers do well across all the metrics at once [@dora2026metrics].

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
Even so, a large chunk of the industry keeps damming up change into huge batches and calling that prudence. The math that matters is different: how much time do you spend holding code back, versus how much time it would take to roll back if it went wrong? If the second number is smaller, you're paying a toll for fear.

> If reviewing and putting things live takes longer than building the feature, the bottleneck is the process, and AI won't fix that for you.

### The Locks That Make Speed Safe

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
This is the part that always gets lost in the argument. Putting things live often is only safe once basic engineering is working. If your infrastructure doesn't let you roll back fast, the problem is the infrastructure, and it needs fixing before any conversation about speeding up.

The minimum I consider mandatory, and that got cheap to maintain with agents:

- [automated tests]{.gls key=teste-automatizado} covering the logic, and [integration tests]{.gls key=teste-de-integracao} covering what happens when two things race each other or one fails halfway;
- a continuous integration server that runs everything on every change;
- a skeptical automated review before any change is merged into the project, one that treats whatever the author claimed as unverified until proven otherwise;
- a [feature flag]{.gls key=feature-flag}, a switch that turns the new thing on for only a slice of users first;
- a [staging]{.gls} environment, a copy of the real system that the change passes through before any customer sees it;
- the ability to [roll back]{.gls key=rollback}, return to the previous version in minutes, and monitoring to know when something broke.

![The layers that let you put things live often without fear: each one catches a kind of error before it reaches the customer, and the return arrow undoes whatever got through.](assets/figures/en/ch19-camadas.png){#fig-ch19-camadas fig-alt="Delivery pipeline combines tests, integration, review, feature flag, staging and production." fig-desc="A conveyor in two rows: change, automated tests, continuous integration and automated review on top; feature flag, staging and production below. Under production, a highlighted U-shaped arrow labeled rollback: back to the previous version in minutes. From production, a dotted arrow runs back to change, labeled monitoring warns that something broke."}

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
In health, finance or regulated environments, one more layer comes in: heavier scenario testing and more than one lock for each identified failure mode. That requirement holds no matter who wrote the code, because a human programmer's mistake takes down a payment system just as well as an AI's.

### Where the Math Doesn't Work

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
The best criticism I got in September hit on real points, and any manager needs to know them before running off to apply the idea:

1. Rolling back the code doesn't undo what has already left the system. If the defect processed a wrong payment or moved something physical, the damage is done. In those domains the review bar before shipping gets stricter.
2. Databases break the analogy. Undoing a version of the program is trivial; undoing a change to the structure of the data that has already run in production, and may already have deleted information, is another story. There's a careful way to handle that: add the new shape without removing the old one, wait for everyone to migrate, and only then remove it.
3. A public library used by other projects runs on different math. When my program breaks, I fix it on the spot. When something hundreds of projects depend on breaks, the people who pay are people who don't even know I exist.
4. App stores put their own review in the middle of your process, and you don't control how long it takes.
5. All the evidence I bring comes from projects where I'm the sole owner and decide alone. I have no way to prove, with my numbers, that the same pace works in a large company, with Legal and Compliance in the way.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
What I'd argue holds for any company is the principle, without my pace: build real rollback capability, automate the mechanical checking and measure the result against real usage, instead of against the feeling of safety. How much speed you apply on top of that depends on how much a mistake costs in your business, and nobody from the outside answers that for you.

### Who Really Reviews

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
The part I think matters most slips by in most discussions. Your own review is worth less than you think. What keeps quality up is published software being used by real people.

In September 2026 I went and counted the numbers for my agent memory system, an open source project. Of the 287 issues (bug reports and suggestions) filed up to then, 249 came from people who weren't me. Of the pull requests, 361 came from outside contributors (not counting the dependency-update bot), some 120 different people, whether or not I accepted the change.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/parem-de-inventar-desculpas-e-facam-mais-deploy-a-premissa-mudou -->
What keeps the project grounded is hundreds of people using it and finding cases I never imagined, far more than the AI or me reviewing. It's the report card from @sec-ch10 seen from the other side: the projects that were any good were the ones that had users.

<!-- src: original -->
For a company, the translation is direct. Real use by customers shows what no test shows, as long as the change reaches them small, controlled by a switch and reversible in minutes, and without skipping the review that comes first (@sec-ch16).

## The Moat and Adobe

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
So far I've talked about how the company works. What's left is what it sells.

In @sec-ch10 you watched the [moat]{.gls key=fosso} of code dry up, and my answer in March to "so what still protects me?" was exclusive data and capital. In October, that theory got the noisiest practical test of the year.

<!-- src: 2026/10/13/engenharia-reversa-com-ia-a-adobe-morreu -->
Since late September, an Atlanta developer, Brandon Thomas, had been publishing seven open source programs meant to replace Adobe's main products, from Photoshop to Acrobat, written with help from Claude Opus 5.5 [@pearl2026artcraft]. The same week, REA, a tool that hands your agent the [reverse engineering]{.gls key=engenharia-reversa} programs that already existed (@sec-ch17), passed 100,000 stars on GitHub. The consensus online came fast: Adobe is dead, no program is sacred.

<!-- src: 2026/10/13/engenharia-reversa-com-ia-a-adobe-morreu -->
I went and looked at what each of those things actually did. The Photoshop clone, PhotoCraft, said in its own notice that it was a very early version that doesn't yet replace Photoshop in professional work [@storytold2026photocraft]. A specialist site that tested it saw a superficial facsimile swallow 5 GB of memory minutes after opening a Photoshop file [@sneddon2026photocraft]. And REA warns, in its own legal notice, that the result may come out incomplete or wrong [@morluto2026rea]: useful for people who already know how to do reverse engineering, with no "copy this app for me" button.

![PhotoCraft in October 2026: the right body, another car's engine. AI-generated illustration.](assets/figures/ch19-ferrari.png){#fig-ch19-ferrari fig-alt="A yellow sports car hides a small, old, smoking engine under its rear cover." fig-desc="Illustration of a sleek yellow sports car, unbranded, with the rear engine cover open, revealing a small, old air-cooled economy-car engine giving off a wisp of smoke. Three people photograph the bodywork with their phones without looking at the engine."}

> The clone is a Ferrari replica built on top of a VW Beetle: the body has the right shape, you take a photo and everybody says "wow." Then you turn the key and out comes the Beetle engine.

<!-- src: 2026/10/13/engenharia-reversa-com-ia-a-adobe-morreu -->
I used Photoshop for years as a paying customer, and I hate Adobe's business practices as much as anyone. That's exactly why I know its value lives in three places no clone can take apart.

<!-- src: 2026/10/13/engenharia-reversa-com-ia-a-adobe-morreu -->
The first is the cloud. The features that sell the most subscriptions, like generative image fill, run on Adobe's servers [@adobe2026genai], and code that never leaves the server leaves nothing to take apart. That goes for any [SaaS]{.gls key=saas}, software sold as a service.

The second is contracts: print color is Pantone, a proprietary library, and in 2022, when Pantone changed its business model, the colors were pulled from Photoshop and anyone who needed them had to pay separately [@petapixel2022pantone]. If not even Adobe, paying, escaped that, imagine a clone.

<!-- src: 2026/10/13/engenharia-reversa-com-ia-a-adobe-morreu -->
The third is accumulated domain knowledge. GIMP, the open source image editor, was born in 1995, and version 3.0, from March 2025, still couldn't edit in CMYK, the print-shop color model [@cgchannel2025gimp]. Thirty years of an open project, with good, motivated people, and the print shop still asks for Photoshop. That knowledge lives in the color lab, almost none of it is in a model's training material, and no agent invents it.

> What's worth money in a mature product is rarely in the code you can copy. It's in the cloud, in the contracts and in decades of knowing the subject.

<!-- src: original -->
For anyone who owns or manages a software company, this completes the list from @sec-ch10: besides exclusive data and capital, cloud, contracts and the craft's accumulated knowledge also hold on to customers. Software with the entire binary in the customer's hands and none of that behind it is going to feel the pressure, and it should.

## Before You Renew the License

<!-- src: original -->
I'll sum up the chapter in a short list. It isn't a recipe, and the process part is still my speculation. But these are the questions I'd ask before renewing any license.

If you're a manager or owner:

- before measuring how much the team uses the tool, measure what reached the customer; a usage leaderboard rewards spending;
- shorten the distance between decision and result, until whoever decided sees the consequence the same week, preferably the same day;
- set aside training time with no delivery target, because nobody learns the new process under the pressure of the old one;
- invest in the locks (tests, continuous integration, release switches, a test environment, fast rollback) before asking for speed;
- ask where the company's moat lives, knowing that code alone is no longer one.

<!-- src: original -->
If you're an employee:

- learn to answer the six questions from the list earlier in this chapter about your own task, before you open the agent;
- decide, and own the decision; with AI, the executing part got cheap, and whoever knows how to decide became more valuable;
- lose your attachment to code: a throwaway prototype is the cheapest way to find out what doesn't work;
- be suspicious of anyone promising multiplied gains without changing anything about how you work.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
The closing line I wrote in June still holds. The change won't come from another ceremony, another spreadsheet of ready-made prompts for the agent, another AI usage policy or another sprint full of tasks written so nobody gets criticized. It will happen when people stop outsourcing decisions.
