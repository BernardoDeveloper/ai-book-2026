# From Zero to Production {#sec-ch06}

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
On February 10, 2026, I tossed an idea out on social media. A weekly newsletter, something that had sat on my to-do list for years, ever since I was publishing videos on YouTube. I never did it for two petty reasons: I hate HTML and CSS, and having to build an email template was enough to keep me from starting. On top of that, yet another newsletter that's just a boring list of links didn't move me one bit.

That day I was wrapping up FrankMD, the editor from the previous chapter, and I figured I had worked out the main tricks for building a complete product the right way. The ideas started coming one after another, and I dumped them all into a short document.

What if, besides the code being written with AI, part of the content was too? Each link of the week would come with two commentators: me and a robot. With *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* in mind, the robot had to be Marvin, Douglas Adams's depressed android [@adams1979guia]. I named him M.Arvin, so nobody would hassle me about copyright.

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
One idea dragged in the next:

- if there was going to be an email newsletter, it needed a blog with the archive of past issues, terms of service and a privacy policy;
- if the text was already written, why not rewrite the whole issue as a podcast script?
- Qwen3 TTS, an open synthetic voice model from Alibaba, had just come out [@qwen2026tts], and I have hundreds of hours of my own voice recorded on YouTube to train a version of it that talked like me;
- and, instead of an admin panel I'd end up too lazy to open, a Discord bot, where I already talk to people every day, so I could send links and comments from my computer or my phone.

I called the whole thing The M.Akita Chronicles. I started coding that same day, the 10th. I finished on Sunday, February 15. On Monday the 16th, the production jobs ran on their own for the first time: they assembled the newsletter, published it to the blog, generated the podcast, uploaded the episode to Spotify and sent the emails.

<!-- src: original -->
The previous chapter ended on a question. Up to that point, everything I had built with AI had a single user, me. Can one person alone ship a real product?

Yes. The way it gets done just looks very different from what shows up in the videos.

## The Day the Amateur Stops

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Somewhere around day two, with about fifty [commits]{.gls key=commit}, I was already thinking: "it works already!" And in fact, in two days I had the bulk of the features done.

That's exactly the point where someone who has never put a real system into production stops and posts: "I [vibe coded]{.gls key=vibe-coding} it in two days." It's the same "programmers are finished" video from the previous chapter, with a bigger system.

The rest of the week was the work that person skips. Deploying to a real server. Writing [automated tests]{.gls key=teste-automatizado} and testing the whole system end to end. Generating and regenerating every issue, exercising every step of every part several times, tweaking, reorganizing, and sometimes throwing away an entire piece because the tests showed it was worth redoing.

That's the part anyone would skip, by the way, with or without AI. It's why so many projects go live full of bugs and obvious security holes.

### What Was Under the Demo

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
When I stopped to run the numbers, a few days after the first send, here's what they were: 8 days, 274 commits, 4 applications (the bot, the newsletter, the blog and the voice server) and 1,323 automated tests. About 16,000 lines of functional code, about 36,000 counting the tests.

I took the 274 commits and sorted them by the kind of work each one did. Some commits fit more than one category, so the slices add up to a little over 100%.

Only 37% were new features, the stuff that shows up in the demo. The rest split between fixing bugs, tests, deploying and looking after infrastructure, documentation, security and hardening against failures. Each of those slices held between 8% and 16% of the commits. Anyone showing only the features is showing you a third of the software.

![Where the 274 commits went: a little over a third became new features; the rest was the work that never shows up in the demo.](assets/figures/en/ch06-commits.png){#fig-ch06-commits fig-alt="Horizontal bar chart with seven commit categories. New features, 37%, is the longest bar. Below it come bug fixes, 16%; tests and continuous integration, 16%; infrastructure and deployment, 11%; refactoring and hardening, 10%; documentation, 10%; security, 8%."}

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
There's a growing narrative that vibe coding means writing a detailed spec, handing it to the agent and getting the system back ready to go. Those are the "I built a SaaS in 10 minutes" videos. It looks magical until you look at what was actually delivered: a prototype with no tests, no security, no error handling, nothing that makes a system survive contact with real users.

And there's a detail that narrative ignores. Several of the important features in my system weren't even in the plan. They were born because something actually went wrong.

An automatic content check, for instance, only showed up after the second newsletter went out with an empty section, because a job had failed silently. No spec in the world predicts that. The correct system emerges as you build and fix.

## A Pair That Never Says No

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Vibe coding done the right way has a name: Extreme Programming.

[XP]{.gls key=xp}, as it's called, is a methodology Kent Beck put together in the late 1990s and published as a book in 1999 [@beck1999xp]. It's famous precisely for the "boring" and "bureaucratic" things every programmer loves to skip. For anyone who has never worked in software, the practices that matter here are five:

- **pair programming**: two people on the same code, one typing and the other reviewing and steering, switching roles;
- **tests first**: every feature is born together with the test that proves it works;
- **continuous integration**: every change automatically runs every test in the project, to see whether something broke somewhere else;
- **small releases**: short changes, each one ready to go live, instead of one big bundle at the end of the month;
- **coding standard**: an agreed way of writing code, kept up with constant cleanup.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
I didn't plan to follow XP item by item. These are the practices that emerge naturally when you program with fast feedback. Except now the partner in [pair programming]{.gls key=pair-programming} was Claude Code, Anthropic's [agent]{.gls key=agente}, running in the terminal.

### Who Drives and Who Navigates

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
I use the agent as a pair, and a pair talks. I interrupt when it takes the wrong path, I give it context it doesn't have, I question decisions, I ask for alternatives. Sometimes I say "stop, this is getting too complicated," and it simplifies.

In pair programming lingo, one drives and the other navigates. I navigate a lot more than I drive: I set the direction and correct course. The agent drives: it writes the code, runs the tests, proposes solutions. Whenever we flipped the roles, with me dictating exact code and it just typing, the result always got worse.

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
In practice, this starts before the first line. I tell the AI to plan first and spell out everything it's going to do. I review, I tweak, and only when I'm satisfied do I let it start. Then I watch every single thing it does, and at the end I tell it to fix whatever it missed.

Treat the agent like a hard-working human programmer, one who will make lots of mistakes but knows how to obey explicit, direct, unambiguous orders. My role in this pair is product manager, tech lead and QA, all three at once.

![The pair programming loop with an agent: the human decides the what and the why, the agent proposes the how, and nothing goes in without a test and a review.](assets/figures/en/ch06-ciclo-xp.png){#fig-ch06-ciclo-xp fig-alt="Circular diagram with five steps connected by arrows: the human asks for a small step; the agent plans and the human reviews the plan; the agent writes the code and the test; continuous integration runs every test; the human reviews and records the commit. An arrow back shows that, if a test fails, the loop returns to the agent."}

### Four Scenes from One Week

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The first was over-engineering. I asked for the system that handles sending the emails, and the agent's first proposal had a state machine with eight states, separate retry queues and special handling for dead letters.

I interrupted: "Simplify. Four states: pending, sending, sent, unknown. When in doubt, don't resend." Out came about 40 lines that solved the whole problem.

The second was knowledge the agent didn't have. To fetch stock quotes, Claude tried to hit Yahoo Finance like any ordinary program. I already knew Yahoo identifies and blocks any access that doesn't come from a real browser. That kind of knowledge comes from trying and failing.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The third was the most revealing one for anyone afraid of AI. In the first version, the weekly market recap was generated by an [LLM]{.gls}, and the LLM made up the market numbers, the same kind of fabrication @sec-ch04 discusses.

The fix was to swap in the real quotes and leave the model only the commentary. The result came out better and cheaper to boot.

The fourth was the characters' voices, and it gets its own section further on.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
All four scenes follow the same pattern. I bring the what and the why. The agent brings the how, and its how is often better than mine. But the how without the what produces correct code that solves the wrong problem.

### The Adult in the Room

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
After 274 commits, I had a pretty clear sense of where the agent gets it right and where it trips. It's very good at:

- boilerplate and scaffolding, which it produces at typing speed;
- tests, where it's surprisingly good at remembering edge cases;
- mechanical reorganization, like renaming, extracting and moving code around;
- quick research on how a technical standard or a library works;
- following the patterns the project has already set, without forgetting.

And it's weak at:

- architecture decisions, where it tends to over-engineer and needs constant braking;
- specific domain knowledge, like the Yahoo blocking;
- opinions, which it softens into mush;
- security on its own initiative, because it implements what you ask for and rarely suggests the protection you forgot to ask for;
- priorities, because it executes anything with the same enthusiasm and never says "this is a waste of time" or "do that first."

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The last item is the one that bites. On that project, the agent never told me "no." When I asked for something over-engineered, it implemented it with enthusiasm; when I asked for something insecure, it implemented it without complaining. You are the brake, the code review and the adult in the room.

<!-- src: original -->
Warning that it's stuck on a task is another matter, and it sometimes does that (@sec-ch09). What I didn't see was it disagreeing with a request it could carry out: saying the idea was bad or that something more urgent came first.

## The Safety Net

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The second XP practice is the one that looks most like a waste of time to anyone in a hurry. In [TDD]{.gls key=tdd}, test-driven development, every feature comes with the test that proves it works, and every bug fix comes with a test that guarantees that bug won't come back.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
On The M.Akita Chronicles I ended up with more lines of test than of code, close to 1.5 lines of test for every line of code, in both main applications. To someone who has never programmed, that sounds like overkill. To me it's what made going fast possible.

Every commit went through an automatic battery of checks, which ended by running all the project's tests. That's [continuous integration]{.gls key=ci}, or CI.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
And were the tests useful? The proof is empirical. Over the 274 commits, I saw CI fail and catch a real bug more than fifty times. The agent proposed a change, a test flagged that something that used to work had broken, and the change got fixed before it went into the project.

Without tests, the agent hands you plausible code that silently breaks something that worked the day before. You only find out when a user complains.

<!-- src: 2022/10/25/akitando-130-rant-projetos-testes-e-estimativa-rated-r -->
In October 2022, a month before ChatGPT, I kept repeating on the channel that "just because it compiles doesn't mean bugs haven't been introduced" [@akitando130]. Agent code that compiles, runs and passes the test the agent itself wrote is the same illusion, with a more convincing voice.

> With AI, tests matter more. Without them, every change the agent makes is a gamble.

### Small Commits, Always Ready

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The rule that ties all this together is simple: only commits with every test passing go into the project's main line. I can go back to any point in the history and the system runs. If a feature was a bad idea, I revert one commit, and nothing else depended on it.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Security followed the same principle. The fixes came spread across the eight days, instead of a "security week" at the end, and each hole was closed in the same commit where it showed up. What the automatic battery checked, the commit rhythm and the holes it caught are in @sec-aptecnico-xp.

### Two AIs Don't Find Everything

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Before going live, I asked Claude Code several times to review security best practices and test coverage. Then I opened Codex, OpenAI's agent, and asked for the same thing. It found more security holes and more missing tests, also several times over.

Even after both of them had run these checks repeatedly, I still found more holes on my own and had them fixed. At no point did either one find everything. In a system with money and subscriber data at stake, the lesson weighs even more: nobody finds everything, not even the AI.

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Even so, compared with a human team, I think the state I shipped in was better than what they would have delivered. Subscriber emails were encrypted in the database, so that not even an accidental leak would expose anyone, and I didn't install a single tracker or tracking pixel.

## Mocks Lie

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Automated testing has a trap, and it gets more dangerous when there's AI in the middle of the system.

Most of the tests programmers write check one piece at a time, isolated from the rest. To do that, they replace everything outside the piece with a [mock]{.gls}, a stand-in that pretends to be the real service and always answers the agreed way. The email service's stand-in says "sent," and the AI model's stand-in returns fixed text, written by hand.

The test runs in milliseconds and goes green. And the green lies: it says everything is working only because the stand-in answered the agreed way.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
My system had a problem those tests can't catch by design. It's made of pieces that pass files to each other: the bot writes the content of each section, the newsletter reads those files to assemble the issue, and the podcast reads the assembled newsletter to write its script. If one piece changes the format of what it writes, the next one breaks silently. Each side of the agreement gets tested separately, and the agreement itself never does.

### Real Data

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The solution was another kind of test, the [integration test]{.gls key=teste-de-integracao}, which runs the whole system together: the full production week on my machine, calling the real services, AI models included, only with the emails becoming files on disk instead of landing in someone's inbox.

And, most important, with real data. Before each run, I copied the actual content from the production server to my machine. Test data is too clean. Real data has the weirdness nobody remembers to make up.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Some bugs only showed up that way. Anime titles in Japanese lost their characters when turned into file names. The stock quote source started refusing requests after the fifth one, something that doesn't exist in a stand-in. None of these bugs would have been caught by an isolated test.

### Ran Without Errors, Came Out Wrong

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
With an AI model in the middle of the system, a trap shows up that traditional programming never had. A job that calls the model can run without a single error, finish with "success," and deliver a section with two items instead of ten, or an answer cut off halfway.

That's what gave birth to the automatic check from the beginning of the chapter, the one that came from the empty section in the second newsletter. It checks only that the structure is there: the file exists, it has the minimum number of items, it has the required markers. In my project, every model error I found was caught by it: truncated responses, wrong format, missing sections. How I dealt with the cost and with answers that change on every call is also in @sec-aptecnico-xp.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/discord-como-admin-panel-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
There was also the silent failure that got me from another angle. The bot had about ten daily jobs that fetched data from outside sites and posted it on Discord. The code looked "robust": if the fetch failed, it returned an empty list and moved on, no error, no alarm.

When I deployed and ran all ten, only one worked. In the logs, not a single error. One of the sources was rejecting an expired credential, and the code swallowed the rejection. Ever since, every failure leaves a log entry with the technical detail and a visible warning in the Discord channel I already check every day.

### Three Hundred Inboxes

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/testes-de-integracao-em-monorepo-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Without the integration test, I would have sent at least three broken newsletters. One missing the anime section, because of the feed that changed format. One with every video thumbnail pointing to images that didn't exist, because the address pattern had changed. One with the previous month's quotes, because the source blocked the requests and the job failed without a word.

The isolated tests would have passed in all three cases. CI would have gone green. And at seven in the morning on a Monday, 300 people would have received a newsletter full of holes.

The integration test takes work to set up, costs money on every run and complicates maintenance. It still comes out far cheaper than sending broken content to real subscribers, especially when you only get one shot a week.

> The confidence you can have in a system is proportional to how real the data in your tests is.

## The Voice the Model Erases

<!-- src: 2026/02/18/servindo-ia-na-nuvem-meu-tts-pessoal-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The podcast was the most ambitious part. Every week, with nobody touching anything, an episode of about 30 minutes with two voices, mine and M.Arvin's, generated from the newsletter text.

The voice was Qwen3 TTS's job, a [TTS]{.gls key=tts} model, which turns text into speech. I [fine-tuned]{.gls key=fine-tuning} it on recordings of myself, and it runs on a rented GPU in the cloud that only switches on when it's time to generate audio. By my February math, each episode cost somewhere between 50 cents and a dollar and a half in rented GPU time; the April numbers, much lower, are in @sec-ch13. Figuring out how to make it work cost dozens of hours of experiments, unusable audio and incomprehensible errors, the usual gap between the tutorial demo and production.

<!-- src: 2026/02/18/servindo-ia-na-nuvem-meu-tts-pessoal-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
But what taught me the most about AI was the script, which another LLM writes before the voice step. The newsletter has opinionated comments from me and from M.Arvin in every section.

When the model turned that into dialogue, it swapped the opinions for "balanced" versions. The Akita who said "this is nonsense" became an Akita saying "this has its merits, but...." Pessimistic Marvin became "mildly skeptical" Marvin.

The model does this because it was trained to be helpful and balanced, and strong opinions seem unhelpful. It's a close cousin of the tendency to agree with you that @sec-ch04 explains. My original instruction, "use the newsletter comments as a starting point for the conversation," was vague and let the model reinterpret at will.

<!-- src: 2026/02/18/servindo-ia-na-nuvem-meu-tts-pessoal-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The fix was to be explicit and inflexible: preserve the original opinions, change the way of speaking, never what the person thinks. Same for the characters. "Make Akita sound confident" produced generic confidence, the motivational-poster kind. What worked was describing how each one actually talks: Akita never says "maybe," Marvin stalls with "well..." and "anyway."

Without that level of detail, the two characters sounded the same. And it holds for any text of yours that passes through an LLM, be it an email, a report or a post: if you don't tell it to preserve your voice, it hands you back the average voice of the internet.

## Same Developer, Same Agent

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
All of this may sound theoretical. Everyone agrees, in theory, that tests and constant cleanup matter. As it happens, I had a nearly controlled experiment on my hands: the same developer, with the same agent, two weeks earlier.

On FrankMD I did what anyone does when it's "just a little personal project." I focused on features and left the rest for later: tests later, cleanup when needed, continuous integration when I remembered.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The single file holding the code that runs in the browser, the one you watched grow in @sec-ch05, started at about 500 lines. In a little over fifty commits it got close to 5,000. Bringing it back to an acceptable size took six big rounds of [refactoring]{.gls key=refatoracao}, each one moving hundreds of lines into dozens of new files, each one taking hours and risking breaking something. The real tests for the browser code only came after the file had bloated, because I needed them to be able to touch it without fear.

It was the cycle of build, build, build, stop everything and refactor. Programmers call what piles up in that cycle [technical debt]{.gls key=divida-tecnica}, and it charges interest.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
The M.Akita Chronicles had refactoring too, 27 commits of it, but all small, minutes long, protected by tests that already existed when the change was made. I'd say "this code is duplicated in four places, extract it," and the agent did in two minutes what would have taken me twenty. Deciding what to extract and how it should look was my call. Left alone, the agent just piles new code on top of the old.

Counting FrankMD's whole history, from the first commit to the week I took stock, it came to 212 commits in 19 days, an average of 11 a day. The M.Akita Chronicles did 34 a day, three times as many.

<!-- src: original -->
FrankMD's average mixes the three hyperfocus days of @sec-ch05 with much calmer ones, so the comparison has some slack in it. The reason for the gap, though, is written in the history: the disciplined project never had to stop everything to pay down debt.

> Same developer, same agent, two processes. What changed the outcome was the process.

### The Size of the Bill

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
How much work is that, in terms a manager understands? I looked at the 274 commits and tried to translate them into "user stories," the unit agile teams use to measure what they deliver. Not every commit is a story; many are fixes inside the same feature.

The count also doesn't come from the 101 commits of new features, the 37% in the chart: I counted by product, piece by piece, like each of the ten newsletter sections, each of the 15-plus bot commands, each blog component and each step of the email delivery. On a conservative count, done by eye by me, it came to between 180 and 200 distinct features, each one working, tested and live.

On a traditional team of two or three programmers, with code review, QA and meetings, a well-done story takes one or two days on average. By my math, that's about 30 weeks of work, or maybe 10 to 15 with a senior team and no impediments. I did it in eight days, one person and one agent.

<!-- src: original -->
Read that number carefully. It compares my work with that of a team I imagined, based on features I counted by eye, and that's why it comes out so much bigger than the other multipliers in this book. In @sec-ch10 I put them all side by side and say what each one measures.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
And here comes a huge asterisk. I have some 30 years of professional experience with software. I know which decisions matter, I know when to stop polishing and ship, and I often know the agent is wrong before it finishes writing. The project was also mine alone, with no review from anyone else and nobody to coordinate with.

The agent's real gain came from eliminating dead time. Without it, about 70% of my time would go to typing boilerplate, reading service documentation, writing mechanical tests and hunting typos. With it, that time disappears, and all of it is left for architecture, domain and quality.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
One more thing worked really well: a living document describing the project for the agent, the file Claude Code reads at the start of every session. It reached 702 lines, with the architecture, the conventions and a list of traps already hit, like the Yahoo one and the softened opinions. Every time we ran into a new problem, it went into the document, and in the next session the agent already knew. How to write that file is the subject of @sec-ch09.

### The Mirror

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
So, to answer the question from the beginning: yes, one person alone can ship a real product with AI, in days. But AI programming by itself is a recipe for disaster. What works has a name and decades of history, and the pair just stopped being human.

XP was right from the start. It was only missing a pair that never gets tired, never argues from ego, never says "but we've always done it this way," and reads 700 lines of documentation before every conversation without complaining.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
AI multiplies whoever is using it. A mediocre programmer with AI produces mediocre code faster. An experienced programmer produces good code at a speed that used to take a whole team. I put it in one sentence at the end of that week, and I stand by it:

> *"AI is your mirror, it reveals faster who you are. If you're incompetent, it'll produce bad things faster. If you're competent, it'll produce good things faster."*

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
If you program, my suggestion is to go through this at least once: a full week of immersion, detached from the code, using AI as your pair, going from zero to production on some small project. Until you do it, it's hard to get. Afterward, it changes the way you see the work.

<!-- src: 2026/02/20/do-zero-a-pos-producao-em-1-semana-como-usar-ia-em-projetos-de-verdade-bastidores-do-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
Except the week didn't end at the first send. The day after the first newsletter came another 31 commits, and the day after that, 24 more: reorganization, new bot commands, a backup job, fixes. The system was live and still changing.

And that wasn't my only project. By the first day of March I would have four projects published and taking changes, and every one of them was about to show me that "done" is the wrong word for software.
