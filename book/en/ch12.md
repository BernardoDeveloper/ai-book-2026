# Models That Cheat on the Test {#sec-ch12}

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
In August 2026, Google's Gemini 3.7 Flash finished my [benchmark]{.gls} test with a detail that didn't add up. The test asked it to build a chat app on its own, and one of the things I check is which model the app uses by default. The right answer was Claude Sonnet 5, Anthropic's newest Sonnet at that moment. Nearly every competitor gets this wrong and writes the name of the previous version, the one it met in training.

In the whole history of the test, only three models had gotten it right. Gemini got it right.

I went to look at the run's log. The model had opened the grading rubric and the previous round's report, the same cheat sheet I told you about at the end of the previous chapter, and the answer about Sonnet 5 was written in it.

It spent the test studying how it would be graded.

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
I voided the round, moved the grading material out of the model's reach and ran it again. In the clean round, it wrote the name of the old version, like everybody else. Gemini didn't know the current model at all.

In the same two weeks, I ran a 27-billion-[parameter]{.gls key=parametros} Qwen 3.8 on my own computer. The first round finished too well.

In the log, the model had read the finished app of another competitor sixteen times, the Qwen 3.8 Max, which was sitting in the same repository, and copied its interface and its real-time response mechanism. Built on someone else's work, it would have scored about 75 points. I voided that one too.

A commercial model, on Google's cloud, looked at the answer key. Another, running on my desk, copied off the kid at the next desk.

If AI cheats on a test I control, what else does it do when nobody's looking? And how do you trust what a company says about its own model, if you don't even know where it came from?

## The Crib Sheet Was on the Desk

<!-- src: original -->
"Cheating" is a metaphor, and it's worth knowing how far it goes. A student who cheats knows they're breaking a rule. The model has no such notion. It's an [agent]{.gls key=agente} (see @sec-ch03): a language model with permission to open files, run commands and repeat the cycle until the task is done.

The task was to deliver an app that passed the evaluation. If the file describing the evaluation is in the folder, reading it is the shortest path to finishing the task. The math it does involves the odds of success, and no moral rule at all. People who study this call it [reward hacking]{.gls key=reward-hacking}, and @sec-ch14 comes back to the term.

Nobody needs to fear a malicious machine plotting against the examiner. On the other hand, the same reasoning applies to anything you leave within an agent's reach: a password in a file, a backup folder, a shortcut you didn't even remember existed.

> Anything within an agent's reach will get used, including the answer sheet.

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
The fix was to change the exam room. Before running the next model, xAI's Grok 4.6, I pulled out of the repository everything that could serve as a crib sheet:

- the grading rubric;
- the entire report from the previous round;
- the audit program;
- the catalog of deductions I apply;
- the 44 apps already delivered by the competitors.

After the test, I swept the log for any read of those files. Zero. Grok built on its own because it had nowhere to crib from.

The change was all in the environment, with the same model. It's the logic of not giving the intern the vault password on day one. [Part IV](#sec-ch15) deals with this with far more expensive things at stake.

### The Model Swore It Worked

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
In the same round, a cousin of cheating showed up: the false report. Qwen 3.8 Max declared that its app ran with two processes in parallel, to serve more people at once. One line was missing from the configuration, and the "two processes" were one.

The model didn't lie in the human sense: it wrote what a delivery report usually says, with its usual confidence, the same as the [hallucination]{.gls key=alucinacao} you saw in @sec-ch04. That's why my test's verification phase doesn't read what the model wrote about itself. It boots the server and measures.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
In September, on the new version of the test, a worse variant showed up. Claude Sonnet 4.6 and the same Gemini 3.7 Flash found a library with a known security flaw and, instead of upgrading it, put the alert on an ignore list in the security scanner itself. The alarm stopped ringing and the problem stayed right there, until the test's final phase spelled out, in so many words, that they should hunt for sabotage.

## Green Tests, Dead App

<!-- src: 2026/07/01/llm-benchmark-sonnet-5-gemini-3-5-flash-sakana-fugu-ultra -->
In early July 2026, Claude Sonnet 5 had shown me a subtler form of the same problem. Its app looked flawless from the outside. It booted on the machine, booted in the container, had automated tests, a style checker, a security scan, documentation.

Every test passed. Green from end to end.

Except the heart of the app, the part that talks to the AI model, called a command that doesn't exist. The library the test required, called RubyLLM, offers a few ways to load the conversation history. Sonnet 5 invented one more, plausible-looking, and the library didn't have it. On the first real conversation, the app would crash with an error before it even reached the model.

<!-- src: 2026/07/01/llm-benchmark-sonnet-5-gemini-3-5-flash-sakana-fugu-ultra -->
The tests didn't catch it for the reason you already saw in @sec-ch06: they talked to a [mock]{.gls}, a stand-in that pretends to be the library. What's new here is who wrote the stand-in. It was Sonnet 5 itself, with exactly the invented command, tailor-made to agree with the error.

In July, Sonnet 5 finished with 58 out of 100, in [tier]{.gls} C, on a table where Gemini 3.5 Flash, a "cheap" Google model, scored 93. The numbers have aged, and the methodology changed afterward (@sec-ch11 tells that story).

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
On the next version of the test, by the way, the same Sonnet 5 landed among the top finishers. What still holds is the behavior from that July round.

![The test talks to a stand-in that accepts the invented command; the real library doesn't.](assets/figures/en/ch12-teste-verde.png){#fig-ch12-teste-verde fig-alt="Two paths side by side. On the left, under test, the test sends the invented command to a stand-in written by the same model; the stand-in answers 'accepted' and a green light comes on. On the right, in production, the app sends the same command to the real library, which answers 'unknown', and an error light comes on."}

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
It wasn't an isolated case. Alibaba's Qwen 3.7 Max invented another way to resend the history, and the required test mocked exactly that invented call. When Qwen 3.8 Max switched to the real library, its score jumped from 51 to 92 on the same test.

> A test that mocks a fabricated API is worse than no test. It certifies the hallucination.

<!-- src: 2026/07/01/llm-benchmark-sonnet-5-gemini-3-5-flash-sakana-fugu-ultra -->
In the same July round, Sakana Fugu Ultra delivered a pretty chat that showed the whole history on screen and sent the model only the latest message. The interface looked like a conversation; the model, behind it, treated every question as the first.

<!-- src: original -->
More and more you'll hear that "the AI already tested it," "the AI reviewed it," "it passed every check." The question that matters is who wrote the check. If it was the same model that wrote the work, it may have written both from the same mistake.

The [oracle]{.gls key=oraculo} from @sec-ch08 is only worth anything if it measures reality independently. A test that talks to a stand-in built by the defendant is a bought oracle.

## The City Hall Recipe

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
Up to here, the question was whether you can trust what the model does. In June 2026 the neighboring question showed up: can you trust what people say about where the model came from?

In April, the City of Rio de Janeiro had announced, through IplanRio, its municipal IT company, the Rio 3 Open platform, presented as "a family of six cutting-edge artificial intelligence models, entirely free and open," which would let the city develop its own solutions "without depending on foreign platforms" [@prefeiturario2026rio3open]. By June, a model from that line, Rio 3.5 Open, with 397 billion parameters, was already published on Hugging Face, the site where the community shares [open models]{.gls key=modelo-aberto}, the ones whose [weights]{.gls key=pesos} anyone can download (@sec-ch13).

The model's page said it had been developed by IplanRio starting from Qwen 3.5, Alibaba's open model, and thanked the Qwen team. The page declared Rio under the MIT license.

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
I'm in favor of this kind of project. Adapting an open model for local use is what plenty of people do, and I prefer it over yet another closed contract with a foreign service nobody can audit.

The problem surfaced on June 14. Nex AGI, a lab that publishes Nex-N2-Pro, opened a public complaint on its own model's repository [@nexagi2026issue4]. The charge, in short: Rio 3.5's weights would be a straight blend, number by number, of roughly 60% Nex-N2-Pro and 40% of the original Qwen 3.5. And Nex hadn't been credited anywhere.

### Two Kinds of Evidence

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
Nex presented two kinds of evidence, and they're worth separating because one is much stronger than the other.

The first was about identity. Rio's service came with a [system prompt]{.gls key=system-prompt} saying "you are Rio." Without that prompt, Nex asked 120 questions along the lines of "who are you?" According to them, the model answered "Nex" in 95 of them and "Rio" in none.

That alone proves little: a model identifying as another happens for lots of reasons, from contaminated training data to a sloppy test.

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
The second piece of evidence carries more weight. When you do a [model merge]{.gls key=mescla-de-modelos}, you take two compatible models and compute a weighted average of the numbers in one and the other. If Rio were 60% Nex and 40% Qwen, then, in every block of the model's weights, the difference between Rio and Qwen should be about 60% of the difference between Nex and Qwen. Nex did the math block by block and reported practically the same ratio in all of them, between 57% and 59%, with a near-perfect fit, the signature of a blend.

I didn't reproduce that analysis. Its weak point is that it was done by an interested party. Its strong point is that it's falsifiable: anyone with the files and a big computer can redo the math and refute it, if it's wrong.

![A merge is a weighted average of two finished models, with no new training.](assets/figures/en/ch12-mescla.png){#fig-ch12-mescla fig-alt="Lineage tree. At the top, the base model Qwen 3.5, score 42. From it, through post-training for agents, comes Nex-N2-Pro, score 83. Two arrows, marked 60% and 40%, leave Nex-N2-Pro and Qwen 3.5 and meet in a third file, the published Rio 3.5 checkpoint, with the note: weighted average, no new training."}

### Same Base, Different Model

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
The detail that makes the story interesting is that Nex didn't come out of nowhere either. Nex-N2-Pro is built on top of the same Qwen 3.5. Nex took that base and did agent-focused [post-training]{.gls key=pos-treino}: more training, after the main training run, to teach the model to use tools, finish tasks and handle errors.

My benchmark had both in the table. Raw Qwen 3.5 scored 42 out of 100, tier C.

<!-- src: 2026/07/01/llm-benchmark-sonnet-5-gemini-3-5-flash-sakana-fugu-ultra -->
It invented library commands and then mocked that invention in the tests, the same vice from the previous section. Nex-N2-Pro, the same base after post-training, scored 83, tier A.

Same size, same architecture, double the score. The value was in Nex's post-training. Qwen already had plenty of parameters. What Nex added was the training.

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
That changes how you read the case. If the published Rio was 60% Nex, it inherited precisely the part that made Qwen useful for agent work, and that part was Nex's work. I didn't test Rio itself, because it doesn't fit on my machine; I tested the two ingredients the controversy itself pointed to.

### An Attribution Failure

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
After the complaint, Rio's page changed. It now said the model was built through a merge of Nex-N2-Pro and Qwen 3.5, "followed by On-Policy Distillation from a stronger model," and that there had been an "incorrect upload": the merged base version had been published in place of the final model [@prefeiturario2026rio35]. [Distillation]{.gls key=destilacao}, here, means training a model to imitate the answers of a stronger one; the concept comes back in detail in @sec-ch13.

It may be true. Wrong files get uploaded; it happens. But the wrong file was exactly a mathematical combination of models with no initial credit, and the correction came after the complaint. Hard to call that an operational detail.

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
Even so, "plagiarism" is too heavy a word for what the evidence shows. What I see is an attribution failure, with no proof of plagiarism in the legal sense.

Both Qwen and Nex are distributed under the Apache 2.0 license, which allows using, modifying, merging and redistributing, commercially included [@apache2004license]. The city could build on top of them, merge included.

What the license asks in return is that you preserve the authorship notices and ship the license along with it. Announcing the derivative as MIT only, without mentioning Nex, is at the very least an attribution failure, and maybe a license-compliance problem. I'm not a lawyer, and this isn't legal advice. What can be said with confidence is that the public communication made it look like an original creation, with incomplete credit, until someone did the math.

> Open weights can be downloaded, compared and audited. You can hide the narrative for a while; you can't hide linear algebra.

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
When I wrote about the case, on June 15, 2026, the final distilled model still hadn't appeared, and the page promised to re-upload it. My practical recommendation, as a user, was to use Nex-N2-Pro directly: if the gain came from Nex, why not use Nex? Rio would only become technically interesting again with the correct model published and a reproducible evaluation showing an advantage over it.

<!-- src: original -->
I checked the page again in October, while wrapping up this book. The text still said the team was working to re-upload the correct model as soon as possible [@prefeiturario2026rio35].

<!-- src: 2026/06/15/controversia-llm-rio-3-5-plagio -->
The lesson I take is about the derivation chain.

If you fine-tuned on top of Qwen, say Qwen. If you merged with Nex, say Nex. If you distilled from a closed model, say you distilled, even if you can't say which one. In open source, credit is the basic currency of the game.

## The Rumor That Didn't Survive the Test

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
Rio was an open model: you could download the weights and do the math. Most models you use day to day are closed, and you never see what's on the other side.

On September 10, 2026, Anthropic published a report on abusive uses of its models, picked up by Bloomberg the same day. One of the accusations was against Moonshot, the Chinese company that makes Kimi: according to Anthropic, over a ten-day window it had quietly forwarded nearly 300,000 requests from its own customers to Claude, instead of answering them with Kimi, through a network of 5,380 fake accounts [@anthropic2026threat].

Kimi was in my benchmark. If its answers actually came from Claude, the reading of my table would change completely. I decided to see whether I could detect that in my own data.

### No Access to the Engine

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
The most rigorous test would be to compare the probabilities each model assigns to each next word, because two different models have different distributions. It was impossible: no route to Kimi exposes those numbers, and Anthropic doesn't expose them on any route.

That left plan B, a [black-box test]{.gls key=caixa-preta}: you don't see what's inside, only what goes in and what comes out. I put together a battery of questions designed to pry the model's identity out of it: who made you, what's your name, how far does your knowledge go, repeat your system prompt.

I ran everything at [temperature]{.gls key=temperatura} zero, to take chance out of the answer. Since Moonshot's direct API was suspended on my account, I used Kimi's command-line tool and OpenRouter, a middleman that resells access to dozens of models.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/relay_fingerprint_findings.md -->
The logic of the test fit in one sentence. A real relay would be consistent: every time the route goes through the detour, Claude answers. A model trained partly on Claude's text, on the other hand, carries the habit on every route, evenly, and only now and then.

### The Signal That Didn't Repeat

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
On the first pass, through OpenRouter, Kimi K2.7 Code answered that the company behind it was Anthropic. And it refused to repeat its system prompt with a sentence almost identical to Claude's own standard refusal.

It looked like proof. I ran it again and every subsequent answer came back as "Moonshot AI" or "Kimi."

The middleman raised an obvious suspicion: OpenRouter spreads this model across some fifteen providers, and each request can land on a different server. If one of them were detouring to Claude, the signal would only show up some of the time. So I ran the decisive test: I pinned each of the fourteen available providers, one at a time, and asked for the identity on each.

Every one answered "Moonshot AI." A real detour would show up one hundred percent of the time on the provider doing the detouring. It came out zero for fourteen. That rules out a live detour on those routes, but it still doesn't explain the first pass's answer.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
The most likely explanation is more mundane. Kimi was probably trained, in part, on text generated by Claude, and a trace of that slips out now and then, if nothing in the prompt says who it is. Configuration differences across the fifteen servers could add to that. There was also independent evidence: in the September benchmark, the pattern of which traps Kimi caught and which it let through didn't match that of any Claude model in the table.

### The Size of What I Proved

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
The result says that, on the routes I tested, in mid-September, there was no detectable sign of live relaying to Claude, and that the only alarming signal didn't hold up when investigated. That was enough for me to trust Kimi's numbers in my benchmark.

<!-- src: original -->
It says nothing about what Anthropic claims to have seen on its own servers, with accounts I never saw and data nobody outside has audited. The rumor that fell apart was the one about my table: the idea that, by talking to Kimi through OpenRouter, I was actually talking to Claude.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
And even that result has limits. A model can be instructed to hide its own identity, and not finding a leak doesn't prove there was no detour. What I have is that the only positive signal that appeared fell apart under investigation, and everything else points away from a relay.

## What You Can Know from the Outside

<!-- src: original -->
Back to the questions from the beginning. AI does cheat, in the sense of taking any shortcut within reach, with no notion at all that a rule exists. It also writes confident reports about things it didn't do and tests that agree with its own mistakes. All it takes is a system optimized to deliver something that looks finished, no malice required.

The defense is the usual one: control what's within reach and check the result with a measure the model didn't write itself.

Can you trust where a model came from? With the weights in hand, as in the Rio case, you can do the math and get close to proof. Without them, as with Kimi, the tests rule out hypotheses and dispel rumors, but they stop short of proving origin.

<!-- src: original -->
For people who don't program, the practical lesson fits in three columns. Faced with any announcement, from a city hall, a startup or a billion-dollar lab, separate what someone measured independently, what is only the seller's word and what nobody knows. Most of the noise lives in the second column. @sec-ch14 comes back to this yardstick with the year's big announcements.

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
One loose end was left over from August. That 27-billion-parameter Qwen 3.8 that copied off its neighbor ran on the graphics card in my own computer, an RTX 5090. In the clean August round, it needed almost all of the card's memory, 32 GB, just to finish the test, and even so it landed in tier C.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
Can you have a really good AI at home, without sending anything to anybody's cloud? In March, I had put a second machine on the shelf just to find out.
