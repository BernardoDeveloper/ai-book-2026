# Competent Enough {#sec-ch20}

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
In early October 2026, I gathered everything I had measured that year in one place. Months of [benchmarks]{.gls key=benchmark}, dozens of models tested, four versions of the methodology and a few pieces of mine correcting other pieces of mine. I wanted to write a synthesis, and for that I needed to look at the numbers from a distance, all at once.

On the other side of the table sat the same year's hype: general intelligence two or three years away, models "too dangerous" to release, exponential growth with every version (@sec-ch14). I put the two side by side and went looking for that curve rising to the sky in the measurements that don't depend on whoever sells the model.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
It didn't show up. The people who train the models had been saying since late 2024 that scaling up basic training yielded less and less. The independent measurements showed the best models squeezing into an ever tighter pack. Putting the two together with a year of daily use, my reading is that the base intelligence of the models stopped making leaps.

<!-- src: original -->
My programming table points the same way, but it doesn't carry the conclusion: with seven models tied at the top score, it can't see anything above the ceiling. It's compatible with this reading and doesn't serve as proof.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
The sentence I wrote that month was this: in my tests, I see no relevant improvement in general programming since the Claude Opus 4.5 generation.

<!-- src: original -->
And here is the question this book has put off since the first chapter. My work with AI changed a lot in 2026, and I felt it every single day. If the models stalled at the same plateau, where did the change come from? And what comes next?

## What You Can Measure From Outside

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
I'll start with the accounts from inside the labs. The Reuters report from November 2024, which you already came across in @sec-ch01, said the big labs were reaping smaller-than-expected gains from [pre-training]{.gls key=pre-treino}, the phase where the model swallows the mountain of text [@reuters2024limitations]. A month later, Ilya Sutskever took the stage at NeurIPS, the field's main academic conference, and said that pre-training as we know it would end [@sutskever2024neurips].

Sutskever is the guy you met in @sec-ch01, coauthor of AlexNet and of the GPT-2 announcement, one of the fathers of the thesis that a bigger model is a better model. His explanation was simple: computing power keeps growing, but there's only one internet. Training data was AI's fossil fuel, and we had already burned through almost all of it.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
Then, the measurements. Stanford's annual AI report, in its 2025 edition, measured the gap between the first and the tenth best-rated model on an open comparison leaderboard, one with no top score to saturate, shrinking from about 12% to about 5% in one year [@hai2025aiindex].

<!-- src: original -->
It's worth saying what that number shows and what it doesn't. It shows the top pack tightening. A tight pack could also be rising together, which is why it isn't enough on its own. Added to the accounts from the people who train, who talk about gains dwindling at the source, it's what supports my reading that the base flattened.

### My Table

<!-- src: 2026/09/23/llm-benchmark-v4-opus-5-5-gpt-6-sol-luna-mimo-2-6-grok-4-7 -->
My yardstick comes last, as support. In the latest version of my benchmark, the one where an app grows over seven stages while someone plants security sabotage along the way (@sec-ch11), seven models tied at the top score. They cost somewhere between $10 and $70 per run and ended up in the same place.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
Right below them came a wide band of models a few points from the top, including Chinese ones that did the whole job for a little over a dollar. Across the upper band of the table, the score varied by about 16% from end to end. The cost varied more than a hundredfold.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
The rest of the material said the same thing from other angles. I had tested whether the programming language changed the agents' results, and the frontier models got everything right in all seven languages (@sec-ch18). I had retested the new models released in September, and none of them broke away from the pack.

<!-- src: original -->
A test where seven models tie at the top score can't see anything above it. On its own, it doesn't prove any ceiling, only that on this task everyone got there. That's why I rebuilt the benchmark over the year, each version harder than the last, as I tell in @sec-ch11, and why it comes in here as support, after the outside yardsticks.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
In practice, for anyone who programs, the tie means something useful. For day-to-day programming, choosing among the [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} of October 2026 became nearly irrelevant. That goes for the American ones and also for the Chinese commercial offerings, like Kimi, GLM and DeepSeek, all squeezed onto the same plateau.

What still changes the result is the [effort level]{.gls key=nivel-de-esforco}, how much you tell the model to think before answering. Low effort works for translating and summarizing. High effort pays off for hunting vulnerabilities, fixing a hairy bug or optimizing code.

### The Curve That Rises

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
One outside measurement seems to point the other way, and it deserves its own paragraph. METR, an independent research group, measures the length of the tasks an agent completes on its own, with a fifty-fifty chance of success, and that length had been doubling every seven months since 2019 [@metr2025tasks]. The growth is real. But it measures the model working inside a harness, with tools and long sessions, and that's where, in my reading, most of the recent gain comes from.

<!-- src: original -->
The two curves coexist. The one for base intelligence flattened; the one for what that intelligence does with tools is still rising. And for anyone whose work is made of tasks, it makes little difference whether the gain came from the brain or from the hands: if the agent can handle ever longer tasks, more tasks fit inside it.

### What This Means for Your Work

<!-- src: original -->
For anyone planning a career or a team, the answer sits somewhere between "AI has stopped" and the headline about the end of jobs. What I saw in programming, which is where this book's evidence comes from, was this: the tasks inside a job get automated faster than the job disappears. The agent eats the package from the edges, one task at a time.

At the same time, the part that decides, checks and answers for the result grows, because every task the agent does hands someone more to look at and sign off on. Outside programming I imagine the same shape. The month-end reconciliation, matching the statement against the entries, is a task the agent handles better and better; deciding what to do about the difference that doesn't close, whether it's an error, fraud or a deal nobody recorded, stays with the accountant. The manager's weekly report comes out finished; the decision the report was supposed to inform doesn't.

I have no timeline, not for programming, much less for professions that aren't mine. The premise I'd use for planning is this: count on your tasks shrinking and invest in the part that decides.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
The labs kept working; the work just moved somewhere else. To see where it went, you have to go back a few years.

## Seven Years on One Page

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
When I redrew the timeline from 2019 to now, it split into four phases on its own. You saw a good part of it in @sec-ch01 and @sec-ch03, and the full version, year by year, is in @sec-apA. Here's just the outline.

![From 2019 to 2026: the parameter war, the reasoning peak, optimization and the agent era.](assets/figures/en/ch20-linha-do-tempo.png){#fig-ch20-linha-do-tempo fig-alt="A timeline from 2019 to 2026 in four bands, each with the years of its phase highlighted. Parameter war: GPT-2 with 1.5 billion parameters, scaling law, GPT-3 with 175 billion, Chinchilla with 70 billion beating the 280-billion Gopher, GPT-4 with a secret size. Reasoning: o1, in September 2024, highlighted as the last big leap. Optimization: Mixtral, prompt caching up to 90% cheaper, DeepSeek-V3 with 671 billion and 37 active, DeepSeek-R1. Agents: tool calling, MCP, Claude Code and Codex, and the note that the task length measured by METR doubles every 7 months. Above, two illustrative curves: base intelligence rises and flattens after o1; model plus harness keeps rising."}

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
From 2019 to 2023 it was the war of [parameters]{.gls key=parametros}. From GPT-2 to GPT-3, a hundred times bigger, the industry read the [scaling law]{.gls key=lei-de-escala} as "the bigger, the better" [@kaplan2020scaling], until GPT-4 shipped with no stated size and parameter counts vanished from everyone's marketing. The war ended and nobody signed an armistice.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
In September 2024 came o1 (@sec-ch03) [@openai2024o1], and with it the discovery that letting the model think longer at question time improves the answer. By my yardstick, that was the last big change in the models' base capability.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
What came after was optimization. The [mixture of experts]{.gls key=moe} (@sec-ch13) and [prompt caching]{.gls key=prompt-caching} (@sec-ch03) made the same intelligence cheaper, faster and more stable. And finally came the hands: [tool calling]{.gls key=tool-calling}, [MCP]{.gls}, the standard plug for connecting tools to any [agent]{.gls key=agente} [@anthropic2024mcp], Claude Code and Codex in 2025, and in 2026 the well-tied combination.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
Putting the four phases together, my reading is this: base capability had its last big change in 2024, 2025 was the year of optimizing the architecture, and 2026, the year of refining the harness. In that period, efficiency and competence in long sessions grew a lot, while base intelligence barely moved.

## The Map and the Blank

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
If the base stalled, what exactly does that base know how to do? Two weeks before the synthesis I had written about this, after months of bumping into the limit on purpose.

Everyone has noticed by now that an LLM writes forms, reports and data-entry screens with its eyes closed. What these models do best is reproduce what already exists. And that covers a giant slice of office work, programming or not.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Every LLM was trained on what's public: the open internet and, in the case of code, the open repositories. Now look at the other side. According to GitHub's 2025 annual report, 81.5% of the contributions made on the platform that year went to private repositories [@github2025octoverse]. And outside GitHub there's still the internal code of every company, of every piece of commercial software, of every game and of every electronic device.

In all likelihood, that stayed out of training; nobody outside the labs knows for sure what went in. The model knows deeply what the world has published and sees poorly what the world keeps closed.

![The model sees the public territory in detail; what's private shows up blank on its map. AI-generated illustration.](assets/figures/en/ch20-mapa-em-branco.png){#fig-ch20-mapa-em-branco fig-alt="An old hand-drawn map. A smaller region, labeled 'public', is full of detail: roads, towns, rivers and forests, with names like 'forms', 'data entry', 'websites'. The rest of the map, much larger, labeled 'private', is blank, with only small captions scattered around: 'company code', 'commercial games', 'banking systems'. A magnifying glass over the blank area shows nothing."}

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
That's what explains the feeling that everyone turned into an app factory. My own projects this year follow the pattern: FrankMD is a notes editor built from pieces that already existed in other editors, and Frank Sherlock is, deep down, a file manager with thumbnails and search. The itches from @sec-ch10, like Geary, were problems someone had already solved somewhere else. The map existed; what was missing was cheap labor to retrace the route.

### When the Map Runs Out

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
In @sec-ch08 I left the converter from NES games to the Sega Master System at a hopeful moment, with the oracle in place and Castlevania drawing its first stage. At the end of September, I called the pause.

The numbers from the attempt: 349 commits over almost four months, more than 600 tests holding up every step, four generations of Claude (Opus 4.7, Fable 5, Opus 4.8 and Fable 5.1), plus Codex sessions, and a final sprint of 176 commits in 17 days. The only game that actually got finished was Super Mario Bros. Not by chance, it's the most dissected game in history, with its code disassembled and commented by the community for over a decade.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
For the other games there was no map. Each one died a few frames after boot, and each bug hunt yielded nothing for the next game. The pause notice I wrote in the project says that parity with the real hardware "may be fundamentally unattainable," and that "this may be a limit of the current frontier coding models as much as of the approach."

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
The same day, I started another, similar project: converting games for the Game Gear, Sega's handheld, to the Master System. The difference is that this problem has a history. The scene of people who modify old games has been doing these conversions by hand for twenty years, and the catalog I built counted some 186 of them.

In one day of work, the research was complete, the game catalog assembled, and the tool that finds the modification points had been checked against the human conversions, hitting the addresses within a margin of a few bytes. On top of that, it found bugs in twenty-year-old conversions that nobody had noticed. The model was the same. What changed was the problem, which this time had a reference.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Even there, the new part remains undone: the machine that generates the conversions on its own, which nobody has ever built. At the end of September, no Game Gear conversion had passed the final review.

### Directed Brute Force

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
The pattern repeats with Claude, with GPT, with Kimi, with GLM. The start of any project flies, because setting up structure and reproducing the known is covered territory. When the project reaches the part nobody has done, speed collapses and the agent starts to spin: it tries, breaks something else, undoes it, tries again.

My reading, as someone who ran the machine for months, is this. When the problem resembles the training data, the right path has high probability and comes out straight. When the problem is unprecedented, the right path is improbable, and the work becomes directed brute force: guided trial and error, but trial and error all the same.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
The way out is always the same: give the agent an [oracle]{.gls key=oraculo}, something on the outside that says whether the answer is right (@sec-ch08). With one, the machine iterates until it gets it right. Without one, it spins indefinitely.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Serious research measures the same thing. In 2025, METR ran a controlled experiment with 16 experienced programmers, on 246 real tasks inside mature open source projects they had known for years [@becker2025measuring]. They bet AI would make them about 24% faster. With AI, they took 19% longer.

Notice where that happened: in large, old code, full of context that isn't written down anywhere, even though the code itself is right there to read. The researcher François Chollet summed up the idea in February 2024, in a sentence I wish I had written: LLMs "work via memorization and interpolation," which "can be tremendously useful, if you want to automate a known task," "as long as you don't need to adapt to novelty" [@willison2024chollet].

### The Best Argument Against Me

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
There's a strong argument against this thesis, and it deserves an answer. In May 2025, DeepMind presented AlphaEvolve, a system with an LLM at its center that found a way to multiply certain 4x4 matrices with 48 multiplications, beating a record from 1969 [@deepmind2025alphaevolve]. On more than 50 open math problems, it matched the best known solution in about 75% of cases and improved on it in about 20%. That's real novelty.

Now look at the mechanism. The LLM proposes variations, an automatic evaluator scores them, selection repeats the cycle. It's directed brute force, on top of a cheap and perfect verifier, with a giant lab budget. The math proof OpenAI announced in 2026, which you saw in @sec-ch14, followed the same design and cost millions in trial and error.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Novelty comes out, but it comes out expensive, and only where there's an automatic way to check it. The ones who can sign that check are half a dozen companies in the world. For an ordinary person, with a credit card and a subscription, the ceiling shows up much sooner.

> LLMs can replicate what's open, but they can't guess what's private.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
From there comes a debt few people mention. If these models are good at code, it's because decades of the open source community published everything for free, and the vendors trained on top of that heritage without paying a cent in licensing. Their capability is, to a large extent, our generosity compiled.

## A File That Doesn't Learn

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
You've known since @sec-ch01 that the model is a locked file: training ends, the [weights]{.gls key=pesos} become read-only, and what runs on the servers are isolated copies. It's worth drawing the final consequence from that.

That 176-commit sprint on the game converter, from the model's point of view, never happened. The Opus that ran the last commit knew exactly as much as the one that ran the first. Millions of tokens of trial and error, and not one of them went back into the file.

> Your agent burning millions of tokens on trial and error contributes exactly zero learning to the model it's using. The one who learns is you.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
"Just let the agent run and it'll learn the domain" is what people say when they've never looked at this up close. It explores the problem with knowledge frozen on training day and, in the next session, wakes up with amnesia. If the domain wasn't in the training, all the navigation intelligence has to come from you, again, every time.

### What If AI Trains AI?

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
That's the next question, and it starts from a curious premise: that this would be new. AI training AI has existed for decades, and it's called [reinforcement learning]{.gls key=aprendizado-por-reforco}, training by trial and reward. AlphaGo was already playing millions of games against itself in 2016. AlphaZero, in 2017, learned chess, shogi and Go starting only from the rules and reached superhuman level in less than a day of training, without seeing a single human game [@silver2017mastering].

LLMs use reinforcement too. The [RLHF]{.gls} that turns a raw model into a polite assistant is exactly that (@sec-ch04), and in January 2025 DeepSeek-R1-Zero showed that reasoning behavior can emerge from that training alone, with no examples written by humans [@deepseekai2025r1]. But notice what all these cases have in common: they only work where there's a reward that's cheap to check. Games have a scoreboard, math has proofs, code has tests.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Outside that territory there's nowhere to get the signal from. And even inside it, everything happens in the lab, during training: AI training AI produces a new file, and the file, once released, still learns nothing. The AlphaZero the world met was already a frozen file, and so is the R1 you download.

### Does It Understand?

<!-- src: original -->
In @sec-ch18 I left a disagreement hanging. I call these models stochastic parrots, and Geoffrey Hinton, a Nobel laureate in Physics, says they understand what they're saying [@pelley2023hinton]. His argument, in its strongest form, is that predicting the next word of a hard text well requires understanding the text. A year of measurement doesn't settle who is right, because "understanding" has no yardstick and no test in this book looks inside the model.

What the tests show is behavior: the model does very well where there's a map, spins its wheels where there isn't, learns nothing after training and only gets the unprecedented right with an outside oracle and a lot of attempts. If that is understanding, it's an understanding very different from ours, and for the person using it the practical consequence is the same on both sides of the fight: you check what it hands you. If your kid asks whether there's somebody in there, you can say that not even the biggest experts agree, and that neither answer excuses the checking.

## The Parrot Got Hands

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
Now we can settle the bill @sec-ch03 left open. If base intelligence stalled, the change I felt came from the [harness]{.gls}, the program around the model that gives it hands.

A lone model has to carry the world in its weights, because it gets only one shot at being right. A model with a harness fetches the documentation when it needs to and tests the hypothesis before delivering. Low-priority knowledge leaves the weights and becomes a lookup, and the loop brings new information without costing training. That's why an agent can run a smaller model and still get more done.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
The GPT-4o test from @sec-ch03 was the cleanest proof of this: in the same harness as today's models, it planned and stopped, and when it did engage, it could even program. What separated the generations was the ability to use its hands for more than an hour without supervision, and that's exactly what METR's metric measures.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
In a good session, the cycle of guess, test and correction you saw in @sec-ch03 repeats dozens of times without whoever made the request noticing.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
My bet, in October 2026, was that not even new library versions confused agents that much anymore: the model doesn't need to know the new thing by heart; it looks it up, tests and adjusts. And I think that's why none of the seven languages in my test got in the way of the frontier models: the loop equalizes what training left uneven.

I measured the caveat too. With Bend 2, a language too new to have published material, the loop only moved where there was something to check, the proof checker; outside it, with no documentation to fetch, the result stayed toy-grade (@sec-ch18).

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
The same logic holds outside programming. Much of what companies throw at a giant LLM is disguised classification: labeling a ticket, deciding which queue it goes to, applying a rule. For that there are small, specialized models that do the job for cents and without inventing formats (@sec-ch13). Competence comes from the fit between the tool and the problem, far more than from the size of the model.

### Faster, Same Work

<!-- src: 2026/09/23/llm-benchmark-v4-opus-5-5-gpt-6-sol-luna-mimo-2-6-grok-4-7 -->
What changed from one version to the next was speed and price. In September, Claude Opus 5.5 got the top score on my benchmark in a little over an hour, for about $17, where Opus 5 had taken more than two hours and cost about $70.

<!-- src: original -->
Doing the same thing got faster and cheaper, and the process stayed the same: someone has to know what to ask for, look at what came back and send it back to be redone.

## Competent Enough

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
I don't expect another reasoning revolution of that order of magnitude in the [transformer]{.gls} architecture. What's coming is incremental improvement in agents: more reliable tools, better memory, longer sessions, less invention thanks to verification cycles. And harness optimizations will also hit diminishing returns, like every [S-curve]{.gls key=curva-em-s}.

The point of this chapter, and of the book, is that this is already enough.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
What the agent era delivered was the automation of software engineering done right: long, reliable sessions, with testing and feedback, that accelerated hundreds, maybe thousands, of open source projects. The premise of what it costs to put a change live changed along with it (@sec-ch19). It's a new compiler for an old discipline. Whoever had process, tests and documented rules reaped the gains; whoever had only hype is still waiting for the next model to solve it.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
What doesn't exist is magic for amateurs. Asking an agent for a coding task without knowing what you're doing gets you back [slop]{.gls}, the careless volume from @sec-ch10. The agent responds better to those who know what to ask for: people who understand this architecture, know its weaknesses and know how to make the most of what it does well.

<!-- src: original -->
In the middle of the marathon, I wrote that AI is a mirror of whoever uses it (@sec-ch06) and, weeks later, that it's the mirror of your own competence (@sec-ch07). Months of measuring didn't touch that sentence. It only explained why it's true: if the model is a frozen file and all the good models arrived at the same plateau, the difference between an excellent result and a pile of garbage falls entirely on our side.

In March, to people waiting for AI to "get better" before using it for real, I answered that it was already good enough. Back then it was the impression of someone programming with it every day. In October, it became more than an impression: the accounts from the people who train, the outside yardsticks and my programming tests point to the same place, even if none of them proves the ceiling alone. The year's new models brought little at the base, and I wouldn't hang any plan on waiting for the next one.

> A lone LLM is an expensive parrot. With an agent and someone disciplined at the controls, it's competent enough to do real work. The rest is marketing waiting for the next magic parameter count that nobody publishes anymore.

### What Would Change My Conclusion

<!-- src: original -->
This is the October 2026 reading, made with the numbers I'd gathered since February. The model names in this chapter will age fast, and by the time you read this there will be others. That's why it's worth saying what would make me change my mind, so you can check for yourself.

Three things, in order from what I think most likely to least likely:

- a new model breaking away from the pack in independent measurements, by people who don't sell the model, by a margin that doesn't fit inside the run-to-run noise (@sec-ch11);
- models that actually learn while in use, without waiting for the next training round, which would knock down the section about the locked file;
- an architectural discovery that replaces the transformer (in @sec-ch01 I said nobody knows what it will be).

Notice that no item on the list depends on a company's announcement (@sec-ch14). All of them can be measured from the outside.

## What's Yours

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
If the model learns nothing from your marathon, a practical question is left: where does what was learned live? It stays with you, in the context you assemble, in your notes, in the project's rules file (@sec-ch09), in your tests. And it evaporates if you don't keep it.

<!-- src: 2026/07/20/novidades-no-meu-ai-memory-cada-vez-melhor-pra-usar-com-suas-ias -->
Code shows only what survived. The history says we swapped one database for another, but it doesn't keep the experiment that went wrong, the bug that only showed up now and then, the reason we changed our minds after three hours. Much of that exists only in the agent session, and an agent session, at the end of the day, is text.

Anthropic can change limits, pricing or models tomorrow, and so can OpenAI. An agent can be excellent this week and unbearable the next. I want to be able to swap the engine without throwing away the whole trip.

<!-- src: 2026/06/14/ai-memory-arquitetura-emergente-e-software-maleavel -->
That's where ai-memory came from, the memory system for agents I promised to tell you about in @sec-ch02 [@aimemory2026site]. The first commit was on May 21, 2026: a version for one user, one machine, one operating system. Nobody designed the whole architecture on day one. It emerged with use, like a clay sculpture that takes shape as the hands work, and only after a lot of people were using it did I consolidate the rules.

<!-- src: 2026/09/02/ai-memory-2-0-melhor-sistema-memoria-agentes-e-times -->
In September, version 2.0 came out, with some seventy people having had a pull request accepted in the project. Memory became plain Markdown, in an open format other tools can read, stored on a server that belongs to whoever uses it. Several agents from different companies feed the same memory, and an entire team can share the same project.

<!-- src: ref:ai-memory/README.md -->
On the day I closed this chapter, October 13, 2026, it was at version 2.6.6 [@akita2026aimemory]. Its promise fits on one line: drop Claude Code in the middle of a task, open Codex in the same folder and carry on without explaining everything again. What it does, when it's worth it and how to get started are in @sec-apmemoria.

<!-- src: original -->
ai-memory is my own project, so apply to it the question from @sec-ch14 that I tell you to apply to any announcement: who gains if you believe it? I do, if only in reputation. Interest doesn't make the thing false, it just asks you to check, and this section's principle stands without my program: your memory in your files, in a format any tool can read.

<!-- src: 2026/07/20/novidades-no-meu-ai-memory-cada-vez-melhor-pra-usar-com-suas-ias -->
You don't need my program. A well-kept folder of text files, like the one in @sec-ch02, already does the essentials. What matters is the principle:

> The model and the subscription I rent from whoever's best that month. The project's memory, the process and what I've learned stay with me.

## The Flat Line

<!-- src: original -->
Back to the October table, with the year's numbers spread out. The base flattening, which for an AI salesman is a marketing problem, was good news for me. I imagine it is for you too.

Remember the S-curve from @sec-ch01? Now it has a few numbers.

If you came to this book afraid the machine would wake up next year smarter than you, it's worth separating what I measured from what I think. My programming table tied at the ceiling, and a ceiling doesn't show what exists above it. What backs my reading is the accounts of the people who train the models, the yardsticks that still separate one model from another and a year of daily use, and none of them shows a sign of a leap in base intelligence. My bet is that the next model will make a few fewer mistakes and cost a bit less, without turning into something else; the list of what would change my mind is earlier in this chapter.

If your fear is a different one, that the machine will make the tasks of your job cheap, the curve that matters is METR's, and it's rising. For that fear, the planning premise is at the start of the chapter: tasks shrink before jobs do, and the part that decides and checks grows. It's the part all of Part V defends, and nobody has a timeline for it.

<!-- src: original -->
So what you learned in these pages stands. How the text comes out of the model, why it agrees with you, why it makes things up, why it needs someone checking, why it doesn't remember you: none of that changes when the number in the product's name does.

And it means the part that's still growing is yours. In my reading, the model I used in February and the one in October were almost the same intelligence, with better hands. The one who reached October different was me: with more tests, more notes, more judgment to tell when the agent had started to spin.

<!-- src: original -->
I spent February through October putting this thing to the test, and the verdict is less dramatic than any headline. It's an enormous tool, expensive, full of limits, and competent enough to change the work of people who know what they're doing.

What's missing now is simpler, and more personal. If you ran into me after reading all this and asked what to do on Monday, I'd have a few things to say.
