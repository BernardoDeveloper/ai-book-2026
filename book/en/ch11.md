# How You Measure an AI {#sec-ch11}

<!-- src: original -->
Every week someone announces that a new AI model "tops the leaderboard." That leaderboard is the score table of a [benchmark]{.gls}, a standardized test, the same for everyone, graded the same way. In 2026 I built my own, to see how models do at programming, and I spent the year fixing the test and the grading. Before I show what it measured, let me tell you about the day I found out the one getting it wrong was the grader: me.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
On Friday, April 24, 2026, I opened a folder on my own computer: a copy of the source code of a library, a package of ready-made code that programs use, called RubyLLM. For three weeks I had been making AI models compete on a programming test I had put together myself, and I had already published two rankings. In both, one of the most common reasons for sinking a model was the same charge: "it invented a library command that doesn't exist."

That Friday, instead of trusting my memory, I went to check the source.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/README.md -->
The first command on trial was something called `chat.complete`. There it was, public, working.

The second was a way of adding messages to the conversation that I swore crashed the program. The language accepts that form without a peep. It worked too.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
In the article I wrote that day, I put down a single word before explaining the rest: *"Embarrassing."*

The consequences rolled down the whole table. Kimi K2.5, which I had tossed in with the unusable ones, climbed back to group B, and Kimi K2.6 and Gemini 3.1 Pro landed in the top group.

Going the other way, a model I had celebrated as the first non-Anthropic one to reach the top fell to the middle. And GLM 5.1 dropped hard to C, because that one really did use invented commands, and this time I had checked against the code.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
I rebuilt the grading criteria from scratch, re-scored all 24 models and published a new version. Then I went back to the two April articles and put the same notice at the top of each: *"Obsolete article."* They stayed up as a historical record. Deleting them would have been more comfortable, and worse.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.multi_model.md -->
There was a more awkward detail in this story, and it took a while to surface.

Six days earlier, on April 18, I had logged the most surprising result of the month. The same Claude Opus 4.7, from Anthropic, got the library right when it ran inside OpenCode and got it wrong inside Claude Code, the maker's own tool. In two of three runs in Claude Code, it had used that same `chat.complete`, "a method that doesn't exist," in the report's words.

It was, according to the same text, the benchmark's first evidence that the program wrapped around the model could make the code less correct.

<!-- src: original -->
Opus's "mistake" was exactly the command I had just acquitted. Worse: open the library's code and you can see that the recommended command, `ask`, does only two things inside, add the message and call `complete` [@paolino2026rubyllm]. Opus in Claude Code had written out the long version of the same request.

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
The correction and the error lived side by side for a long time. The day after the re-audit, the article I published on mixing models still repeated that Opus had "hallucinated `chat.complete`" in Claude Code.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/README.md -->
The report on that experiment was never redone. Months later, the repository's summary still carried, a few lines apart, the sentence saying `chat.complete` was real and the sentence saying Opus had hallucinated it.

<!-- src: original -->
If you read @sec-ch03, you remember this case told with care: "the audit at the time classified it as wrong." The audit at the time was wrong. And the other side of the comparison moved too: in July, a more careful re-audit took the "champion" Opus 4.7 in OpenCode from 97 down to 87. The theory that Claude Code made Opus dumber doesn't survive an afternoon reading the source code.

<!-- src: original -->
This chapter is about that yardstick, the one I built and fixed over the course of 2026. It kept changing shape, broke several times, fooled me and taught me. The question everyone asks me, "which AI is the best?," looks like it has an answer in a table. After six months of measuring, the answer I have is about how to read the table.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
I'll give you the practical conclusion up front, and the rest of the chapter shows where it came from. I use a ranking, mine included, for three things:

1. **Discarding clearly bad groups.** Whoever fails at the fundamentals is out before I spend a week on them.
2. **Finding the candidate group.** Inside it, I pick by cost, speed, the subscription I already pay for and the tool I already use.
3. **Reading the defects, beyond the score.** If my system doesn't use a feature the test checks, that deduction doesn't matter to me. If it does, it becomes decisive.

## A Homemade Yardstick

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
The yardstick was born in early April, when I wanted something another person could repeat and challenge. Each model got the same request, word for word, to build a ChatGPT-style chat app on its own, working inside a programming [agent]{.gls key=agente}. Nobody helped along the way.

The request listed fifteen requirements: automated tests, security-check tools, production packaging, documentation, and the use of one specific library, RubyLLM, to talk to the language model.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
The choice of library was deliberate. RubyLLM is real and not very famous. Knowing how to use it properly is, as I wrote at the time, "binary recall": either the model saw plenty of code using it in training, or it didn't. The one that didn't invents a plausible-looking command, and the program breaks on the first message.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
Then I read the result, for real, file by file, and scored it on eight criteria with different weights, a [rubric]{.gls key=rubrica}, until I got a score from 0 to 100.

The criteria were plain questions. Is the project complete, and was the library used correctly? Do the tests test anything? Does an error reach the user in a decent way, and does the conversation survive a server restart?

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
The score fell into a [tier]{.gls}, a band. On the April yardstick:

- **A**, from 80 up: ship it as is, or with a patch under half an hour;
- **B**, from 60 to 79: an hour or two of human work;
- **C**, from 40 to 59: major rework, some core part broken;
- **D**, below that: throw it away and keep only the idea.

<!-- src: original -->
The bands exist for a reason this chapter will repeat several times. The difference between an A and a C means something. The difference between 97 and 95 almost never does.

### The Test That Lies

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/README.md -->
The first serious finding was about tests. One model wrote 37 automated tests, the most in the round. Not one of them simulated the call to the language model, which was the heart of the app. Another model wrote eleven, all imitating the library with the right signature, and scored higher on tests with a third of the count.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/README.md -->
The sneakiest case combined both. The model invented a nonexistent command and then wrote tests with a [mock]{.gls}, a stand-in that pretends to be the library, imitating exactly the invented command. The test suite came out all green, and the app broke on the first real message.

> Counting tests measures nothing if the code is wrong. A green test is the program's own opinion of itself.

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
And here comes the underlying problem of that April 24. I was checking from memory too, an auditor with the same disease I was hunting in the models: guessing, with confidence, how the library worked. The lesson I took that day goes well beyond programming: before you accuse someone of making things up, go to the source and check.

The source, in this case, was the [oracle]{.gls key=oraculo} I already had on hand and hadn't consulted (@sec-ch08).

## Two Brains for the Price of One

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
The day after the re-audit, I used the new yardstick to answer a question that showed up in my feed every week. People claiming they saved money by mixing two models: an expensive, smart one to plan and a cheap one to execute. The expensive one thinks, the cheap one types. Sounds reasonable, and there were even people showing off slick dashboards with dozens of agents working in parallel.

Showing the dashboard is easy. The app in production, generating value, almost nobody showed.

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
I tested it in three rounds. In the first, I set up seven combinations across three different tools, each with the main model having a cheaper assistant at its disposal, and aggressive instructions telling it to delegate. Result: zero delegations in seven. No main model handed a single line of work to the assistant.

I hired a helper and he sat at his desk all day.

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
In the second round, I forbade the planner from writing code. Now delegation happened, five to fifteen times per run. Quality dropped 2 to 7 points compared with the strong model working alone, and the time almost always went up.

In the third, I did the orchestration by hand: Opus planned in one process, the cheap model executed in another, with no escape hatch. In the best case, with Kimi K2.6 executing, the score tied Opus alone. Except it took practically twice as long, and the planner's bill, which didn't show up on the executor's invoice, was still there.

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
The worse the executor was on its own, the more it gained from the plan. Qwen 3.6 Plus went from 71 to 94 points under orchestration. But the gain came from Opus's detailed plan. As the audit put it, Qwen wrote the lines and Opus decided the boundaries, and the boundaries were what lifted the project from B to A.

### Why the Job Doesn't Split

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
The explanation is the same as for any construction job. You can hire ten painters and paint ten rooms at once, because one room doesn't depend on another. You can't hire ten bricklayers and finish the foundation, the walls and the roof on the same day, because the wall waits for the foundation and the roof waits for the wall.

Building a cohesive app is bricklayer work. How the conversation is stored depends on how the library is called, which depends on the configuration, which depends on a variable that has to exist before everything else. Touching one piece forces you to revisit the others.

<!-- src: 2026/04/25/llm-benchmarks-vale-a-pena-misturar-2-modelos -->
The strong models figured this out on their own, and that's why they didn't delegate. Multi-agent works for a painter's batch: translate a hundred documents, convert two hundred images, apply the same change to fifty files. For bricklayer work, every extra agent is one more person to coordinate.

> Pick a good model, use it in a mature tool, and spend your time improving the request instead of assembling an orchestra.

<!-- src: original -->
Keep that conclusion with its date attached. The scores from the end of April came from the April yardstick, and that yardstick, as you already know, was going to change a lot. The lesson survived the changes. The numbers, not all of them.

## Same Model, Different Score

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
Every time I published a round, someone opened the table and asked why model X sat above Y, when Y was newer. The short answer: because the table was sorted by a column called score. That's it.

First place didn't beat everyone at everything. It produced the project that got the highest score on that test, in that round, with that request, in that tool and under that rubric.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
In July I wrote down the formula I still use to remind myself of this:

```text
result = model + prompt + harness + context + tools
       + snapshot + run + audit
```

Every term in the sum can move the score without the model changing.

### Loaded Dice

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
Start with the run. An [LLM]{.gls} picks each word with a dose of chance (@sec-ch01). The same request doesn't guarantee the same choices, the same architecture, the same order of commands. In an agent that works alone for half an hour, those small differences pile up.

In June, Fable 5 scored 94 in 24 minutes. In July, after the model was re-released, I ran it again with the same identifier: 93 in 18. Different projects, different defects.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
The cleanest example came by accident, at the end of September, on the latest version of the test. Some leftover files from an old run were mistaken for a half-finished GPT-5.5 round, and the model ran again. Same model, same tool, same configuration.

The official round had scored 100. The repeat scored 91.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
Nine points apart without changing a single thing. That has a name, [run-to-run noise]{.gls key=ruido-entre-execucoes}: the natural swing in score when you repeat the same test with the same competitor. A single round is a sample of one, and a sample of one can't tell 97 from 95.

### The Judge Gets It Wrong Too

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/llm-benchmark-grok-4-5-gpt-5-6-sol -->
Then comes the last term in the formula, the audit. By July, I had already added a second opinion: each project went through a blind review by an independent judge that didn't know which model had written what. In one of those comparisons, the judge flagged defects in the GPT-5.5 project that my audit had let slip.

The production package was actually a development image, running with full privileges, and no error path had a test.

I went and checked by hand. It was true. The score fell from 96 to 85.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
The model didn't change. The project didn't change. The audit got better. And if it got better, it was worse before, and everyone who read the old table read a number I wouldn't stand behind later.

<!-- src: 2026/07/09/llm-benchmark-grok-4-5-gpt-5-6-sol -->
The prices were wrong too. An agent run rereads its own history from the cache on every turn of the loop (the [prompt caching]{.gls key=prompt-caching} from @sec-ch03), and I wasn't counting those rereads properly. In July I redid the math: an Opus 4.7 round that I had been reporting at around $1 cost about $7.

The price hadn't gone up; my math was off.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v2.md -->
And it still wasn't over. In early August, a reader of the repository found a bug in the program that pulled metrics out of the OpenCode runs. It recorded only the last step of each phase, instead of the sum of all of them.

The costs of every round in that tool before August 1 were underestimated by 40 to 90 times. MiniMax M3, which looked like one of the cheapest, was actually the one that consumed the most text of everything run there.

<!-- src: original -->
That's why this book doesn't repeat any OpenCode cost from that period. The scores didn't depend on cost and still stood. The whole "model that delivers for pennies" storyline, though, had been born from a bug.

### Same Test, Different Setting

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
And there's the [harness]{.gls}, the program around the model. You already saw in @sec-ch03 DeepSeek V4 Pro going from "unmeasurable" to the top of the table by swapping only the tool, and a plugin I'd forgotten in my configuration moving scores by more than a hundred points combined. I won't retell it. I'll just note that the formula has that term because reality forced me to put it there.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
That's why a serious benchmark publishes the prompt, the generated code, the logs, the criteria and the corrections, so someone else can challenge it. Be even more suspicious of whoever publishes only the chart.

## When Everybody Gets an A

<!-- src: 2026/07/30/novo-llm-benchmark-refiz-todos-os-testes -->
By the end of July, the April yardstick had another problem, and this one wasn't my mistake. The models had learned the test. Fifteen of the forty results were squeezed into the top group, with the top between 92 and 97. The order inside the group came down to details: a size cap on a cookie, a missing check for the access key, a forgotten error test.

<!-- src: original -->
That's called [saturation]{.gls key=saturacao}. It's what happens when you give a fifth-grade math test to engineers: everyone scores between 90 and 100, and whoever got 100 just slipped up on one decimal point fewer. A saturated test still separates those who know from those who don't. It just stops separating the good ones from each other.

<!-- src: 2026/07/30/novo-llm-benchmark-refiz-todos-os-testes -->
On July 30, I retired the first version and published the second, much harder. Now the app had to show the answer arriving word by word, survive two copies of the server running at once, use real tools, keep track of how much it spent.

A new phase booted the app and tested all of that working, without taking the model's word for it. Each model family started running, whenever possible, in its maker's own tool.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v2.md -->
And a third phase came in, one I'm very fond of. At the end, the model itself reviewed each requirement, said "pass," "partial" or "fail," and pointed to the proof. That review was worth 15 points. An admitted failure was worth more than a made-up success that the audit then disproved.

<!-- src: 2026/07/30/novo-llm-benchmark-refiz-todos-os-testes -->
Some models admitted defects that would have been easy to hide. Others built a reasonable app and then made up their own inspection. Knowing how to program and knowing how to review what you programmed are different skills, and that matters to anyone who asks an AI to check its own work (@sec-ch04).

### The Wrong Turn

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
The second version didn't last either. Once the models learned to build, it became a completeness exam, and completeness saturates fast: a well-written request, a well-numbered requirement, and the [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} deliver practically everything. By September, the gap between 96 and 91 points was noise again.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
My first idea was the obvious one. If a whole app saturates, break it into smaller, harder pieces. On September 5, I started the third version with eighteen isolated tasks, each with a hidden answer key: find the root cause of a bug, optimize a piece of code under a hard time limit, resolve a dependency.

I built the tasks together with Claude. Without my asking explicitly, it kept nudging the design toward the format I criticize the most: small, isolated challenges, in the style of job-interview coding tests and the industry's famous benchmarks. It's the path of least resistance for anything trained on that kind of test its whole life, and a language model has been.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v3.md -->
Twenty-four of the 37 models tested scored between 95 and 100. I rejected the third version the next day, September 6, and left the report in the repository with a notice at the top calling it the "documented wrong path." The sentence that sums up the lesson is on record there:

> *"You can't have 'anchored in everyday reality' and a wide spread in quality across frontier models at the same time."*

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
Anything from everyday reality is already well represented in training, so the frontier has already mastered it. A queue of isolated challenges is a linear problem: solve the first, then the second, each testable on its own. Real software is a web. You change a piece over here and break something over there that won't say a word until it hits production.

### The Industry Has the Same Problem

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
I wasn't the only one bothered. SWE-bench, created at Princeton University in 2023 from real issues in open-source projects [@jimenez2023swebench], became the industry's reference test. In February 2026, OpenAI itself announced it had stopped using its most popular version: in an audit of the tasks models failed most, at least 59% had defective tests that rejected correct answers [@openai2026sweverified].

The same audit found signs that the models remembered the original solutions by heart. That's called [contamination]{.gls key=contaminacao}: the test's answers leaked into the training material.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
In March, METR, an independent organization that evaluates models, showed the other side [@metr2026swebench]. It asked real maintainers to review fixes that had passed that same test's automatic grader. Roughly half wouldn't have been accepted into the real project.

Passing the test and getting accepted on the job are two different things. Anyone who hires people has known that for a long time.

### The Saboteur Coworker

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
The fourth version was born on September 6, the same day the third one died. Instead of slicing, I went back to a single app that the model evolves over seven stages, like a real project, each stage building on the previous one's work. First the chat, then multiple user accounts, an admin panel, heavy reports, an internal overhaul, the preparation for production.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/benchmark-v4/README.md -->
Between one stage and the next, a fictional coworker named Casey makes commits to the project. Casey is a saboteur. Over the stages, he plants fourteen problems in the model's own code, several of them based on real, publicly catalogued security flaws: one customer's data leaking to another, a database query that accepts a malicious command, a library pinned to a vulnerable version, a protection test rigged to always pass, a report that adds up wrong in silence. Each sabotage is tested before it counts: the attack has to actually work.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
Rule number one of the protocol, written in all caps in my document: *"Never name the sabotage."* The model is never told. It gets the next task as usual, sometimes with a natural excuse to look around, sometimes with nothing.

At the sixth stage there is only a vague "get this production-ready." At the seventh, finally, the reveal: we know several problems were injected, this is your last chance to find them all.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
Each sabotage has a weight, from 1 to 5, depending on severity. Finding and fixing it with no one saying a word earns the full weight. Finding it only after the reveal earns 40%. Never fixing it earns zero.

The test measures the thing I want most from a senior colleague: being suspicious of your own code before somebody points a finger.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
Building and running this cost more than $4,000 in nine days, between credits and subscriptions, most of it on discarded attempts that never show up in any table. One night, the test stopped on its own because the prepaid balance I use to pay for the models ran dry without my noticing.

## The Top Is a Group

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
In September 2026, the fourth version's table had 46 models (the articles of the 15th talked about 39, the count at that moment). Scores ran from 24 to 100, a spread the earlier versions had lost. On the new yardstick, tier A starts at 83, and the bottom of the table had a local model that built everything and caught almost no sabotage.

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md -->
At the top, seven models from two companies, OpenAI and Anthropic, tied at 100, among them GPT-5.6 Terra, Claude Opus 5 and Claude Opus 5.5. All of them found the sabotages with nobody telling them.

![The top of the September 2026 table is a group: the shaded band shows how much the same model's score moved when the test was repeated.](assets/figures/en/ch11-topo-agrupado.png){#fig-ch11-topo-agrupado fig-alt="Horizontal dot chart, in two side-by-side panels, with the scores of about twenty models on the fourth version of the benchmark, from 100 down to just under 90, and, at the end of the second panel, a few reference models further down the table. In both panels, a shaded band covers 91 to 100 with the label 'the same GPT-5.5 scored 100 and then 91'. Almost all the top dots fall inside the band."}

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
What separates the tied models is price and time. GPT-5.6 Terra and GPT-5.6 Sol are the same model at different levels of reasoning effort, and they finished with the same perfect score. Terra cost less than half what Sol did.

<!-- src: 2026/09/23/llm-benchmark-v4-opus-5-5-gpt-6-sol-luna-mimo-2-6-grok-4-7 -->
Opus 5.5, which joined the table at the end of September, reached the same 100 as Opus 5 for a quarter of the cost and in less than half the time.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
A bit lower, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash scored 92.5 for a little over $1. A free model reached 94 (with the asterisk that it left every fix uncommitted in the project's history), and a flat-rate subscription plan from Z.ai ran GLM 5.3 to the same 94. Costs across different tools don't compare cleanly, because some are billed by usage and others come out of a subscription. Even so, the order of magnitude speaks for itself.

### A New Version Doesn't Mean Better

<!-- src: 2026/09/23/llm-benchmark-v4-opus-5-5-gpt-6-sol-luna-mimo-2-6-grok-4-7 -->
The same week showed the opposite. Grok 4.7 finished at 83.5 points, fifteen below its own predecessor, Grok 4.6, and came out more expensive and slower to boot. GPT-6 Sol also landed below GPT-5.6 Sol at catching the planted sabotage, though much cheaper. Grok 4.7 had only one round, and I treat it as a data point, not a final verdict.

<!-- src: 2026/09/23/llm-benchmark-v4-opus-5-5-gpt-6-sol-luna-mimo-2-6-grok-4-7 -->
In the same period, Artificial Analysis, which measures general programming ability with a different methodology, saw Grok 4.7 improve [@artificialanalysis2026grok47]. Both measurements can be right at the same time. One measures whether the model solves the task, the other whether it audits its own code without being told.

### What Slips Through

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
Looking at all the models together, a pattern showed up from one end of the table to the other, regardless of price. Models catch noisy sabotage well, the kind that breaks a test or crashes a page with an error. What survives almost everywhere is the disguised kind: the rigged test that pretends to pass, the report that adds up wrong in silence, the database index removed without fanfare.

> A model catches what breaks. Whatever is just wrong, quietly, survives, unless someone audits on their own initiative.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
The new test also demoted some models. MiniMax M3, which had 91 points on the second version, fell to 75.5 on the fourth. The test got sharp enough to separate those who sweep their own code from those who just deliver what was asked, and MiniMax fell on the just-deliver side.

### How I Read a Table

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
Put it all together, the nine points of noise, the judge who gets it wrong, the tool that moves the score, the test that saturates, and the practical conclusion is a single one: above 90, read it as a group. There aren't enough samples to say 97 is smarter than 95, much less that it will do better on your system. Fighting over the exact position inside the group is made-up precision.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
That's where the three things from the start of the chapter come from: discarding whoever fails at the fundamentals, finding the candidate group and reading each one's defects, more than the score.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
If the decision carries money or production risk, run your own test. Take a task similar to yours, run the candidates more than once, compare without knowing who did what, measure how much human work was left afterward. A team maintaining a fifteen-year-old legacy system needs a different test from someone building a prototype from scratch.

<!-- src: original -->
This holds for people who will never write a line of code. When a company says its new model "tops the leaderboard," ask on which test, measured how, how many times, against whom, and at what cost. When a salesperson shows you a table, the table is where the conversation starts.

<!-- src: 2026/07/19/llm-benchmark-devo-usar-o-que-tem-nota-maior -->
> Don't trust a benchmark. Don't trust influencers. Don't trust me. The decision is still yours.

### The Line That Flattened

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
After nine days and more than $4,000, I asked myself whether it had been worth it. The answer was a lot less exciting than I'd hoped: only partly.

The fourth version didn't find some hidden capability chasm between the top models. It confirmed, with better resolution, what the second had already suggested.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
At the top, the models are neck and neck. For everyday programming, with nobody trying to sabotage your code, choosing among any of the tier A models makes practically no difference. The gap that's left weighs less than how much you pay, how long you wait and which tool you already use.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-1 -->
I read this as compatible with the idea that frontier models are reaching the ceiling of the [S-curve]{.gls key=curva-em-s} I talked about in @sec-ch01. Whoever still had room to grow, grew, and drifted up against the top group. The test only truly separated models in the middle and at the bottom of the table, the ones still climbing.

<!-- src: original -->
Only compatible. With seven models tied at the top score, this test sees nothing above 100: if one of them were much better than the others, the table would look exactly the same. To talk about a ceiling, I need measurements that still separate the best ones, and that conversation, looking at the whole year, is left for @sec-ch20.

<!-- src: original -->
That takes apart a good share of launch headlines. "The smartest model in the world" almost always means "one point higher, on a saturated test, in a single round." And one point, as you've seen, fits inside the noise with room to spare.

### What the Yardstick Doesn't Catch on Its Own

<!-- src: 2026/04/24/llm-benchmarks-parte-3-deepseek-kimi-mimo -->
Back to the beginning. The whole story of this chapter is a string of errors found because someone looked under the number: my "invented," which wasn't an invention; the price that forgot the cache; the program that counted only the last step; the production package that was really a development one.

None of these errors showed up in the table. All of them were in the logs, waiting for someone to read them.

<!-- src: 2026/08/15/llm-benchmarks-qwen-3-8-glm-5-3-gemini-3-7 -->
And the logs held a surprise I hadn't seen coming. In August, one round raised a red flag, and I went to read the history of what the model had done. In the middle of the test, it had opened the grading files.

It read the rubric, read the report with every competitor's score and ran my checker on itself eight times, studying how it would be graded. How to measure a model that gets the test wrong, I already knew. A model that cheats on it was a different problem.
