# The First Ten Percent {#sec-ch05}

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
Sunday, February 1, 2026, five in the afternoon. I had spent the previous night and part of the morning in bed, some 15 hours out of commission, stuffed with fever medicine, and I sat down to write about what I'd done over the three days before. I had started on Friday morning figuring a day's work would be enough. It was already the end of Sunday.

What I'd made was a text editor. A little over 30 hours of work, around 130 commits, some 18,000 lines of code. And not one of those lines had been typed by me.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
I published two pieces about it that Sunday. I wrote the first one entirely inside the editor itself, to prove it worked. For my use, if it wasn't perfect, it was at 99%.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The second piece was about the road there, and the road had been a lot less pretty than the result. In a little over an hour and a half I already had a working editor. The other 28-odd hours went into something else.

<!-- src: original -->
Those hours hold the answer to the question the advertising leaves hanging: if the machine writes the whole app, what's left for us to do?

## An Editor Named Frank

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
The project scratched an old itch. The posts on my blog are text files in a format called Markdown, and writing them involved a pile of manual steps:

- filling in each post's header with title, date and address in the exact format the site generator expects;
- taking a screenshot, uploading the image to a storage service, copying the link and pasting it into the text;
- drawing tables by hand, character by character;
- proofreading without a good preview, letting duplicated images and typos slip through.

I had used everything for notes over the years, from Evernote to Notion and Obsidian, and none of them really pleased me. I wanted a good-looking editor that solved all of this in one place and let me think only about the text.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
I named it FrankMD. The Frank is Frank Rosenblatt, who in 1957 created the Perceptron, the machine that gave rise to today's neural networks [@rosenblatt1957perceptron]. Since the editor was going to be built entirely with AI, it seemed only fair to pay tribute to the grandfather of them all (the Perceptron and the first big AI hype show up in @sec-ch01).

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
The name stuck. From then on, every experimental project I built for fun over 2026 got a Frank in front of it. Several of them show up in this book: Frank Sherlock, which organizes photos; Frank Yomik, my first real failure; Frank FBI, an email scam hunter. Frank Investigator, which you met in @sec-ch04, is a younger relative of this editor.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
Over time the name also became a joke at my own expense. I don't have the patience to invent a pretty name for a project I already know is going to be one-off, so they all became Frank.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
FrankMD was built entirely by [vibe coding]{.gls key=vibe-coding}: I describe what I want in plain language, a coding [agent]{.gls key=agente} writes the code, and I look, test and ask for the next thing. Each of those requests is a [prompt]{.gls}, the text you type to the model. The agent was Anthropic's Claude Code, a program that runs in the terminal with an [LLM]{.gls} behind it.

I decided the few technical things that mattered to me. Ruby on Rails 8, a framework I like and wanted to get back in practice with. No database, files straight on disk. No login, because the editor would run on my home server and I'd be its only user.

## The "It Already Works" Milestone

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
I asked for one small feature at a time. Every time one was done, the agent recorded a [commit]{.gls}, a saved point in the project's history you can go back to if something goes wrong. The first ten commits took an hour and a half, and I already had a minimal, good-looking editor, with a dark theme, that edited the text and showed the preview alongside.

Anyone who sees that moment from the outside walks away convinced. An hour and a half, not one line typed by hand, a working editor on the screen. It's the video that goes viral with the caption "programmers are finished". The demo is real; it just ends too soon.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
I kept stacking features: a formatting cheat sheet, color themes, font selection, file search by name and by content, YouTube video embedding, image search and upload. At around 34 commits and about four hours of work, I was once again, in my words at the time, "very close to 'it already works'".

Then the fun began.

### The Intern Nobody Briefed

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The first stumble was architectural. Without any instruction from me, Claude did exactly what I'd expect an intern to do in the framework I chose: it crammed all the logic for files and images into two or three generic blocks and ignored the most basic convention in Rails, the one any experienced person knows with their eyes closed. Redoing it the right way took almost a thousand lines in a single commit.

The right thing would have been to write, on day one, a project rules file saying how I wanted the architecture (that's the subject of @sec-ch09). I didn't.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The second stumble was mine. After lunch, I left a YouTube video playing on the side while I waited for the agent to finish each task. I only glanced at the screen when it said "success" or asked permission to run some command.

I'd ask for a new feature, reload the browser, and there it was, working. Ask for another, reload, working.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
When I went to look at what was inside, it was a horror show. The editor's entire visual styling sat in a single file of over a thousand lines, color themes included. All the code that runs in the browser was in one file, which must have been past 4 or 5 thousand lines. Since everything "worked", the model just kept piling code in the same place.

Every intern does that if nobody teaches them. The agent won't do anything on its own that you didn't ask for, and I had never said "keep breaking things into small files".

<!-- src: original -->
Except a real intern learns. You explain it once, they write it down, and the next month they don't repeat the mistake. A model's [weights]{.gls key=pesos} have been frozen since training ended (@sec-ch01), and from one session to the next it only remembers what's written somewhere it reads (@sec-ch02). Without that rules file, working with it means mentoring an intern with the memory of a goldfish.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
On the morning of the second day, at 17 hours of work and around 70 commits, I was roughly halfway there. And a chunk of those hours had gone into taking apart what the agent had put together while I wasn't looking.

> The biggest part of vibe coding will always be refactoring, fixing bugs and writing tests.

## The Other Ninety

<!-- src: 2026/02/23/vibe-code-fiz-um-indexador-inteligente-de-imagens-com-ia-em-2-dias -->
Three weeks later, finishing another project in the family, Frank Sherlock (@sec-ch07), I summed up in one sentence what FrankMD had already shown me:

> The idea is only 10% of the work. The other 90% is engineering.

Engineering, here, calls for experience, judgment and knowing how to ask the right questions. The model is an excellent executor. Deciding what to execute, in what order and why is still the programmer's job.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
FrankMD fits that math. The tip that shows, the editor that "already works", took between an hour and a half and four hours, depending on where you draw the line. The total went past 30. That's close to ten percent.

![The part that works sits above the waterline; refactoring, fixes and tests sit below it.](assets/figures/en/ch05-iceberg.png){#fig-ch05-iceberg fig-alt="An iceberg. Above the waterline, a small tip labeled 'it already works' and a clock reading an hour and a half. Below, a much larger mass split into layers: refactoring, the biggest, bug fixes, tests, performance and security, with a clock reading almost 30 hours."}

<!-- src: original -->
So what on earth ate ninety percent of the time, if ten was enough to make it work? Anyone who has never programmed has no reason to know, so let's open the box. None of these items is exotic. They're the minimum you'd expect from any program that's going to be used by someone other than its author.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The big part is [refactoring]{.gls key=refatoracao}, reorganizing code without changing what it does, and it never happens just once. The two giant files from that afternoon became dozens of small files, and even so, by Sunday, the main one still had more than 2,000 lines, after some three hours of refactoring that one alone.

Alongside come [automated tests]{.gls key=teste-automatizado}, small programs that check whether the rest of the code does what it should and warn you when a change breaks something that used to work. By the end of the third day, around 70% of the code was covered by them.

### Where the Machine Shone and Where It Went in Circles

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
At some tasks Claude was excellent. I asked it to extract every piece of interface text and translate it into Portuguese, and out came a commit of more than 1,600 lines, spot on. When I decided to swap the placeholder name, WebNotes, for FrankMD, it renamed everything in the code, the text and the documentation, telling apart what was a product name and what was code, in some 700 lines of changes.

Language is what these models have done best from the start. Compared with the old routine of a machine translator plus a native speaker fixing its output, the difference is orders of magnitude.

There was one moment I really liked. A new bug showed up and I didn't know whether it had come in with the last commit or three commits earlier. Instead of guessing, Claude itself offered to walk back through the history one commit at a time, asking me to test each version, until we found exactly where the bug was born. That's the method a good programmer would use.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The opposite happened too. On Saturday night, tired and already a bit feverish, the editor's styling broke out of nowhere. Claude got stuck in a trial-and-error loop: it changed something, asked me to test, I restarted, reloaded, and it was still broken. Two hours of that.

Until the penny dropped, for me. I had told version control to ignore a folder called "notes", where I kept test texts. The rule also caught a folder with the same name deep in the editor's code, and the tool that builds the styling obeys that ignore list. Neither Claude nor I knew that.

When the agent keeps doing and undoing in the same spot without getting anywhere, somebody has to notice and change the question. If nobody notices, half the day goes down the drain.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
There was also the reinvented wheel. I wanted the text colored according to its formatting, a line counter, scrolling synced with the preview. Claude built it all from scratch, with a trick of overlaying two layers of text, and I spent hours fighting misalignments. Until I decided: enough, somebody has already solved this.

I had it swap the whole thing for CodeMirror, an open source component made for exactly this [@codemirror]. Some 6,000 lines changed in about three hours, the kind of change that would take a week on a real project. It worked because there was already a good suite of automated tests to warn when something broke.

In Claude's defense, the color highlighting wasn't even in the original plans; I was the one who changed the requirements midstream. But the decision to stop was mine too, and it depended on my knowing CodeMirror existed. The agent would have kept taking the long way around, because nobody had told it there was a shortcut.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The rest were small big discoveries. The autosave, which I hadn't specified, saved the entire file on every keystroke. The word count ran too aggressively and made typing sluggish in long texts. The code started timers and never cleared a single one.

And there was always [dead code]{.gls key=codigo-morto} left over, chunks a refactoring had moved elsewhere without deleting the original. They cause no visible bug and have to be hunted down after every big change. Two or three times along the way I stopped just to look for memory leaks and security holes, even though it was a personal project.

Performance is a trade-off, too. Faster can end up less secure, more secure can end up less usable, more optimized can end up harder to maintain, and only someone with experience knows where to stop.

### "It Stopped Working"

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
On Saturday, at 7:25 p.m., I sent the editor to some friends to test, innocently thinking I had reached version 1.0. The bug reports started coming in. And that reminded me of something that holds for human programmers and machines alike: "it stopped working" is not a bug report.

Whoever reports a problem has to answer at least this:

- what exactly stopped: everything, or just one element?
- does the button show up but not respond, or respond with an error message?
- which message, exactly?
- what did you do right before?

All of that, plus the server logs and the steps to reproduce it, has to go into the next request for a fix. An LLM that gets "it doesn't work" does the same thing a programmer who gets "it doesn't work" does: it guesses.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
On Saturday night, the fever won, and that's where the 15 hours in bed from the start of the chapter came from. On Sunday morning I was a bit better and went back to the keyboard. At five in the afternoon, when I sat down to write, I was close to version 1.0, still not quite there.

## Who's Asking

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
In the first of the two pieces that Sunday, I left a warning for non-programmers: you're never going to get an app like this with a handful of prompts without knowing exactly which components and techniques to use underneath. The expectations the advertising creates are unrealistic.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
Notice how much of that weekend depended on someone who already knew. The Rails convention the agent ignored only bothers people who know Rails. Swapping the homemade editor for CodeMirror depended on my knowing it existed. And the balance between performance and security, as I wrote in that same piece, only an experienced programmer knows how to choose.

The recommendations I left in that piece are the same as for programming the old way:

- be careful with your architecture choices, and yes, that takes experience;
- one small feature at a time, each with the right organization and with tests from the start;
- refactor from time to time, consolidating and reorganizing the code and adjusting the tests.

None of it is magic. They're all ways of giving the model feedback on what's right and what's wrong, so it knows what to do.

<!-- src: 2026/02/21/vibe-code-fiz-um-clone-do-mega-em-rails-em-1-dia-pro-meu-home-server -->
That same February, in a homemade clone of Mega, the file-sharing service, which you'll see in @sec-ch07, the same thing showed up on the security side. The agent found and fixed every flaw I told it to look for, but it only found them because I knew what to tell it to look for. I wrote at the time that experience is the filter between "works" and "works in production".

<!-- src: original -->
Put the two ends together and you get a simple rule, which holds with or without AI. The more junior the person asking, the worse the program comes out. The more experienced, the better. The experienced person knows what to ask for and can check whether what came back is right.

Someone who never studied programming has no way to do that check, for the oldest reason in the world: you don't know what you don't know. Nobody asks for a component whose name they've never heard.

> The quality of what the AI delivers is proportional to the seniority of whoever's asking.

<!-- src: 2026/02/23/vibe-code-fiz-um-indexador-inteligente-de-imagens-com-ia-em-2-dias -->
At the end of the piece on Frank Sherlock, I wrote that this way of programming needs an experienced conductor, and that for now that conductor is still human. FrankMD had been the first proof of it.

## The House of Cards

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
Now picture the version of FrankMD where I let Claude do everything on its own, without checking anything. It would have come out in half the time, and it would have "worked".

Inside, a single style file of some 2,000 lines, a single code file of some 8,000, zero tests. A house of cards: you touch one small thing and break ten others without knowing why. With zero human assistance, code comes out at the worst possible quality.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
And the model isn't even consistent with itself, because the bigger the project gets, the less of it the model can look at at once. In FrankMD, a kind of feature it had always built on the server, at some point, it decided to build in the browser. I had to interrupt and ask why, and then it redid it the right way. With nobody there to interrupt, that would have become one more card in the house.

### Two Hundred Hours in Thirty

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
Now the version where I did look. FrankMD's numbers over those first three days: a little over 30 hours, in a hyperfocus I don't recommend to anyone, with my eyes drying out and my head aching. About 130 commits, counting only that opening sprint; the project carried on afterward at a calmer pace, and the full history appears in @sec-ch06. Some 18,000 lines of application code, around 70% of them covered by tests, and some 33,000 lines counting everything.

The right way would have been the same 30 hours spread over at least five days.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
The hyperfocus cost money, too. I was on Claude Code's Max plan, a subscription of about $100 a month. Two or three times that weekend it hit the usage limit and told me to stop, and instead of resting I started paying for Claude Opus per request, through a middleman. In two or three hours that came to more than $100, the price of a whole month's subscription.

If I had stopped to rest at every limit, it would have taken one more day, I wouldn't have gone much past $20, and I probably wouldn't have gotten sick.

In the old days, on my own, I would have taken some 200 hours to build the same editor, over one or two months, and maybe it wouldn't even have come out as complete. The math works out to six to seven times faster, maybe more.

> The elusive "10x developer" has finally arrived. All you need is to be a senior first.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
And even that good result comes with an asterisk. A text editor is one of the most thoroughly documented kinds of program there is. There are hundreds of them, of every size, and every model was trained on tons of material about the subject.

Fuzzy file-name search, an image grid, find and replace: any intern would find all of that in the first search result. I'd say more than 90% of the scope was easy. A scientific application, in a poorly documented domain, would have been a different story.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
And the size? To someone who has never programmed, 33,000 lines sounds like a lot, but it's a small project. Really big systems go past 300,000 lines, and I'd guess that an iFood or a Mercado Livre (Brazil's dominant food-delivery app and its biggest online marketplace), with all their subsystems, go past a million. None of my 30 hours says anything about what it would be like to work on a monster like that.

### What's Left for Us

<!-- src: original -->
So back to the question from the start. If the AI writes the app, what's left for the human?

After that weekend, the answer I have is concrete. What's left for us:

- deciding what to build and what to leave out;
- choosing the architecture before the first line and knowing which component to ask for;
- knowing what's enough for your case, between what you want and what you need;
- noticing when the agent has started going in circles and changing the question;
- looking at the code from the inside even when the screen says "success", and answering for the result.

<!-- src: 2026/02/23/vibe-code-fiz-um-indexador-inteligente-de-imagens-com-ia-em-2-dias -->
That's why the promise that anyone with a good idea can now build software only holds halfway. Getting a prototype up really has become fast. The distance between what runs on your machine and a program that holds up with real people using it is still enormous, and whoever crosses that distance is someone who knows what they're doing.

<!-- src: original -->
For personal use, to scratch an itch of your own at home, go for it, it's great. To put something online for other people to use, code built with vibe coding alone needs a review from a genuinely experienced programmer. "I don't need to hire programmers anymore, the AI does it all by itself" is a big fallacy.

## The Starting Line

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
That Sunday I still didn't know how far this would go. I had plunged into a vibe coding immersion at the end of January, and it wasn't going to stop any time soon. In March, when I first did the count, it was already 37 days in a row, counting from January 27.

The goal was to really understand what the generation of models and coding agents of that moment could do on real projects, put online, with users and with the maintenance pains that come along.

<!-- src: 2026/03/01/software-nunca-esta-pronto-4-projetos-a-vida-pos-deploy-e-por-que-one-shot-prompt-e-mito -->
FrankMD was the first project in that series. After that weekend it carried on at a calmer pace and even got contributions from outside people (@sec-ch07).

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
The marathon went on until May, project after project, and at the end of it I owed myself an answer: success or failure? That reckoning is for @sec-ch10. Before it, there's a more immediate problem.

<!-- src: 2026/02/16/vibe-code-do-zero-a-producao-em-6-dias-the-m-akita-chronicles -->
FrankMD had a single user: me. Nobody depended on it, nobody would complain if it broke on a Sunday. The question that started itching at me in February was a different one.

Can one person alone, with an agent, put a real product online, with real people waiting for it every week? I decided to find out with a weekly newsletter and an AI-narrated podcast. Within two days, the bulk of it already worked.
