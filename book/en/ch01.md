# A Very Good Text Generator {#sec-ch01}

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In early February 2026, my name started circulating in video clips. They were snippets of a conversation I had recorded in June 2025 on Flow Podcast, one of the biggest talk shows in Brazil, in an episode called *Mentiram pra você sobre inteligência artificial* ("They lied to you about artificial intelligence") [@flow2025mentiram]. Hours of conversation became one-minute pieces, and the pieces became a line I never said: "Akita said AI will never replace any programmer."

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-part-1 -->
The timing was bad for that version. I was doing exactly the opposite of what the clips suggested: programming with AI every day and publishing every experiment on the blog. On the first day of February, I had written about how I built a text editor in three days without writing a single line of code (that marathon is Part II of this book).

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
So the heat came from both sides. One crowd said I was an enemy of AI who had been dead wrong. The other said I had sold out, that after years of criticizing the hype I had caved to it. Both crowds were answering an Akita who didn't exist.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
On February 8 I wrote a piece to set the record straight. What I had said on the podcast was something else: AI will never replace programmers like me. And I had said a second thing, which I still say: an artificial general intelligence won't come out of the current architecture without a new breakthrough, and nobody knows what that will be.

From those two ideas I had drawn, years before, the line a lot of people know me for. I illustrated the piece with an image of me holding a card with it, signed with my name. Anyone who looked at the card up close saw the letters scrambled in the middle of the sentence: *inversarenty proporonal ao sseu knoveemto*. The line, written properly, goes like this:

> Your hype about AI is inversely proportional to your knowledge of AI.

<!-- src: original -->
I was already saying this in January 2023, in a video on my channel, a little over a month after ChatGPT launched [@akita2023chatgpt; @openai2022chatgpt]. Back then I rounded out the idea with a comparison I still think is the best one: it's the difference between someone seeing a magic trick for the first time and someone who already knows how the trick works. The one who doesn't know is enchanted. The one who knows finds it useful, with caveats.

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
And I never stopped using AI. In September 2023, when I made a video programming in 16 languages in 16 days, I already had an AI helping me write code. Liking a tool and knowing its limits were never opposite positions.

<!-- src: original -->
This chapter is about how the trick works. If you came here afraid that there's someone on the other side of the screen, or that this someone is getting smarter every month, the best thing I can do is open the box. You don't need math, just patience for a few ideas. Anyone who wants to go one level down afterwards has @sec-apmecanica, optional and also formula-free, and the reading list in @sec-antes.

## Your Phone's Autocorrect, Only Huge

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
Around the same time as the clips, somebody dug up another line of mine: "AI is just a glorified text generator." The reaction was confusion. How can something be good if it's "just" a text generator?

You use a text generator every day. It's the autocorrect on your phone's keyboard, the one that suggests the next word while you type a message. Nobody says it has a personality or intelligence, and everybody agrees it's very useful. It slips up now and then, but most of the time it gets it right.

An [LLM]{.gls}, short for "large language model," is the same idea taken to an absurd scale. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, GLM, DeepSeek: they're all programs whose only job is to look at a piece of text and calculate which piece is most likely to come next. They've all worked this way since ChatGPT made them popular, at the end of 2022.

In my writing I use two nicknames for this. "Glorified text generator" I'll explain in a moment. The other is "[stochastic parrot]{.gls key=papagaio-estocastico}": something that repeats what it learned with a dose of dice-rolling in the middle. "Stochastic" is just the fancy way of saying "with chance built in."

### Pieces of Words

<!-- src: original -->
The model doesn't see letters or whole words. Before it goes into the model, all text gets chopped into pieces called [tokens]{.gls key=token}. Short, common words usually become a single token. Long or rare words become two, three, four pieces, a rare word like "unconstitutionally" might become three pieces: "un," "constitution" and "ally."

Each company has its own way of chopping, and the result varies from language to language, but the idea is always the same. That's why you'll see the token used as a unit of measure all over this book: the model reads in tokens, writes in tokens, and companies charge by the token.

### One Piece at a Time

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
Now the whole mechanism. You send a piece of text, which in the jargon is called a [prompt]{.gls}, the request you type. The model looks at all the tokens in that text and calculates, for every possible piece in its vocabulary, the chance that it comes next. Then it draws one token from among the few most likely.

That chosen token gets glued onto the end of the text. And the process starts over from scratch with the text one piece longer: look at everything, calculate the chances, draw, glue, repeat. A three-paragraph answer takes hundreds of turns of this loop, one piece at a time.

![Each turn of the loop calculates the chances of the next pieces, draws one and starts over with the longer text.](assets/figures/en/ch01-proximo-token.png){#fig-ch01-proximo-token fig-alt="A starting text, 'The cat sat on the', on the left. Arrows lead from it to several possible pieces, each with a bar showing its chance: 'roof' with the biggest bar, 'wall' and 'sofa' smaller, others almost invisible. A die marks the draw. The chosen piece is glued onto the text, and the process repeats from the longer sentence, forming a path that branches out."}

<!-- src: original -->
Notice the draw. It explains why the same question, asked twice, can get two different answers.

There's a setting that controls how much chance goes into it, called [temperature]{.gls key=temperatura}. In chat apps it's usually hidden, picked by the vendor. Low temperature makes the model almost always pick the most likely piece. High temperature lets less obvious options in, which sounds more creative and gets more things wrong.

<!-- src: original -->
When OpenAI announced GPT-2 in 2019, the most famous example in the announcement was a piece about unicorns that spoke perfect English, hidden in a valley in the Andes. Reading the fine print, the text on display was the best of ten tries [@radford2019better]. Ten draws, and the announcement showed the prettiest one. Hold on to that story, because the same announcement comes back later in this chapter.

### Likely and True

<!-- src: original -->
The model picks the piece that best fits what came before. Most of the time, the most likely piece is also the correct one, because the model was trained on an enormous amount of text written by people who knew what they were talking about. But the criterion is fitting, and the model has no internal mechanism that checks whether the result is true.

That's where [hallucination]{.gls key=alucinacao} comes from, when the model makes up a date, a book or a law with the same confidence it uses for real things. It's a natural side effect of how the machine works, and @sec-ch04 is entirely about it.

> The model picks what's likely. Whether it's true, it has no way of knowing.

## Where the Learning Happens

<!-- src: original -->
Where do those chances come from? Here's the distinction that confuses newcomers the most: a model's life has two completely separate phases, [training]{.gls key=treinamento} and use.

### Training

<!-- src: original -->
Picture a sound mixing panel with billions of little adjustment knobs. At the start, they're all in random positions, and what comes out of the panel is noise. Training consists of taking a mountain of text, hiding the next piece of each passage and asking the model to guess it. When it guesses wrong, an automatic procedure turns each knob a tiny bit in the direction that would have led to the right answer.

This repeats over trillions of pieces of text: web pages, books, forums, public source code. Nobody turns a single knob by hand. In the end, the knob positions hold, in a way not even their creators can read properly, the patterns of everything the model saw.

<!-- src: original -->
Those knobs are the model's [parameters]{.gls key=parametros}, also called [weights]{.gls key=pesos}. When you read that a model is "70B," that's 70 billion of them. GPT-2, from 2019, had 1.5 billion parameters and was trained on the text of 8 million web pages [@radford2019better]. GPT-3, a year later, jumped to 175 billion [@brown2020language].

<!-- src: original -->
Training a large model takes weeks or months, fills entire buildings with [graphics cards]{.gls key=gpu} and costs a fortune. Stanford University's annual artificial intelligence report estimated that the computing used to train GPT-4 cost some 78 million dollars, and Google's Gemini Ultra some 191 million [@hai2024aiindex]. After that main training, the model still goes through rounds of tuning to learn to behave like a polite assistant, one that answers questions instead of just continuing the text.

### Use

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
When training ends, the result is a computer file. A huge file, full of numbers, that gets locked and becomes read-only. What runs in the data centers are copies of that file, one on each server, isolated from each other. Your conversation doesn't talk to anyone else's, and nothing that happens in it flows back to the original file.

Using the model even has a technical name: [inference]{.gls key=inferencia}. Every time you send a question, your text passes through the file's numbers, and the numbers don't change. To the model, today's question and next month's arrive at an identical machine.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
Hence the consequence that surprises people most: the model doesn't learn from you. New knowledge enters in only one place, the next round of training, when the company spends millions and weeks producing a new file and releasing it under a new version number. Between one release and the next, the file doesn't change a single byte.

I felt this firsthand. I spent months on a project converting old games, which you'll meet in @sec-ch08, with hundreds of rounds of trial and error. From the model's point of view, none of it happened: the one that ran on the last day knew exactly as much as the one on the first. I'm the one who learned something.

<!-- src: 2026/09/28/llms-sao-boas-em-reproduzir-o-que-existe-e-coisas-novas -->
That's why the intern comparison, which I use a lot myself when talking about AI, has a limit. Interns accumulate experience. The model wakes up every day with its knowledge frozen on the day training ended. What accumulates during the work is yours: your notes, your tests, the text you saved.

> The model you use today is a locked file. The one who learns in the conversation is you.

<!-- src: original -->
"But it remembered what I said ten messages ago!" It did, and the trick is simple. Every time you send a new message, the app resends the entire conversation up to that point along with it. The model rereads everything from the top, every single time. That text riding along with each request is called the [context]{.gls key=contexto}, it has a limited size, and it's the subject of the next chapter.

### The 2017 Architecture

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
All these models, from OpenAI to Anthropic, from the Americans to the Chinese, share the same skeleton. It was published in June 2017 by Google researchers, in a paper called *Attention Is All You Need*, and it's called the [transformer]{.gls} [@vaswani2017attention].

<!-- src: original -->
The central idea fits in one sentence: to decide what comes next, each piece of the text looks at everything that came before it and weighs which parts matter most at that moment. In "I went to withdraw money at the bank," when the model reaches "bank" it looks back at "withdraw money" and realizes this is the kind with tellers, not the kind with a river. That mechanism of weighing relationships is called attention, hence the paper's title.

The transformer's other advantage was practical: it processes the pieces in parallel, which was a perfect fit for graphics cards. That's what made it possible to train ever larger models on ever more abundant text.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In February 2026, almost nine years later, no other breakthrough had surpassed that milestone. Everything that came after, however impressive, was optimization on top of the same foundation: better ways to train, to align, to make the model "think" more before answering. That will matter when we get to the S-curve.

## Why It Feels Like Someone's There

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
If it's just a text generator, why does the conversation feel so much like talking to a person? The first part of the answer is in the training material. The model learned from billions of conversations, letters, arguments and heart-to-hearts written by people. The most likely continuation of a human conversation sounds human.

The second part of the answer is in us. Human beings attribute personality to almost anything, and it takes very little.

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
In 1966, an MIT professor named Joseph Weizenbaum published a program called Eliza [@weizenbaum1966eliza]. It imitated a psychotherapist using a handful of simple rules: it took the person's sentence and handed it back as a question. If you wrote that you'd been feeling sad, it asked why you'd been feeling sad. There was no learning in there at all, just rewriting rules.

<!-- src: original -->
Weizenbaum himself wrote that some people were very hard to convince that Eliza was not human. He also told how his secretary, who knew perfectly well it was a program, asked him to leave the room so she could talk to the machine in private [@tarnoff2023weizenbaum]. That was sixty years ago, with a program infinitely simpler than any chatbot today.

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
That's where the "glorified" in my nickname comes from. People who know little about AI look at it the way the ape in *2001: A Space Odyssey* looks at the monolith: as a divinity that must be worshipped. The ape doesn't know what the monolith is, and that's why it kneels.

<!-- src: original -->
Knowing how the trick works changes that relationship without making the tool any less useful. The text that comes out of the model can be excellent, and often is. It comes from patterns learned from millions of real people, recombined competently.

The feeling of talking to someone is real. On the other side, though, there's only the math of probabilities.

### A Hammer

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
It also means the model has limits built into its very nature. Ask it to find every security problem in a program and it will find some, with no guarantee that it found them all. It was made to give the most likely answer based on what it saw in training, and what wasn't in training it doesn't know. It's a tool, like a hammer.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Hammers don't turn screws either, and nobody calls that a flaw in the hammer. For a lot of repetitive work, like writing tests, reorganizing code, building a new screen similar to an old one or translating a system's interface, the LLM is the perfect hammer. To interpret a law, decide on an exception or tell you how your company should run, it needs an experienced human approving or rejecting.

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
That's why I don't understand the over-hyped or the over-haters. Why glorify a hammer? Why hate a hammer?

## The First Hype

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Anyone who thinks today's hype is unprecedented needs to meet Frank Rosenblatt. This book will bring him up again, so he deserves a proper introduction.

<!-- src: original -->
Rosenblatt was a psychologist working at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, in the United States. In January 1957 he described, in a technical report, the Perceptron: a machine that would learn to recognize patterns by adjusting internal connections after each mistake, loosely inspired by the neurons in the brain [@rosenblatt1957perceptron]. It was the same principle as the little adjustment knobs I explained above, in a tiny version. It's the grandfather of the [neural networks]{.gls key=rede-neural} behind every LLM today.

<!-- src: original -->
In July 1958, the US Navy, which funded the research, presented the work to the press. The demonstration ran on a room-sized IBM 704 computer fed with punched cards. After 50 tries, the machine taught itself to tell cards marked on the left from cards marked on the right [@lefkowitz2019perceptron].

That was all. But the *New York Times* of July 8 reported that the Navy had revealed the embryo of an electronic computer that it expected would be able to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence [@sejnowski2020unreasonable].

![The Mark I Perceptron, the hardware version of Rosenblatt's machine. Photo: US Navy / public domain.](assets/figures/ch01-perceptron.jpg){#fig-ch01-perceptron fig-alt="Historical photograph of the Mark I Perceptron, released by the US Navy in 1960. An operator adjusts the machine's camera, pointed at a card with the letter C and lit by two lamps. In the background, panels with the wiring of the machine's adjustable connections."}

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Rosenblatt also made a lot of noise. As Sam Altman, of OpenAI, and Dario Amodei, of Anthropic, do today, he talked about machines that would soon be much smarter than human beings, and the newspapers of the day printed it. The promises died as promises.

<!-- src: original -->
In 1969, Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published the book *Perceptrons*, which went after the machine's limitations and all but buried that line of research. Public money for artificial intelligence dried up, and there followed decades of little progress that became known as the AI winter. Rosenblatt didn't live to see his ideas come back: he died in 1971, in a boating accident, on his 43rd birthday [@lefkowitz2019perceptron].

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Neural networks only came back in force when the three things missing in 1958 showed up: absurd amounts of data, thanks to the web and social networks; graphics cards able to do billions of calculations in parallel; and a few new training tricks. The turning point was AlexNet, from 2012, a network by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever and Geoffrey Hinton that recognized images far better than anything that existed [@krizhevsky2012imagenet]. Five years later came the transformer.

### Bubbles Burst, Technologies Stay

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
That's why I've been saying, since the podcast, that this is the second AI bubble. The first was Rosenblatt's, in the 1960s. A [bubble]{.gls key=bolha} is the period when expectations about a technology climb far beyond what it delivers, until they correct abruptly.

And here lies another confusion the clips spread. "But you said AI is a bubble that's going to pop!" I did, and I still say it.

The dot-com bubble, which inflated from the mid-1990s to 2001, burst. Lots of companies went bankrupt and lots of people lost their jobs. The internet kept existing, and grew.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
It'll be the same with AI. The bubble bursts, companies that bet everything on the wave disappear, and LLMs keep existing, roughly in the same form, a bit faster and more efficient. Economic cycles and technological cycles are different things. Whoever was an enemy of the internet in 2001 lost out too.

> The bubble bursts and the technology stays. Don't bet your whole life on the same horse.

### This AGI Business

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
On the podcast I was reacting to something else: the hysteria around predictions that [AGI]{.gls} was two or three years away. AGI stands for artificial general intelligence, a machine that reasons and decides on its own about any subject, like a human being, and that evolves by itself. Executives at AI companies talked about it as if it were right around the corner.

<!-- src: 2026/02/24/rant-o-akita-abriu-as-pernas-pra-ia -->
My position has a premise the clips always cut out: with today's technology. As long as every new GPT or Claude is an evolution of the previous one, on top of the same 2017 architecture, there are limits, and you can estimate what it would cost to push them. Tomorrow some breakthrough may appear that changes everything. But nobody plans their life counting on a breakthrough that hasn't happened yet.

None of this makes today's AI useless. That's the conclusion I never managed to understand from the people who watched the clips.

## Too Dangerous

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
On February 14, 2019, OpenAI announced GPT-2. The announcement said the model had been trained simply to predict the next word in 40 gigabytes of internet text, and that it could summarize, translate and answer questions without having been trained specifically for any of that [@radford2019better]. It was the mechanism I described at the start of the chapter, at a scale nobody had seen work so well.

<!-- src: original -->
The announcement also said that, out of concern about malicious applications of the technology, the company would not release the trained model. It put out only a much smaller version, for researchers. The text called it an experiment in responsible disclosure and admitted it wasn't sure this was the right decision [@radford2019better].

The press read it in the most dramatic way possible. That same day, the British newspaper *The Guardian* reported that the new fake text generator might be too dangerous to release, according to its own creators [@hern2019dangerous]. It was that "too dangerous" that went around the world.

<!-- src: original -->
What happened next, almost nobody followed. OpenAI released larger versions gradually over the year and, on November 5, 2019, published the full 1.5-billion-parameter model [@openai2019gpt2]. The world didn't end. In October 2026, when I retraced that timeline, the caution of 2019 struck me as almost cute: a model that size today runs comfortably on a home computer.

<!-- src: original -->
There's a detail in the announcement's author list worth remembering. Among them were Ilya Sutskever, whom you just met with AlexNet, and Dario Amodei, who by 2021 was already running Anthropic, the company behind Claude [@anthropic2021raises]. I have no way of knowing what was going through each of their heads. I do know that "our model is too powerful to release" became a genre of announcement, and that the genre makes headlines.

You'll run into exactly this script again in @sec-ch14, in June 2026, with another model, at the company run by the same Dario Amodei, and with a lot more money at stake. When a company says its product is too dangerous, it's saying, in the same breath, that its product is more powerful than the competition's.

## The S-Curve

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In 2023 I made a prediction: the evolution of this architecture would follow an [S-curve]{.gls key=curva-em-s}. It's the shape almost every technology traces when you chart its progress over time. First a slow childhood, then a fast and exciting climb, and finally a slowdown, when the technology matures and each step forward costs much more than the last.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Look at the GPT family through that lens. From GPT-1 (2018) to GPT-2 was a huge leap [@radford2018improving]. GPT-2 to GPT-3, another gigantic leap; 3 to 4, an excellent improvement, but visibly smaller. 4 to 5, most regular people barely noticed the difference.

![The GPT family on an S-curve: the first leaps are huge, the latest ones cost much more and deliver less.](assets/figures/en/ch01-curva-em-s.png){#fig-ch01-curva-em-s fig-alt="Chart with two curves over a timeline from 2018 to 2025. The capability curve is S-shaped: it rises slowly, shoots up between GPT-2 and GPT-3, and flattens between GPT-4 and GPT-5. The training cost curve rises faster and faster, without flattening. The points GPT-1, GPT-2, GPT-3, GPT-4 and GPT-5 are marked on the capability curve."}

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Meanwhile, the cost of each generation kept climbing. Each new model demands considerably more graphics cards, more memory and more energy to deliver a version that isn't much better than the previous one. That's the ceiling of the curve. Until a new architecture comes along, we only get closer and closer to it, spending absurd amounts of resources.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
The vendors themselves gave signs of this. In 2023, the GPT-4 technical report refused to publish the model's size, citing the competitive landscape and safety implications [@openai2023gpt4]. Since then, parameter counts have vanished from marketing.

In November 2024, the Reuters news agency reported that the big labs were reaping smaller gains than expected from simply making models bigger and were looking for other routes [@reuters2024limitations]. It was the curve flattening, told by the people footing the bill.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Nobody stood still, of course. Scientists and engineers did what they always do: they optimized. Out came models that write a long chain of reasoning before answering, cheaper ways to run giant models, programs that let the model use tools and check its own work. None of that replaces the foundation.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
There's something here that looks like a contradiction. In 2026, programming with AI got a lot better, and I felt it day to day. But by my measurements over the year, that improvement came almost entirely from what was built around the model, with the base intelligence practically standing still. How that's possible is the subject of @sec-ch03, and the balance sheet for the whole year comes in @sec-ch20.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
For you, if you don't program, the S-curve brings good news. The next model will be a bit faster and make a few fewer mistakes. It won't turn into something else. What you learn now about how these tools work still holds for the next version.

<!-- src: 2026/10/12/llms-agentes-competente-o-suficiente -->
On its own, an LLM is an expensive parrot. Used well, by someone who understands what it is, the same parrot becomes a tool competent enough to do real work, and the rest of this book is about the difference between those two situations.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
One question was left hanging. If the model is a locked file that learns nothing from you, how does the assistant remember what you agreed on half an hour ago? And why, an hour later, does it seem to have forgotten everything?

At the end of March 2026, the entire source code of Claude Code leaked by mistake. Suddenly it was possible to see inside how a commercial product fakes memory.
