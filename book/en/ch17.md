# What the Apps Know About You {#sec-ch17}

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
In early July 2026, I downloaded three Android apps that plenty of people carry on their phones without a second thought. TikTok, version 46.0.1. Clash of Kings, a strategy game from the Chinese studio Elex. And gov.br, the Brazilian government's official digital-identity app, which a lot of Brazilians install because they have to, with little say in the matter.

I didn't want to know whether they worked. I wanted to know what was inside. What data they ask for, who they send it to, what identifiers they build about the device of whoever uses them.

So I handed the code of all three to Claude Fable 5, the Anthropic [frontier model]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} you met in @sec-ch14, and asked for a full analysis of each.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Before I tell you what turned up, here's the disclaimer that changes how you should read the rest of this chapter: not everything that follows is verified. Each of these apps has hundreds of thousands of files. I have no way to guarantee how deep the model actually went without re-auditing everything myself, file by file. And I didn't.

There are more layers of uncertainty. The install packages came from a mirror site, APKPure, instead of Google's official store, so there's a small chance they were tampered with along the way. And even after cracking them open, a pile of [binary]{.gls key=binario} components went unread, on the assumption that they were common Google libraries. What you're about to read is a panorama, a long way from a forensic report.

<!-- src: original -->
The question many people bring to this chapter comes in two parts. Is my phone spying on me? And now that anyone has an AI that writes flawless prose and makes realistic video, are scams about to become impossible to spot? Both answers share the same root.

Whoever collects your data and whoever tries to fool you are counting on the same thing: that you won't read the label.

## Opening the Box

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Few people know how easy it is to open an Android app. The install package, the APK file, is basically a zip. You unpack it, run a free tool called jadx on it [@skylot2026jadx], and Java that a programmer can read comes out the other end.

If the app was built in Flutter, like gov.br, another tool, Blutter, reconstructs a good chunk of the code [@worawit2026blutter]. If it's a game built on the Cocos2d-x engine, like Clash of Kings, the scripts are often sitting right there in plain text, no decryption needed. Clash of Kings had almost 12,000 of them [@akita2026clashofkings].

<!-- src: original -->
This kind of work has a name: [reverse engineering]{.gls key=engenharia-reversa}. You figure out how a program works on the inside starting from the finished program, with no access to the original code its programmers wrote. Picture a mechanic who takes apart a rival's car engine to see how it was made. He doesn't have the factory blueprints, only the part, and still, looking carefully, he learns a lot.

Reverse engineering is an old discipline, long predating LLMs, with mature tools and specialists who spend whole careers at it. What changed in 2026 was the cost of entry, and I'll come back to that at the end of the chapter.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
There are limits, and they matter. The code jadx spits out won't let you recompile the app. The names of the program's parts come scrambled on purpose, a technique called [obfuscation]{.gls key=ofuscacao}: instead of "sendLocation," you read "a.b.c." Resources are missing, and so are the signing keys.

For reading and understanding, though, it's more than enough. You can see the internet addresses the app contacts, the permissions it asks for, the identifiers it assembles and the third-party components it bundles inside. This kind of reading, done without running the program, is called [static analysis]{.gls key=analise-estatica}.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
And then there's the main limit. The most sensitive part of an app usually lives in [native code]{.gls key=codigo-nativo}, programs in C or C++ already turned into machine instructions, which these tools can't make readable again. You can see the file exists, you can see a few loose strings of text inside it, and the rest is a wall of numbers. In TikTok's case, that's precisely where the guts are.

## A Serial Number You Never Authorized

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
You've heard it a thousand times: if it's free, you're the product. The line is true, and it almost always stays abstract. How, exactly, do you become the product? The answer has a technical name: the device [fingerprint]{.gls}.

A fingerprint is an identifier a company assembles by combining dozens of signals from your phone:

- device model, OS version, screen resolution, processor and memory;
- carrier, timezone and language;
- battery level and installed fonts;
- nearby Wi-Fi networks and Bluetooth devices;
- motion sensor readings.

None of those data points says who you are on its own. Millions of people have the same phone model, and millions more share the same timezone. The combination, though, is so specific that it becomes practically unique. It works like an implicit serial number for your device, one you never authorized anybody to write down.

![Weak, common signals add up to an almost unique identity that's hard to erase.](assets/figures/en/ch17-fingerprint.png){#fig-ch17-fingerprint fig-alt="Eight small generic icons in a column (model, screen, processor, battery, timezone, nearby Wi-Fi, nearby Bluetooth, sensors) converge through arrows into a single fingerprint in the center, labeled fingerprint. Beside it, a trash can with the words clear data and reinstall, and an arrow showing the same fingerprint coming out of it, unchanged."}

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
What makes it valuable is the persistence. A cookie you can clear. The [advertising ID]{.gls key=id-de-publicidade}, the code your phone's operating system hands to advertisers, you can reset in the settings. A well-built fingerprint survives you clearing the app's data and reinstalling it, and sometimes it even survives a factory reset.

Every time you try to start from scratch, it recognizes you as the same person. With that, whoever collects it can cross-reference your behavior across apps, across sessions and across reinstalls, and build a profile that follows you even when you think you wiped your tracks. That profile is the product in the old line.

## Three Apps, Three Portraits

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
The three apps landed at very different points on that spectrum. Everything below applies to the versions downloaded that July of 2026 and to what static analysis managed to read. All three full analyses are published, with step-by-step instructions for anyone who wants to reproduce them [@akita2026tiktok; @akita2026clashofkings; @akita2026govbr].

### TikTok and the Black Box

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
TikTok was the densest: 37 packages of compiled Java code and 204 native code libraries [@akita2026tiktok]. I started with the reassuring side. The analysis found no secret servers. Every address the app contacts belongs to TikTok itself, to ByteDance, its owner, or to some ad or analytics vendor with a known name.

There was no sign of IMEI collection either, the phone's official serial number. The app doesn't read SMS or call logs, and it doesn't ask for the permission that would let it list every installed app.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
On the worrying side, the code is capable of collecting a lot:

- a persistent device fingerprint;
- precise location;
- scans of the Wi-Fi networks and Bluetooth devices around you;
- your contacts, if you turn on "find friends";
- motion sensor readings;
- a check against a fixed list of 91 named apps, to see which ones you have installed, among them competitors, PayPal, Venmo, Spotify and ByteDance's sister apps.

And the code is set up to pass your advertising ID on to about six third parties. The limit I explain in "What the Blueprint Doesn't Show," further on applies to the whole list: it is what reading the code shows the app can do, and nobody measured how much of it the app actually sends.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
And the central finding: the fingerprint and much of the telemetry are assembled in native code, in a library called libmetasec_ov, and each request's signature goes out encrypted. Even decompiling the whole app, you can't tell exactly which bytes leave the device.

To attack that black box, one of the steps pulled the readable strings out of the 204 native libraries. libmetasec_ov gave the game away. You can read the names of the fields it was built to collect (device identifiers, battery level and state, screen size), the function that signs the requests, the ByteDance telemetry endpoints baked into the binary, and even the scheduler that fires off the upload on a timer.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
The same sweep went looking for what everyone is afraid of finding: a keylogger, a program that injects taps on the screen as if it were you. It found none.

The scary words that showed up in the search were false friends. "keylog" was the debug log for the encrypted connection. "screenshot" belonged to the video player and to a content-authenticity system.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
The portrait that remains holds two true statements at once. Nobody has proven that TikTok is spyware. And it was built to collect far more than it needs to show you videos, in a way designed to be hard to audit.

We know what libmetasec_ov does and where it reports to. The exact contents of each upload stay locked away.

### Clash of Kings and the Wallet

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Clash of Kings was a pleasant surprise on the privacy front. No GPS, camera, microphone, contacts, SMS or IMEI. The two most sensitive reads it can do, the list of installed apps and the physical address of the Wi-Fi chip, both require consent [@akita2026clashofkings].

The concern is the same one as with TikTok, in a simpler version. The game writes a device identifier in four different places: the system settings, the game's own preferences, the memory card and the cloud backup. Four copies exist so that at least one survives a reinstall. There's also telemetry going to servers in China.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Where the game really gets you is money: loot boxes with random prizes, countdown-timer offers, VIP tiers that give paying players an edge, and leaderboards for whoever spends the most. The drop odds, at least, are shown in the game. Decent privacy, wallet in danger.

### gov.br and the Right Way to Do It

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
gov.br, version 3.8.4, left me relieved [@akita2026govbr]. By the standards of a government app you're practically required to have, it's well built and respectful of privacy. Traffic goes almost entirely to the government's own addresses, to SERPRO and to Dataprev, the federal government's data-processing companies.

The connection only accepts the specific certificate of the government's server, which makes it harder for anyone to sit in the middle of it. Credentials are stored in Android's encrypted vault. And the app defends itself hard when it runs on a tampered device.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Most important: no commercial ad trackers. None of the ad-tech companies that show up in TikTok.

There were two caveats. The app bundles Firebase, Google's analytics and crash-reporting package, with collection on by default, which is the lazy default of almost every app today. And the proof-of-life selfie gets uploaded to the government's servers, which is the point of an identity check, and it doesn't go to third parties. One question stayed open, and only watching the real traffic would answer it: whether your CPF, the Brazilian taxpayer number, goes along in the crash reports sent to Google.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
If every app were built with this kind of care, I wouldn't have a reason to write this chapter.

### The Group Effort

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Cross-referencing the three, a pattern shows up. Almost every app you download carries components from other companies inside it, the [SDKs]{.gls key=sdk}, ready-made packages a programmer drops into the app instead of writing them from scratch. The ones that show up most:

- Firebase and Google's services, present in all three, so ubiquitous they became invisible infrastructure;
- ad attribution SDKs, which link "you clicked this ad" to "you installed and used the app" (TikTok had five at once, each grabbing your advertising ID on its own);
- ad networks, like Google's;
- region-specific analytics services, like Tencent Bugly in Clash of Kings, with code to send data to China;
- a specialized fingerprinting service.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
That last one deserves a closer look. Inside TikTok was ThreatMetrix, from LexisNexis Risk Solutions, a company whose product is a digital identity for whoever is on the other side of the screen, built from the device, the location, the network address and the behavior, and sold to detect fraud [@lexisnexis2026threatmetrix]. It's a legitimate use. And it's literally fingerprinting sold as a service, embedded in the app.

So the product in the old line comes off an assembly line. Five, six, seven partners, each grabbing a piece, and the fingerprint stitching it all back into a single profile. None of them has to spy on you alone, because the job gets done as a group effort.

## What the Blueprint Doesn't Show

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Static analysis shows what an app is capable of: what was built into it, what permissions it asks for, what addresses it knows. It doesn't show what actually leaves your phone at any given moment. For that, someone has to run the app on a prepared device and intercept the connection, reading every packet that goes out. That's called dynamic analysis, and I didn't do it.

![Static analysis reads the blueprint; only a live traffic capture shows what actually went out the door.](assets/figures/en/ch17-capacidade-vs-prova.png){#fig-ch17-capacidade-vs-prova fig-alt="Two halves side by side. On the left, a magnifying glass over an app's floor plan, with labels like permissions, addresses and identifiers, under the title static analysis: what it can do. On the right, a phone connected to a pipe carrying packets out to the internet, with an interceptor in the middle, under the title live capture: what actually went out. Some packets carry a padlock, marked as encrypted."}

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
Having code that reads your location proves the app can read your location. It doesn't prove when it reads it, how often, or who it sends it to. In TikTok, where the fingerprinting engine sits in native code and the uploads go out encrypted, that gap gets even wider.

That's why nothing here proves or disproves that any of these apps is safe or dangerous. It's a glimpse, with no pretense of a verdict.

> Static analysis shows what an app is capable of. Only a capture of the real traffic shows what it did.

<!-- src: original -->
There's a second layer of caution, and it's about the model itself. Fable 5 read hundreds of thousands of files at a speed no human can match. But an LLM also summarizes, skips and sometimes makes things up (@sec-ch04), and I had no way to check everything it reported.

The right order is the same as with any tool: it points you to where to look, and whoever says something in public needs to have looked.

<!-- src: original -->
That goes for both sides of the hysteria. Whoever says "app X is spying on you" based on a list of permissions is claiming more than the evidence supports. So is whoever says "it's all fine, they found nothing" based on the same list. The correct sentence is usually more boring: the app has the capability, and nobody has shown the use yet.

## What You Can Do About Your Phone

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
You won't zero this out short of leaving the internet. But you can cut down a lot of what's exposed, and none of the steps below requires knowing how to program:

- Don't consent to what isn't needed. GPS is the big one. A video app doesn't need your precise location to work. Deny it, or grant only "while using the app." Same goes for contacts, microphone and camera: allow them only when the feature you want to use right then requires it.
- Reset your advertising ID now and then. On Android, you can even delete it outright. That doesn't kill the fingerprint, but it breaks part of the trail.
- Be suspicious of "find friends." That's the trigger for uploading your contacts. You hand over the entire network of people who trusted you with their number.
- Prefer the web version when you can. A browser with a tracker blocker gives off fewer signals than the app, which has access to sensors the browser doesn't hand over.

<!-- src: original -->
I'd add a habit that takes five minutes: once a month, open your phone's permissions screen and see who has access to location, contacts and microphone. There's almost always some app you installed for a single errand and forgot about.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
And last, accept the trade-off with your eyes open. Nobody needs to go paranoid and uninstall everything; it's enough to know the price.

If you find TikTok fun enough to pay for it with your fingerprint, great, that's an informed decision. The problem is paying without knowing you're paying.

## The Scam Comes In Through Vanity

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
There's a much older way than fingerprinting for someone to take things from you: asking politely.

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
I've been getting an absurd amount of email since my YouTube days. Event invitations, collab proposals, sponsorship offers. I delete all of them, unread. I don't answer the phone, and I block anyone who messages me directly on WhatsApp.

It works for me, but I know that way of living is the exception.

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
From what I see, people who fall for [phishing]{.gls}, the scam where a message poses as someone trustworthy to steal data, passwords or money, rarely fall because of technical ignorance. They fall because of vanity, fear or rush. Fear and rush drive the classic bank scam, the account that "will be blocked in 24 hours." Vanity is the bait I see aimed most at people like me, and the least talked about.

*"We'd like to invite you to our exclusive event."* *"Your brand has been selected for a special partnership."* *"Congratulations, you've been nominated as a reference in your sector."* The dopamine hits before reason has a chance to step in. Someone recognized your work, someone wants to give you money. That's exactly when the scammer gets you.

The person who falls for the Nigerian prince scam is rarely the multinational CEO. It's the micro-influencer who gets a sponsorship offer too good to be true, the small business owner invited to an event that looks legitimate.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/pseudo-techcrunch-tentou-me-pegar-em-golpe-calendly-e-uma-merda -->
In September 2026 a scammer tried it on me. A DM landed on X from a verified account, old and active, presenting itself as a journalist covering AI for TechCrunch. It wanted to interview me about how coding agents handle a project's context, which is exactly the subject I write about. The request ended on a scheduling link.

The link went through a legitimate scheduling service and landed on a real authorization screen from X itself, asking to give an unknown app control over my account: posting, following, editing my profile and blocking on my behalf.

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/pseudo-techcrunch-tentou-me-pegar-em-golpe-calendly-e-uma-merda -->
I recognized the pattern because, in March, José Valim, the creator of the Elixir language, had told the world he'd fallen for the exact same lure: someone claiming to be from TechCrunch, an interview on the topic he knows best, and his account temporarily hijacked [@valim2026hacked]. In both cases, the bait was the victim's own line of work.

> The modern scam shows up with flawless grammar, carrying a compliment cut to your measure.

### The End of the Typo

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
For years, the standard anti-scam tip was to look for spelling and grammar mistakes. A "bank" message full of typos and broken grammar was a scam. That tip is dead.

With an [LLM]{.gls}, any criminal generates emails in perfect Portuguese in seconds, without a single grammar error, with professional formatting and the exact tone of a real company. The same model that fixes your writing writes theirs. The old "look at the typos" doesn't work as a filter anymore.

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
In March 2026, that got me thinking the other way around. If teaching everyone to spot scams doesn't work, because vanity, fear and rush speak louder than training, why not a tool that does the suspecting for you?

### One Layer Among Several

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
That's how Frank FBI was born, short for Fraud Bureau of Investigation [@akita2026frankfbi], an experiment of mine: you forward the suspicious email to a dedicated address and, within a few minutes, get a risk report back in the same thread. Behind the scenes, the email goes through six layers: the sender's authentication and reputation, the content, public databases of malicious links, a search to see whether whoever signs it exists and is who they claim to be, and finally the LLM analysis.

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
The AI layer weighs 30% of the score, and the other five add up to 70%. Three models from different companies read the email separately, and what they claim is checked against the concrete data from the other layers. If a model writes that "this domain was registered a day ago" and the registry lookup says otherwise, the claim is dropped.

![The suspicious email goes through six layers; the LLM is one voice among several, and its claims are checked against the other layers' data.](assets/figures/en/ch17-camadas-frank-fbi.png){#fig-ch17-camadas-frank-fbi fig-alt="An envelope labeled suspicious email enters at the top and passes through six stacked horizontal bands, each with its weight: sender authentication 15%, sender reputation 15%, content 15%, known malicious links 15%, signer identity 10% and LLM analysis 30%. In the LLM band, three small boxes feed a vote. Dotted cross-check arrows on the left connect the LLM band to the other five. On the right, the six bands converge into a risk gauge from 0 to 100."}

> An LLM is a good layer in a scam detector. It should never be the judge.

### Thinking Like the Attacker

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
The program came together in about 17 hours of work, with a lot of the code written by the AI, and none of the important security hardening came from the model. It was me who asked what happens if someone spoofs the address of whoever is allowed to use the service. And what happens if an attacker sends emails crafted to make the system report an innocent company's domain as a scam, an attack that has a name: poisoning.

The model implemented the defenses when I asked. Spotting the hole was my job, the same message as @sec-ch16 seen from another angle: AI speeds up whoever already knows where the danger lives.

### Why I Don't Offer This as a Service

<!-- src: 2026/03/09/atacando-fraudes-via-email-frank-fbi -->
Turning a tool like this into a public service is an idea I advise against in no uncertain terms. Whoever runs it has access to every forwarded email: personal data, company correspondence, conversations of people who are already in a vulnerable situation. That's why the code was built to run on your own server, with the data staying with you.

<!-- src: original -->
Notice the pattern. The "free" scam detector that reads all your email is the same business as the "free" app that builds your fingerprint. When the service costs nothing and you don't know who's on the other side, the suspicion starts with the tool itself.

### Habits Against Scams

<!-- src: original -->
You don't need to install any of this to protect yourself. The logic fits into habits anyone can follow:

<!-- src: 2026/09/22/pseudo-techcrunch-tentou-me-pegar-em-golpe-calendly-e-uma-merda -->
- A cold contact, with no introduction, asking for a meeting, a payment or your data, is a scam until proven otherwise.
- A real scheduling tool doesn't ask for a social media login. A screen asking to let an unknown app "access your account" is a red flag, even when the screen belongs to the social network itself.
- If you already authorized something like that, revoke it in your account's connected-apps settings.

<!-- src: original -->
- A tailored compliment, a scare or a rush is the warning sign. The better the offer, the slower you reply.
- Confirm through another channel. If the "bank" messaged you, call the number on your card, never the one that came in the message.
- Perfect grammar, the right logo and a professional tone don't prove anything anymore.

## Seeing Is No Longer Believing

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/seedance-2-0-finalmente-foi-lancado-primeiras-impressoes -->
If perfect text got cheap, so did video.

On April 15, 2026, ByteDance, the same company that owns TikTok, opened Seedance 2.0 to the public, a model that generates video from a mix of text, up to nine images, three video clips and three reference audio tracks. It hands back clips of up to 15 seconds with synced sound [@bytedance2026seedance].

I tested it on launch day, and the test I consider serious for this kind of tool uses a reference video. I put together a rough scene of an animated 3D character in Blender and asked Seedance to redo that scene with a polished character. With an explicit instruction to follow the reference's motion and camera, the result followed the choreography and came out more polished than anything I'd render by hand. It still took liberties with timing and framing.

Asking with text alone is a slot machine. Sometimes something pretty comes out on the first try, and that's luck, with no control at all.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/seedance-2-0-finalmente-foi-lancado-primeiras-impressoes -->
That April, the price still held back professional use. The Standard plan cost about $25 a month on annual billing and came with 2,500 credits, with every 10 seconds of video burning 6. Counting three to five tries per usable scene, that worked out to somewhere between 80 and 140 short clips a month.

Good enough for clips and prototypes. For continuous production, the math didn't close.

The market was moving fast, too: in March, OpenAI had announced the end of Sora, its video app [@axios2026sora].

### Deepfakes Are No Longer Hypothetical

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/seedance-2-0-finalmente-foi-lancado-primeiras-impressoes -->
Seedance has heavy filters. It blocks references to real people, like celebrities and politicians, and trademarked characters. But those filters only stop people using the tool above board and within the law. Anyone with bad intent finds another path, through an open model, a trick to get around the filter, a less strict platform.

Photos on the internet stopped counting as proof a long time ago. In 2026, video joined them in the same bucket. An ordinary person, with no training, can no longer tell generated video from real video at a glance.

<!-- src: original -->
The [deepfake]{.gls}, the fake AI-generated video or audio of a real person, has become ordinary. That changes some very concrete habits. The video of your son asking for money, the call in your boss's voice authorizing a transfer, the video of a politician saying something absurd on the eve of an election: none of them proves anything on its own.

The defense that works is the same as with email: confirm through another channel, with a question only the real person could answer. Agreeing on a code word with your family for emergencies costs nothing and solves a lot.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/seedance-2-0-finalmente-foi-lancado-primeiras-impressoes -->
The sooner we accept this as a society, the better off we are. And, as always with these models, a year or two from now what I showed in April will look primitive.

## Cheap, but Not Magic

<!-- src: original -->
The beginning of this chapter holds a second story, less obvious than the TikTok one. Reading the code of a big app was always possible, just expensive. It took a reverse engineering specialist, time and a lot of patience to wade through hundreds of thousands of files with scrambled names. That's why almost nobody did it out of curiosity, and the public depended on researchers, on news reports or on the word of the companies themselves.

<!-- src: 2026/07/08/fable-5-analisar-codigo-tiktok-clash-of-kings-govbr-fingerprint -->
In 2026, with a good LLM and a free afternoon, any curious developer can crack open their own phone and see what's running inside it, without depending on some tech influencer or an alarmist headline. It's the closest thing to reading the label before you eat that we have in the software world.

> Reverse engineering got cheap. It still takes someone who knows what to ask and who distrusts the answer.

<!-- src: original -->
"Cheap" comes with an asterisk, and you've already seen the main ones. The model doesn't open native code; it only reads the loose strings left inside it. It doesn't see what runs on the company's servers, where much of the logic of any modern service lives. It doesn't capture the traffic either.

And I can't prove it read everything it said it read.

What the AI did was clear the grunt work out of the way. The judgment of whoever asks is still the part that decides whether the result is worth anything. How far this goes, when someone promises to clone entire programs with an agent, is a conversation for @sec-ch19.

<!-- src: original -->
There's also the other side of the coin. The same tool that let me read TikTok without being a specialist lets a criminal read your bank's app looking for a gap, and lets the scammer write the perfect email and generate the convincing video. The barrier dropped for everyone at the same time. Whoever knows that is best placed to protect themselves.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
And that leaves the question many people have carried into this book since the first page, now with more force. If a model does in short order a reading that used to require a specialist, writes the email, generates the video and reads the code, what happens to the people who made a living doing those things?

In April 2026, watching an agent type code faster than anyone ever has, I remembered a technology that vanished from companies while programming only kept growing: the punch card. Part V starts there.
