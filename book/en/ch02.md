# The Memory of a Goldfish {#sec-ch02}

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
On the morning of March 31, 2026, a Tuesday, a security researcher named Chaofan Shou noticed that a software package anyone in the world could download had something inside it that wasn't supposed to be there [@vigliarolo2026anthropic].

The package was version 2.1.88 of Claude Code, Anthropic's coding agent, distributed through npm, the big public registry where programmers download tools written in JavaScript. Along with the program came, by mistake, a file of almost 60 megabytes called a [source map]{.gls key=source-map} [@franzen2026claude].

A source map is there for debugging: it leads from the squeezed code back to the original, so the programmer can find where an error happened. This one carried the original code, in full, some 512,000 lines in about 1,900 files.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
Within hours the code was mirrored in dozens of places. Anthropic said it had been a human packaging error, not a security breach, and that no customer data had leaked [@vigliarolo2026anthropic]. In practice, one of the most popular AI tools among programmers was wide open for anyone who wanted to look, and I, who used Claude Code every day, went to look.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The headlines went after the hidden features, and there were plenty. What hooked me was another part.

What hooked me was another part. For the first time you could see, line by line, how a top-tier commercial tool handles the problem every AI user feels firsthand and almost nobody understands: why does this thing forget what we agreed on an hour ago? And, when it seems to remember me, what exactly is it remembering?

## An Injustice to the Goldfish

<!-- src: original -->
In @sec-ch05 you'll see me spend three days mentoring an AI agent that kept repeating the same mistakes as if it had never heard the correction. The image that came to me at the time was an intern with the memory of a goldfish: motivated, fast, helpful, and unable to hold on to what it learned five minutes earlier.

The image works, but it's unfair to the fish.

<!-- src: original -->
The story about the goldfish's three-second memory is folklore. Animal cognition researchers have shown for decades that fish learn routes, recognize other fish and remember how to escape a net for months [@brown2015fish]. The goldfish in your living room tank remembers that you're the person who drops the food in every morning.

The AI model doesn't even remember that.

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
In @sec-ch01 we saw that an [LLM]{.gls} is a program trained to predict the next piece of text, and that its [weights]{.gls key=pesos}, the numbers that hold everything it learned in training, stay frozen once training is over. Nothing you say in a conversation changes those numbers. The model you use this afternoon is identical to the one this morning, and it will be identical tomorrow.

So how does it "remember" what you said three messages ago? It doesn't remember. It rereads.

### The Desk

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
With every message you send, the program behind the chat gathers everything: the company's opening instructions, the whole conversation from the start, the files that were read, the output of every command that ran. It assembles one big bundle of text and sends it to the model from scratch. The model reads all of it, generates the next answer, and forgets. On the next message, the program assembles the bundle again, now with one more message, and sends it again.

That bundle is the [context]{.gls key=contexto}: everything the model sees at a given moment. Whatever is outside it, as far as the model is concerned, doesn't exist.

I like to think of the context as a work desk. Every message, every open file, every command result is a sheet of paper on the desk. The model has no drawer and no filing cabinet. With every answer it reads the entire desk, from the first sheet to the last, and answers based on what's there.

![The model's work desk: everything it sees is on top of it, and whatever falls off the edge stops existing for it. AI-generated illustration.](assets/figures/ch02-mesa.png){#fig-ch02-mesa fig-alt="Illustration of an office desk covered in stacked sheets of paper. The oldest sheets are at the edge and some are falling off the side. There are no drawers or cabinets; just the desktop."}

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
The desk has a fixed size. That size is the [context window]{.gls key=janela-de-contexto}, measured in [tokens]{.gls key=token}, the little pieces of words the model processes (@sec-ch01). A number in tokens means nothing to anybody, so it's worth translating. One token is, on average, about three quarters of an English word, and often less in other languages.

- 100,000 tokens is a full-length novel, something like *The Great Gatsby* twice over.
- 200,000 tokens is a big novel, something like *Pride and Prejudice* with room to spare.
- 1 million tokens is the entire *Lord of the Rings* trilogy plus *The Hobbit*.

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
These sizes changed fast, and it's best to read the numbers as a snapshot of their time. Between 2022 and 2023, GPT-3.5 had a window of about 4,000 tokens, a few pages; 32,000 was a luxury. By April 2026, Claude Opus 4.6, Gemini 3.1 Pro and other frontier models already offered 1 million. By the time you read this, the number is probably something else.

### The Price of a Full Desk

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
A Tolkien-sized desk seems to solve the problem. Just pile everything on it and let the model sort it out. In practice, filling the desk has three costs.

The first is money. Every token on the desk is billed, or deducted from your quota, on every message, because the whole desk is reread on every message. The second is time: the fuller it is, the longer each answer takes.

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
The third is the most treacherous. The more paper on the desk, the worse the model gets at finding the sheet that matters. In 2023, a study led by Stanford researchers showed that models with large windows easily found information at the beginning or the end of the context and stumbled on whatever sat in the middle [@liu2024lost]. The 2026 models handle the middle of the desk much better, but the problem shrank without going away.

An important decision made early in the afternoon, buried under fifty pages of command output, becomes a sheet at the bottom of the pile.

## Notes That Lose Detail

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
Sooner or later the desk fills up. In an ordinary chat, the conversation simply stops, or the tool tells you to start a new one. A coding [agent]{.gls key=agente}, the model placed inside a program that lets it carry out tasks on its own, reading files and running commands (the subject of @sec-ch03), can't stop in the middle of a three-hour task. So it does something clever and a little dangerous.

When the desk gets close to the limit, the agent asks the model itself to write a summary of everything that has happened so far. Then it throws away the original conversation, puts the summary in its place and keeps working on top of it. That's called [compaction]{.gls key=compactacao}.

It's as if, in the middle of a long meeting, someone collected every paper on the table, wrote two pages of notes and threw the rest in the trash. The space comes back, and the detail goes into the trash with everything else.

![As the conversation grows, the window fills up; in compaction, the old messages become a short summary and the recent ones stay whole.](assets/figures/en/ch02-compactacao.png){#fig-ch02-compactacao fig-alt="Three horizontal bars, one under the other, representing the context window over time. In the first, a few messages and lots of free space. In the second, the bar is almost full of old and recent messages, touching a dashed red line that marks the limit. An arrow labeled compaction leads to the third, where the old messages were replaced by a small block labeled summary, followed by the same recent messages, with free space left over."}

### What Goes First

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
In a compaction, what goes first is the beginning of the conversation.

Imagine that, right at the start of the session, you spent half an hour with the agent exploring how to organize the project. You weighed three alternatives, dropped two, and settled on one for a specific reason.

Fifty messages and two compactions later, that whole discussion has become one line of summary. If you ask "why did we decide not to use that database again?," the agent doesn't know. Worse: it may make up a plausible reason, because that's what a text generator does when information is missing.

That's exactly the experience of anyone who uses these tools for hours on end. The agent "forgets" decisions from three hours ago, goes back to suggesting the approach you'd already ruled out, repeats the mistake you corrected in the morning. The sheet where that was written got summarized until it disappeared.

> What leaves the desk stops existing for the agent. And what leaves is decided by the pressure for space, with no idea of what mattered to you.

### What the Leak Showed

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
The leaked code showed how much effort Anthropic had put into exactly this point. The Claude Code of that March had more than one layer of compaction. One of them, the lighter one, wiped the content of old command results when the conversation sat idle for more than an hour, replacing each one with a notice that an old result had been there and was removed.

The main summary followed a nine-section script: the original request, the technical concepts, the files touched, the errors and how they were fixed, the pending tasks, what was being done at that very moment. And one section asked, literally, for a list of every message the user had written.

I find that choice revealing. Of everything that can be summarized, Anthropic decided that what you said in your own words is the one thing that can't be.

The competitors did it differently: Codex asked for a short handoff note, written for another model to pick up the task, and OpenCode kept the most recent messages intact while updating a single summary of the older ones. The details change with every version. The principle is the same in all of them: to make it fit, someone has to decide what to throw away.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The code also showed that not even Anthropic always got it right. A comment left in the code admitted that 1,279 sessions had had 50 or more consecutive compaction failures, one of them reaching 3,272, wasting some 250,000 calls to the model per day worldwide. The fix was three lines: after three consecutive failures, stop trying.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
And there were more limits the user never saw. According to the findings that circulated that week, Claude Code read at most 2,000 lines of each file and cut off any tool result longer than 50,000 characters, silently [@iamfakeguru2026claude]. If you asked it to analyze a 5,000-line file, it may well have read less than half and answered with all the confidence in the world.

Anyone who thinks the agent "saw everything" is picturing a bigger desk than the one that exists.

## Memory Is a Text File

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
Compaction solves the problem within a session. In the next session, the desk starts out empty. To remember anything from the week before, the agent needs something off the desk. That was the part of the leak that caught my attention most, because it deflates a lot of the fantasy around what an AI's "memory" is.

Claude Code's [agent memory]{.gls key=memoria-de-agente} was text files. Ordinary files, on disk, the kind you open in Notepad. Organized in three layers [@franzen2026claude]:

- a short index, called MEMORY.md, always placed on the desk, with a line of about 150 characters for each subject, saying where to find each thing;
- topic files, with the actual knowledge, which only went onto the desk when the agent decided it needed them;
- the raw transcripts of old conversations, which never came back to the desk whole and were only searched by keyword for a specific name or term.

![The three layers of Claude Code's memory, according to the leaked code: an index always on the desk, topic files fetched when needed, and old transcripts that are only searched.](assets/figures/en/ch02-memoria-em-camadas.png){#fig-ch02-memoria-em-camadas fig-alt="Diagram with three stacked levels. At the top, inside the context window, a card labeled index, always loaded. In the middle, several folders labeled topic files, with an arrow to the window indicating they come in on demand; beside them, a moon represents the dream, the background consolidation that rewrites those files. At the bottom, a large stack of old transcripts with a magnifying glass, only searched by keyword; a dashed arrow indicates that only the matching snippet reaches the window."}

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The index had a discipline rule: write the topic file first, update the index only after. The index never became a dumping ground for facts. If it did, it would take up more and more space on the desk, permanently, and the whole system would get worse. The code kept that index under about 200 lines.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
And then there was the dream, literally: a process called autoDream, still switched off in the public version, that would run in the background while you weren't using the tool, rereading the notes, swapping relative dates for absolute ones and deleting whatever had been contradicted later [@deeplearningai2026kairos].

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
The name recalls the brain consolidating memory during sleep, but the result is far less mystical: a program rereading text files and rewriting text files. The details of how it would work are in @sec-aptecnico-autodream.

### A Note Is a Hint

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
The detail I found most elegant in the entire leak was this one: the system treated its own memory as a hint. The code started from the assumption that a note could be stale, wrong or contradicted by something that happened later, and instructed the model to check against the actual project before trusting it [@franzen2026claude].

It's the behavior you'd expect from a serious professional. You write down that the client prefers blue, but before painting the wall you ask again.

<!-- src: 2026/06/16/ai-memory-memoria-longo-prazo-karpathy-wiki-auto-aprendizado-hermes-projetos -->
And it makes perfect sense, because bad memory is worse than no memory. With no memory, the agent asks. With bad memory, it acts with false certainty.

A system that automatically wrote down a "rule" every time something went wrong would turn, within a week, into a graveyard of superstition: never use such-and-such tool, always run such-and-such command, that library is broken. And many of those failures were temporary: an expired password, the internet down, a bug fixed five minutes later.

### What This Means for You

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
Strip away the technical layers and you're left with a conclusion that holds for any AI tool, including the chat you use on your phone. When the product says it "remembers you," it hasn't stored you anywhere inside the model. It stored a note about you, in text, and the app puts that note on the desk at the start of every conversation.

> AI doesn't remember you. It reads a note about you, which someone wrote, which may be wrong and which takes up space on the desk.

That has practical consequences, and several of them are good news.

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
The first is that memory doesn't lock you into a vendor. If all memory is text, you can take it to another tool. I do this often: I ask Claude Code to write a document with the state of the project, the decisions made and the next steps, close it, open another agent and ask it to read the document and carry on. It works, and the loss is the same an internal compaction would have caused.

The second is that you can read the note. If a tool has memory, look for where it shows what it stored. Read it, correct it, delete whatever is wrong.

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
The third is that you can write your own notes, and I think this one matters most. For months I've kept, in the projects that matter, a documentation folder with short files by subject: architecture, decisions, known gotchas, weird settings.

When something important happens in a session, I ask: save this to the decisions file. In the next session, or after a compaction, the agent rereads it. The cost is discipline: the agent won't do it on its own, and I have to remember to ask.

### The Wiki That Doesn't Die

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
In April 2026, the researcher Andrej Karpathy published an idea that gives this habit a name and a method, which he called LLM Wiki [@karpathy2026llmwiki]. Instead of treating knowledge as something to be fetched again with every question, the model maintains a wiki in text files, with one page per concept, pages linked to each other, contradictions flagged.

When new information arrives, the model updates the affected pages. Every so often, it does a review looking for contradictions and outdated claims.

The advantage is that knowledge compounds. The first time you discover that a library has a timezone gotcha, it becomes a page. The second time the subject comes up, the agent reads the page instead of rediscovering the problem from scratch.

Wikis made by humans usually die because nobody can stand the boring work of keeping them up to date. Karpathy's bet is that there's finally a machine that's good at exactly that boring work.

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
In May 2026 I tested a third-party tool that promised to automate all of this, gave up on it within a week over architectural defects, and ended up writing my own, ai-memory [@aimemory2026site]. The story of how it grew and what it taught me about owning your own memory is told in @sec-ch20, and what it does and how to start using it are in @sec-apmemoria.

<!-- src: 2026/06/16/ai-memory-memoria-longo-prazo-karpathy-wiki-auto-aprendizado-hermes-projetos -->
The point here is simpler, and no salesman will tell you: the best memory for agents I know of is a well-kept folder of text files. And be wary of any product that promises the AI "learns on its own." What really exists, when it works, is a program that reviews sessions, proposes notes, checks whether they make sense and leaves a trail of what changed.

## The Vector Database Craze

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
The leak interested me for a second reason, tied to one of the biggest crazes in AI between 2023 and 2025. In 2022, when the desk held 4,000 tokens, not even one chapter of your company's manual fit. The solution that became standard is called [RAG]{.gls key=rag}, retrieval-augmented generation: a separate system searches for the relevant passages and puts only those on the desk. It became an industry, and the piece everybody sold you was a [vector database]{.gls key=banco-vetorial}, which stores each passage as a list of numbers, the [embedding]{.gls}, and finds the ones with a similar meaning.

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
Claude Code's memory had none. The company that sells one of the most expensive models on the market, with the money to build whatever infrastructure it wanted, chose [lexical search]{.gls key=busca-lexical}, search by literal word, and a generous desk. Once the desk could hold an entire novel, the one who sorts out what matters became the model itself, actually reading.

> When the desk was small, the librarian had to be a genius. With a huge desk, a hardworking librarian and an attentive reader will do.

<!-- src: 2026/04/06/rag-esta-morto-contexto-longo -->
RAG isn't dead: gigantic collections, images and audio, and answers in fractions of a second still call for one. But in 2023, anyone who wanted to do anything serious with AI and documents was told they needed a vector database, and in 2026 one of the most popular AI coding tools on the market lived without one.

The mandatory piece of one era became optional in the next, in under three years. Remember that pattern; it will come back many times in this book, always with a new product to sell you. The technical details, with the flaws of vector search, the cost math and what the research says, are in @sec-aptecnico-rag.

## What Stays With You

<!-- src: 2026/06/16/ai-memory-memoria-longo-prazo-karpathy-wiki-auto-aprendizado-hermes-projetos -->
Anyone who works with an agent for hours on end knows the cycle. You start the session, explain the project, show the important files, correct a hallucination, find a trap, decide on an architecture. After two hours, the agent is "warmed up": it knows what not to do, it knows where the holes are, it knows that one weird function exists for a reason.

Then the session ends. The next day you open another one, and it all starts over. "No, this project uses that." "No, we already tried that route." You've become the agent's memory.

<!-- src: original -->
You don't need to be a programmer to go through this. Anyone who has had a long conversation with a chatbot about a contract, a medical diagnosis or planning a trip has seen the answer at hour ten contradict what was agreed at hour one. Now you know why. Knowing why, you can defend yourself.

<!-- src: 2026/05/18/como-memoria-de-agentes-funciona-e-como-melhorar -->
What I do, and recommend, works with any tool:

- repeat what matters, instead of assuming something agreed an hour ago is still on the desk;
- when the conversation gets long and starts to slip, ask for a summary of what was decided, check it, and start a new conversation from it;
- keep important decisions somewhere of your own, in a file, in a document, outside the tool;
- if the product has a "memory" feature, go read what it stored, and delete whatever is wrong;
- be wary of any promise that the AI "learns from you" or "learns on its own"; at best, it's someone writing notes on its behalf.

> Your memory and your notes are yours. No model is going to keep them for you.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/codigo-fonte-do-claude-code-vazou-o-que-achamos-dentro -->
There's an irony at the end of this story. The leak that showed the world such a careful memory system happened because the publishing process was missing the instruction to leave the source maps out of the package. All it took was one line in a configuration file, or an option in the packaging tool, or a five-line automated check before publishing. The company taught the machine to distrust its own memory and forgot to check its own suitcase before checking it in.

<!-- src: 2026/05/04/llm-benchmarks-deepseek-unlocked-deepclaude -->
The leak made something else clear to anyone who read the code. A good part of what makes Claude Code seem intelligent lives in the program around the model: the hidden instructions, the tools, the compaction, the memory in files, the loop that decides what to do next.

In May 2026, that stopped being a hunch. I watched a Chinese model, DeepSeek V4 Pro, fail to even finish my programming test inside one tool. I put the same model, without changing a thing in it, inside another program, and it jumped to the elite of my ranking. What that surrounding program is, and why it weighs so much, is what we open up next.
