# The End of the Marathon {#sec-ch10}

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
In mid-May 2026 I realized the hyperfocus was over. I had started ramping up in late January and only stopped around May, project after project, to answer a question that seemed simple: can you code 100% of the time with the coding [agents]{.gls key=agente} from Anthropic and OpenAI, the [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira}, the ones the big companies sell as the most advanced thing out there?

The short answer was yes. To get there, I didn't want to run a one-day demo and walk away saying "I think so." I went all the way: over 500 hours, almost half a million lines of code and documentation, 24 repositories, some public and some private.

Then, on May 14, somebody asked me a question I didn't expect. More or less this: so are all 24 projects perfect, models of code excellence?

I thought the answer was obviously no. If I believed any of them was that good, I'd be spinning up a company around it. I don't lack money to invest. If I wasn't doing that, it was because everything there had been built at hobby pace.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
But the question made sense to anyone looking from the outside. I had published the code. I had written about every project. Plenty of people assume that if someone pushes code to the internet, it's because they think it's good.

So I sat down and did what I should have done earlier: a report card. A score from 1 to 10 for each project, measuring just one thing, how stable and usable it was at that moment.

The report card answered the question you may have been carrying since @sec-ch05: when all is said and done, was it a success or a failure?

## The Report Card

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
I'll start with the detail that matters most: no project got a 10.

Two projects got an 8. The first is ai-jail [@aijail2026site], a [sandbox]{.gls} that keeps the agents from touching what they shouldn't on my computer (its story is in @sec-ch15). It was the only one really close to a stable release, and the one that drew the most outsiders: 19 code contributions closed and 23 reported problems resolved. The second is The M.Akita Chronicles, the newsletter with an AI-narrated podcast from @sec-ch06, the project I built with the most care and the one I touch most often, because it has to ship every week.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
At the bottom of the barrel, with 1s and 2s, were projects like Frank Sherlock, the photo indexer from @sec-ch07, which I used at first, until I lost interest, and which is probably full of bugs for that reason. In the middle sat everything from small programs for personal use to two-day experiments. The full list, project by project and with each one's score, stayed in the blog post.

![The marathon report card, in May 2026: no project reached 10.](assets/figures/en/ch10-boletim.png){#fig-ch10-boletim fig-alt="Horizontal bar chart with eighteen projects sorted by score from 1 to 10. Two projects at the top with 8, three with 7, four with 5, five between 3 and 4, and four between 1 and 2. A dashed line marks 10, which no bar reaches."}

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
The six missing to make 24 are private. They're notebooks Claude Code uses to administer my machines, and there's no point describing what nobody is ever going to see.

### What the Score Measures

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
Looking at the whole report card, a pattern emerges that has nothing to do with the tool.

The two projects with an 8 are the ones with users. ai-jail has outsiders using it and complaining. The Chronicles has readers waiting every week, and I'm the first to notice when something breaks. The ones at the bottom of the barrel are exactly the ones nobody uses, not even me.

And that makes sense. A project with no users will always have dozens of defects that never get fixed, because nobody has tripped over them. It's what @sec-ch07 showed up close: the version you think is "finished" is 0.1. From there come months of real people reporting problems, the fixes, the reorganizations that show up because the initial assumptions were wrong.

> A project with no users is dead. Software only gets better when real people trip over its defects.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
The rest I abandoned without guilt. If I jumped from one project to the next within a few days, it was because I lost interest.

It's what I've always recommended to anyone who wants to learn: exercise on many projects, even if most of them end up in the trash. Nobody learns by trying to build a single perfect project. I threw everything at the wall to see what stuck, and the ones that stuck pulled in contributors on their own.

## Forty Minutes and a Score of One

<!-- src: 2026/03/07/portando-10-mil-linhas-de-python-pra-crystal-com-claude-easy-subtitle -->
easy-subtitle deserves a stop, because its story sums up the whole marathon.

In March, I wanted to automate a chore for anyone who keeps a movie and TV collection: finding subtitles in the right language and syncing their timing with the video. There was already an open source project that did this, Subservient, written in Python [@subservient]. It worked, but installing and maintaining it depended on a stack of packages that could break with every system update. I wanted a single executable file I could drop on a server and forget about.

<!-- src: 2026/03/07/portando-10-mil-linhas-de-python-pra-crystal-com-claude-easy-subtitle -->
I asked Claude to study the whole project and rewrite everything in Crystal, a not-very-popular language I had picked for this kind of tool. Beforehand, I made the design decisions that mattered: swap the syncing piece for another that didn't depend on Python, use commands instead of menus, change the configuration format.

Five commits. Less than 40 minutes from the first to the last.

The original had about 10,000 lines squeezed into six enormous files and not a single [automated test]{.gls key=teste-automatizado}. The new version came out with 2,500 lines, organized into more than 40 small files, 76 tests, automatic releases for Linux and macOS, and an executable of about 6 MB that needs nothing else installed.

<!-- src: 2026/03/07/portando-10-mil-linhas-de-python-pra-crystal-com-claude-easy-subtitle -->
That made me revisit an old opinion. I always said that rewriting a program in a new language just for the love of the language was a waste of time. When the cost drops from weeks to under an hour, the math changes. I wrote that in March, all fired up.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
Two months later, on the report card, easy-subtitle got a 1. Started it, lost interest, never stopped to really test it. Just pushed it and forgot.

Both things are true at once. The 40-minute rewrite happened, and it's impressive. And the result sat in a corner, with nobody around to find out whether it worked with somebody's actual movies.

The May report card had one more piece of information that counts against the project.

Of the languages and tools I tested, the models moved most freely with Ruby on Rails, a popular technology full of conventions that they saw by the truckload in training. With Flutter, a tool for building apps, they moved forward in fits and starts. With Tauri, another one of the same kind, it was rough. With Crystal it was really rough.

> Forty minutes to write it says nothing about whether the program is any good. That only shows up with time, users and review.

## Slop

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
The hardest lesson of the marathon fits in one sentence: days of sixteen hours of [vibe coding]{.gls key=vibe-coding}, week after week, always produce [slop]{.gls}.

Slop is what the internet calls AI-generated content made in bulk and without care, which takes up space and isn't good for much. In code, it's that house of cards from @sec-ch05: it works in the demo and collapses when somebody touches it.

There's no way to avoid it by forcing volume. The only remedy I know is to review by hand, each batch of changes, one by one.

Why didn't I do that on all of them? Because nobody was paying me. I really reviewed two or three of the 24. On the rest, I just wanted to test the concept and move on to the next.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
I had already described the right way with the Chronicles, and jokingly called it Agile Vibe Coding. That's the sarcastic way of saying: use [XP]{.gls key=xp} and normal software engineering techniques. Small steps, tests, review, continuous integration ([CI]{.gls key=ci}), monitoring. None of that changed with AI (@sec-ch06 shows how it went in practice).

### Five Times, Maybe Ten

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
How much faster did it make me? My estimate, in May 2026, on average across the marathon, came to about five times. Ten, at most, if you count the slop along for the ride, which I don't recommend. That's less than the ads promise.

<!-- src: original -->
The book has already shown you other multipliers. They don't fight each other, because each one measures something different:

| What was measured | Number | What kind of number |
|---|---|---|
| FrankMD, a single project (@sec-ch05) | six to seven times | my guess: 30 hours against the roughly 200 I imagine without AI |
| Marathon average, February to May | about five times | my estimate |
| Chronicles newsletter (@sec-ch06) | 30 team-weeks against 8 days | comparison with an imagined team |
| METR study (@sec-ch20) | 19% slower | controlled study, with experienced programmers on code they had known for years |

Only the last row has a control group. My reading: the gain shows up on a new project, with someone experienced checking every step; on old code the person already masters, it can vanish or turn into a loss.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
There was one practical recommendation that works as a snapshot of the period. In May, what worked for me was using Codex or Claude Code inside the companies' own monthly subscriptions, which came out much cheaper than paying per [token]{.gls}, request by request. You could switch to cheaper models, like Kimi K2.6 or DeepSeek V4 Pro. But my projects that scored below 5 would have scored below 2 with them.

Prices and model names age fast, and Part III deals with them in numbers.

### Success or Failure

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
Which brings me to the answer to the question in the title. Success or failure?

Success, on what I wanted to measure. Frontier agents can keep up with a programmer working with them full time, for months, across very different languages and kinds of project. That's what I had set out to test.

Failure, if the question is whether they make good software on their own. Most of what I built was disposable proof of concept, and it came out with disposable proof-of-concept quality. AI helped me get through those experiments faster, even if half-baked. Fine by me: without it I'd never have started, and now I can cross them off the list and move on.

The conclusion from the Chronicles week, in @sec-ch06, now had three months of evidence behind it: AI speeds up what you were already doing, carelessness included, and that's why it doesn't replace a good programmer.

<!-- src: 2026/05/14/terminando-maratona-ia-sucesso-ou-fracasso -->
What decides it is the objective. Everything I built during the marathon could go in the trash without hurting anyone. If your objective is to build a business and charge people, you have to take ten times more care than I did.

And that brings me to the other half of the conclusion, the one I had written two months earlier, in the middle of the marathon, and which is about money.

## The Moat Ran Dry

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
On March 5, 2026, after 37 days straight, I stopped to tally things up partway. Between January 27 and that date, there had been 653 commits and about 144,000 lines of code across 8 published projects, not counting the history of the newsletter repository, which is closed and on its own was already past 300 commits. The May count, with 24 repositories, came later and swallowed this one.

In March I was more excited than in May. I wrote that those projects were real software, with tests and automated deploys. Some of them were.

The May report card showed that most of them were experiments. But the conclusion I drew in March survived the report card, and it's about business.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
Things that used to take weeks or months for an experienced developer were taking days. A complete Markdown editor in less than a week. The first version of a Mega clone in one. A data analysis system with dozens of commands in three.

And it wasn't just me. In February 2026, Cloudflare told how a single engineer, using Claude as the main tool, had reimplemented the programming interface of Next.js, one of the most widely used foundations for building websites, on top of another tool, in less than a week [@cloudflare2026vinext]. Cost: about $1,100 in tokens, over more than 800 sessions, with more than 2,000 automated tests. There was debate over whether the reimplementation was complete, but the core point stood: a job for a whole team, over months, became a job for one person, in days.

### The Death of the Easy Startup

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
For years, the script for a new tech company went more or less like this. Someone has an idea, puts together a small team, spends three to six months building the first version of the product, raises money from investors and tries to scale. The barrier to entry was the cost of building: programmers are expensive, development is slow, and whoever gets there first has the edge.

That script broke. If I can build the first working version of a Mega clone in a day, what's the first version of a cloud file storage startup worth? A service that charges R$ 500 a month (around $90) to monitor social media is competing with something I built in three days as a side project.

<!-- src: original -->
Operations, support and the trust of people who store files with a service like that are still expensive, and my clone needed 22 security fixes in its first audit (@sec-ch07). What got cheaper was the first version, precisely the one that used to give whoever got there first the edge.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
Every entrepreneur who shows up saying "it's like Uber, but for..." needs to stop and rethink. When any competent developer with a good agent can copy the first version of your product in a week, the idea alone isn't worth anything anymore. Execution got too cheap.

In business, a [moat]{.gls key=fosso} is whatever protects a company from competitors, like the water-filled ditch around a castle. For decades, code was a moat: it was expensive and slow to write, and copying it was too. In 2026 that moat ran dry: when everyone can build the same thing in a week, the code no longer protects whoever wrote it.

### What the Agent Didn't Do for Me

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
So what does protect you? The best answer I found was inside one of my own projects.

Frank Yomik, the one from @sec-ch08, reads manga pages in Japanese and overlays the reading or the translation of the text. The whole pipeline works: find the speech bubbles, read the text inside them, translate, draw on top. But the most important piece of the system wasn't written by me. It's a small model, trained by someone else on some 11,000 labeled comic images, that learned to find speech bubbles [@ogkalu2025bubble].

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
I didn't train that model. I couldn't, at least not easily. Collecting thousands of varied pages, labeling every bubble on every one by hand, configuring the training and running it on the right hardware is work of a different nature. Vibe coding doesn't solve that.

And that's a small model, one you can train in hours on a single [GPU]{.gls key=gpu}, the processing card that does AI's heavy lifting. Move up to frontier models and the bill explodes. Epoch AI, an institute that tracks these numbers, calculated that the cost of hardware and energy to train the largest models had been growing about 2.4 times a year since 2016 [@cottier2024rising]. That's why only a handful of giant companies make those models.

![In 2026 the cost of building software collapsed, while the cost of training models and gathering exclusive data shot up.](assets/figures/en/ch10-fosso.png){#fig-ch10-fosso fig-alt="Conceptual chart, without numbers, with two curves over time. One, dashed, labeled 'cost of writing code', drops almost to the floor; at its end, a small castle with a dry moat. The other, solid, labeled 'cost of training models and gathering exclusive data', climbs ever more steeply and ends near a castle whose moat is full of water."}

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
The question for anyone starting a business has changed. It used to be "can I build this software?," and now the answer is almost always yes, fast and cheap. The new one is: do I have data nobody else has, and do I know how to turn that data into something useful and hard to copy?

Public internet data is reaching the limit of what can be used to train models, as Epoch AI itself has been documenting [@villalobos2024data]. Whoever has exclusive data (medical, financial, industrial, from sensors) has something vibe coding can't replicate. And even that advantage doesn't last long: another competitor gathers similar data and trains a rival model in months.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/37-dias-de-imersão-em-vibe-coding-conclusão-quanto-a-modelos-de-negócio -->
The garage still works. But what comes out of it can no longer be just an app.

<!-- src: original -->
What this means for a company that already exists, with employees, processes and AI licenses already bought, is another conversation, and it's saved for @sec-ch19. Here I care about the individual. And for the individual, the same drop in cost that dried up the startups' moat opened a door I didn't expect.

## The Economics of Small Itches

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
Everybody has a list of small daily irritations they swallow in silence: the program that almost does what you want, the screen that shows the wrong information, the repetitive chore every week. We swallow them because fixing them would cost a whole weekend, and none of those itches is worth a weekend.

With agents, that math flipped. The cost of turning an irritation into a tool dropped so much that today it's worth fixing almost everything. Once the marathon was over, that's what I did most.

![Before agents, only irritations that cost more than a weekend of work were worth fixing. After, the line came down.](assets/figures/en/ch10-limiar.png){#fig-ch10-limiar fig-alt="Two side-by-side panels with the same points, each a small everyday irritation (clock, email, song lyrics, typing, kanji in manga, investments and others), on a vertical scale of annoyance. In the 'before' panel, a high line labeled 'costs a weekend' leaves almost every point below it, hollow: they go unfixed. In the 'after' panel, the line drops and is labeled 'with an agent', and almost every point sits above it, filled: worth fixing."}

### A Clock, an Email Client, a Song

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
I have a second monitor, turned vertical, next to the main one. I wanted a big desk clock there. I found an old little terminal clock program, open source and abandoned for ages, and made a [fork]{.gls}, a copy I now maintain on my own.

A whole screen just to show the time was a waste. So I had the agent add panels around the clock: one shows my appointments for the day, pulled from my calendar; another lists the contributions and problems waiting for an answer in my projects. Later someone commented that the look resembled Evangelion, and I spent a day adding two color themes inspired by the show. Challenge accepted.

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
For email, I picked Geary, a Linux program, because it's the closest to Apple's Mail. It had two defects for me.

The folder column didn't collapse, which on the vertical monitor was a squeeze. And the recipient field practically never autocompleted names, so I had to type the whole address by hand. In 2026.

My first solution was to leave the program alone and inject two small modules from the outside, one for each problem.

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
In June I defended that approach with conviction. Keeping your own copy of a big application is debt: you blink and spend the rest of your life resolving conflicts between your version and the original.

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
By July I had given up. The modules worked, but they required recompiling all of Geary on every system update. I made the fork, Frank Geary, with both fixes straight in the code, and started tracking the original's changes. With the agent, switching strategies midway came cheap.

<!-- src: 2026/07/12/usando-ia-pra-resolver-meus-probleminhas-do-dia-a-dia -->
The list goes on:

- Frank Lyrics, a browser extension that repeats exactly one line of a song on YouTube, so I can memorize an anime opening without hunting for the right spot on the timeline;
- Frank Type, typing practice with excerpts from public-domain books, because I hate typing random words and I hate waiting for other players;
- a version of Frank Yomik that overlays furigana, the small reading guide (see @sec-ch08), on top of the hard kanji in manga for adults that don't print it, all running on my own computer, over the page I already bought.

### Who Fights the Compiler

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
A curious change came along. When I wrote everything by hand, for a little program like this I'd pick a comfortable language, like Python or Go. I started picking Rust, which produces fast, safe programs but is famous for fighting the programmer all the time, rejecting code over memory rules that cause headaches.

Now the model is the one fighting those rules. I stay in the role of reviewer and end user. And the models of that June were surprisingly competent in Rust, as long as I made them run the tests, fix the warnings, and didn't accept the first version as if Moses had brought it down from the mountain.

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
There was also a program I'll never publish: a [TUI]{.gls key=tui}, a full-screen application inside the terminal, to track my investment account in the United States. It was born glued to my spreadsheets, my statements and my hacks. It's the purest example of the category: software made for a single user, which no company would ever build, because its market is me.

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
The process became a sequence of small, supervised tasks. I describe the itch, create the project, ask for a prototype, test it, ask for a reorganization, ask for tests, ask for packaging. If it sucks, I throw it away. If it works, I install it and use it.

It still takes work, but it's supervision and finishing work, without the grunt work of writing boilerplate.

### The Bar Doesn't Move

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
There's a catch, and it's the same one as in the whole chapter. I don't let these little programs turn into duct tape dumped in some corner of the disk. They have tests, they're built automatically, many have published releases and install through the system's package manager, and they keep their configuration in the right place. They can be upgraded, removed, used and contributed to by someone else.

That's the same discipline as the Chronicles, pocket-sized. The small itches got cheap to scratch, and they still demand care.

<!-- src: 2026/06/08/brincando-tui-llms-ratatui-bubbletea -->
And there's one thing no report card measures. Building little tools for yourself is the fun part of programming, the part that had gone missing for a while. With agents, it's back.

> Let the model carry the boring weight and use your brain to decide what's worth keeping.

## What the Race Leaves Behind

<!-- src: original -->
Tying the threads together, the conclusion I take from the marathon is a personal one.

If you're an experienced programmer, by my count, AI makes you about five times faster and gives you enough rope to hang yourself ten times faster. The difference between the two is the discipline you already had before it.

If you're not a programmer, what this marathon has for you is the itches. For fixing an annoyance of your own, at home, in programs only you will use, it has never been this cheap to try. For selling software to other people, code got too cheap to be what sets you apart, and review by someone experienced is still mandatory (@sec-ch05).

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/20-anos-de-blog-o-ano-em-que-a-ia-finalmente-me-deixou-traduzir-tudo -->
And there was one job, in the middle of all this, that didn't make the report card because it wasn't a code project at all. In April 2026, while the marathon was still running, my blog turned twenty. I had owed it something, hundreds of posts that only existed in Portuguese and that I always promised to translate, and I collected on a Monday in April.
