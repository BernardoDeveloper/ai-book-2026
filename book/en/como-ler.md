# How to Read This Book {#sec-como-ler .unnumbered}

<!-- src: original -->
The best way to read this book is in order, front to back. It tells the story of February through October 2026, the months in which I put artificial intelligence to the test almost every day, and the story has a sequence. First what this thing is, then what it did in my hands, how to measure it, where it's dangerous and what all of it means for your work. A concept explained early on gets used without ceremony later.

But I know plenty of people arrive with one specific worry and little patience to wait the whole book for the answer. That's fine. Each part answers a question I heard a lot, and you can jump straight to it.

## If You're in a Hurry

<!-- src: original -->
If you arrived with a specific question, these are the chapters that answer it. Each one can be read on its own.

- What about my job? @sec-ch18 and @sec-ch19b; for changes at your company, @sec-ch19; for next steps, the epilogue (@sec-epilogo). The three bubbles in @sec-apbolhas show the pattern of other crises.
- Is anybody in there? Is a superintelligence coming? @sec-ch01, @sec-ch14 and @sec-ch20.
- Are you a manager being sold an AI transformation? @sec-ch14, @sec-ch19 and the checklists in @sec-apC.
- Is it safe to let AI touch your stuff? Is it spying on me? @sec-ch15, @sec-ch17 and the bare-minimum kit in @sec-apB.
- Can you build one of these AIs at home, or is it genius territory? @sec-ch13b, with the model I trained from scratch on a graphics card, and then @sec-apestudar.
- Just curious, or in the mood for good stories? @sec-ch01, @sec-ch05, @sec-ch15 and the two interludes (@sec-interlude1 and @sec-interlude2).

<!-- src: original -->
If you program, the behind-the-scenes material I took out of the chapters so as not to tire the readers who don't is in an appendix of its own (@sec-aptecnico): step-by-step debugging, hardware math, library names and the mechanics of the tests.

<!-- src: original -->
Between the parts there are two interludes, shorter and lighter, to catch your breath. The appendices, at the back, hold whatever would get in the way of the story:

- an optional tour of the machine's mechanics, with no math, and a short list of books and videos for anyone who wants to learn the mechanics for real (@sec-apleituras);
- a short guide to studying with AI at your side (@sec-apestudar) and the three bubbles I lived through, side by side and with the numbers for each (@sec-apbolhas);
- a timeline, from the 2017 transformer to October 2026;
- a bare-minimum kit for using AI agents safely;
- the memory tool for agents that I built over the year;
- two short checklists for sizing up a benchmark or an ad;
- the technical behind-the-scenes, for those who program.

If you skip around, you'll trip now and then over a term or a character that showed up earlier. The text points to the chapter where it was explained, and the glossary covers the rest.

## A Snapshot of October 2026

<!-- src: original -->
This book was closed in October 2026. The story takes place between February and October of that year, and the dates are spelled out on purpose: "in March 2026," never "recently."

<!-- src: original -->
The names of models and products will age fast. By the time you read this, Claude Opus 4.5, GPT-5.5 or Kimi K3 will probably sound like the car models in an old novel: they tell you when the story is set, and nobody reads the novel to pick a car. The mechanisms underneath change much more slowly. How a language model generates text, why it forgets what the two of you agreed on, why it agrees with you, why an unsupervised agent screws things up: that's what I want you to take away.

<!-- src: original -->
Numbers deserve the same care. Price, benchmark score, amount of memory, position in a ranking: each one is a snapshot taken in a specific month, and the text says which. A score from September 2026 tells you what happened in September 2026, and that's it.

## Glossary, References and Sources

<!-- src: original -->
Every technical term is explained in one plain sentence the first time it shows up. To help anyone who skips chapters or simply forgets, technical terms are marked with a dotted underline, like [token]{.gls}. The mark leads to the Glossary (@sec-glossario), at the back of the book, where each term gets a short definition in everyday English.

<!-- src: original -->
Acronyms are spelled out the first time they appear in the book, and after that it's just the acronym. Most come from English, because that's how the field talks; the glossary spells them out.

<!-- src: original -->
When I state a fact that didn't come out of my own experience, like a date, a figure from a study or something an executive said, it comes with author and year in parentheses. That points to the References (@sec-bibliografia), at the back of the book, with the title, date and address of each source. The whole book repeats one piece of advice that applies to me too: check the source instead of trusting whoever is talking.

<!-- src: original -->
This book grew out of articles I published on my blog during 2026. I rewrote everything for people who don't program, cut code and repetition, and merged pieces that covered the same subject in different weeks. If you want to read the original version of a chapter, with the context and date of each piece, the Sources appendix (@sec-fontes) lists the original articles, chapter by chapter.

<!-- src: original -->
The diagrams were drawn for the book. Some illustrations were generated by AI under my direction, and each one is identified in the Image Credits (@sec-creditos), along with the credits for third-party photos. A book about AI that hid where it used AI would be doing exactly what it criticizes.

<!-- src: original -->
One more warning before the first chapter. There's a part of the subject I left out on purpose, and you deserve to know which one before you start.
