# The New Punch Card {#sec-ch18}

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
On Saturday, April 11, 2026, I had maxed out everything. I'd burned through the entire quota of Anthropic's most expensive Claude Code plan, the Max 20x, and hit the extra-usage ceiling on top of it. I moved over to OpenAI's Codex, burned through that plan too, and upgraded to Pro so I could keep working through the weekend.

By then Claude Code had become my terminal. The code editor, where I'd spent decades typing programs, had moved into the background. I described what I wanted, the [agent]{.gls key=agente} combed through the project, edited twenty files, ran the tests, fixed whatever broke and handed me back a proposed change in minutes. My job was to read it and decide whether the direction was right.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
I had the LGP-21 on my mind, a 1963 computer, small by the standards of its day [@wikipedia2026lgp30]. Programming that machine meant typing the program in binary, zeros and ones, on a typewriter wired to the computer, instruction by instruction, with one eye on the numbers in the register. Then you flipped a lever to execute, and the result came back typed onto paper.

The metric on that machine was characters per minute. A calculation any app does today in the blink of an eye took hours of human work: type, check, test, check again.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
Watching the agent work, I felt the same distance, seen from the other side. We're living through one of those transitions in slow motion, and a lot of people don't want to see it.

And that same week, once again, someone asked me whether juniors would stop learning to program now that the machine writes the code. It's the version of the question you may have brought to this book, the one Part V tries to answer: is this thing going to take my job?

My reaction was the usual one. The question confuses two things: getting code into the machine, and software engineering. Those were never the same thing.

## One Interface After Another

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
There was a time when programming meant converting numbers to binary in your head and jamming instructions straight into a memory address, bit by bit. The Altair 8800, from 1975, was still programmed that way: a panel with rows of little switches, one per bit, that you flipped up and down to build each instruction [@wikipedia2026altair].

There was a time when programming meant organizing a deck of [punch cards]{.gls key=cartao-perfurado}. Each card was one line of the program, encoded in holes. You had to know the right order of the deck, know that one bad hole wrecked the whole run, and debug the mistake by staring at the cards, without the modern luxury of a backspace key.

![An IBM card punch operator at the 1950 U.S. census: the interface between paper and machine. Photo: U.S. Census Bureau / public domain.](assets/figures/ch18-cartao-perfurado.jpg){#fig-ch18-cartao-perfurado fig-alt="A woman operates an IBM card punch during the 1950 US census." fig-desc="Black-and-white historical photograph of a woman seated at an IBM card punch machine, typing from a sheet of paper, with punch cards in the machine."}

<!-- src: original -->
The cards predate computers. IBM introduced the 80-column card in 1928; it served the tabulating machines of businesses and governments first and later became the front door to computers [@ibm2026punchcard]. For decades, transcribing programs and data from paper onto cards, at a keypunch machine, was office work, done mostly by women [@chm2026dataentry].

That profession vanished, and it's worth saying what that meant for the people in it. The task of punching ceased to exist, and anyone who knew only how to do that was left without it. Those who managed to climb to the next layer (operating the computer, checking and organizing the data, programming) kept working. Programming, which depended on the cards, only grew.

The analogy in this chapter cuts both ways, and I'll come back to it later, when I talk about the other professions.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
Then came the era when real programming meant knowing the assembly language of processors like the 6502 and the Z80, [Assembly]{.gls key=assembly}, because computers had so little memory that every byte mattered. Then better [compilers]{.gls key=compilador} arrived, programs that translate a language closer to human speech into the machine's instructions. C showed up, machines got more powerful, and Assembly stopped being the center of everything and became a niche tool.

I don't know a serious programmer who looked at that transition and said programming was over because the compiler now wrote the machine instructions for you.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
The 21st century brought the web, and an entire generation got shoved into HTML, CSS and a pile of markup bureaucracy that adds little intellectual value and demands a ton of manual labor just to get it mostly right. I think the industry dragged that model out way too long. For years, a lot of programmers became glorified form operators, CRUD assemblers, people who line up little boxes on a screen.

![Every era changed the way instructions reach the machine; the work of deciding what the machine should do stayed.](assets/figures/en/ch18-interfaces.png){#fig-ch18-interfaces fig-alt="Interface evolution: panel switches, punch cards, Assembly, compilers, editor and AI agents." fig-desc="Six panels in a row, linked by arrows: panel switches, punch cards, Assembly, compilers, code editor and AI agents, the last one highlighted. Above each panel, what the person needed to know how to do: flip each bit, order the deck, save every byte, convince the compiler, stitch together dozens of layers, orchestrate an agent. Below, a continuous band running through every panel: what stays: judgment, fundamentals, domain knowledge."}

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
Notice the pattern:

- first you needed to know how to type the instruction;
- then, how to order the deck of cards;
- then, how to convince the compiler;
- then, how to stitch together frameworks, HTML, CSS, containers, cloud, databases and fifty other layers of junk;
- now, how to orchestrate an agent.

At every step the interface changed, and the machine kept demanding precision.

### VS Code Is the New Punch Card

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
VS Code is the most popular [code editor]{.gls key=editor-de-codigo} among programmers: the program where we type code, line by line. When I say it's the new punch card, I don't mean it got bad. Punch cards weren't bad in their own context either. They were a massive step up from typing bits by hand.

The card was its era's mechanism for feeding instructions into the machine. The text editor is becoming the same thing: a mechanism that still works, will be around for a long time, and is no longer the center of the activity.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
For years the industry sold the fantasy that programming meant learning the framework of the month. Now it's going to sell the fantasy that programming means learning [prompts]{.gls key=prompt}.

> Prompts are an interface. Frameworks are an interface. Editors are an interface. Punch cards were an interface. Programming is still the act of instructing a machine to compute something useful under real constraints.

### What Really Changed in 2025

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
[LLMs]{.gls key=llm} went mainstream at the end of 2022, with ChatGPT [@openai2022chatgpt]. Mainstream, though, is not the same thing as useful for serious work. Through 2023 and 2024 I was already using AI to write code, and it worked, but with plenty of nonsense: too many hallucinations, context getting lost, tooling breaking, too much cost for too little reliability. It was useful for experienced programmers who knew how to keep the thing on a leash.

For me, the turn came at the end of 2025. Better models showed up together with [prompt caching]{.gls key=prompt-caching}, reliable [tool calling]{.gls key=tool-calling} (the model's ability to call tools) and, above all, real agent programs wrapped around the model, the [harness]{.gls} from @sec-ch03. The thing stopped feeling like conference-demo theater and started feeling like a work tool.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
The big gain shows up when you stop treating the agent like autocomplete on steroids and start treating it like a pair programmer, the [pair programming]{.gls key=pair-programming} from @sec-ch06. You move up a level: instead of a typist, you act as tech lead, product owner and QA at the same time. You define the intent, explain the context, ask for a plan, have it run the tests, ask it to review its own change.

There's a balance there. Letting go of the wheel and letting the agent ship everything blind is outsourcing responsibility. Turning into a comma cop, nitpicking every detail, strangles productivity. The right point is the one Part II showed: [XP]{.gls key=xp}, tests, [continuous integration]{.gls key=ci}, small changes and review.

## The Bubble That Popped First

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
The Akitando series, on my YouTube channel, ran from 2018 to the end of 2023 [@akita2024akitando]. It had one declared purpose: to help prepare real programmers to survive the popping of a hiring [bubble]{.gls key=bolha} that had been inflating since 2010, with interest rates near zero.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In those years, programming became "the profession of the future," and it looked easy. What you saw most were ads for courses and bootcamps promising things like "become a programmer in 2 months and earn a Google salary." I called that kind of professional the "instant noodle chef": knowing how to boil water and make instant noodles doesn't make anyone a chef.

There's a detail few people notice. To work as a doctor or a lawyer in Brazil, you need to be registered with the CRM or the OAB, the medical and bar councils. To call yourself a "software engineer," you need no council at all: anyone can write that on LinkedIn.

<!-- src: 2019/06/11/akitando-50-a-bolha-de-startups-vai-estourar-winter-is-coming -->
In June 2019, in episode 50 of Akitando, I said the startup bubble was going to pop, as it always does, and that the only question was when. My guess was that the market would start to change from 2021 on [@akitando050].

<!-- src: 2020/02/19/akitando-72-rant-programacao-nao-e-facil -->
In February 2020, weeks before the pandemic, I repeated it in episode 72: the next time the bubble popped, "whoever doesn't know how to really deliver results proportional to what they earn is going to be left without a job" [@akitando072].

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
The pandemic, which I didn't predict, inflated the final phase even further. The bubble popped at the end of 2022, when the big tech companies started mass layoffs.

Meta alone cut more than 11,000 people on a single day in November, and the year closed with some 165,000 laid off across the industry, by the count of the Layoffs.fyi site [@zuckerberg2022layoffs; @layoffsfyi2026tracker]. ChatGPT showed up around the same time, by coincidence. It was, at most, the last nail in the coffin: the layoffs would have happened anyway.

<!-- src: 2022/11/22/akitando-132-rant-a-bolha-de-startups-estourou -->
I explained the mechanism in episode 132, in November 2022, with the layoffs already under way [@akitando132]. With American interest rates near zero for years, money that earned nothing sitting still went chasing risk: stocks, startups, mass hiring. When rates rose, over the course of 2022, that money went back to fixed income. The whole cycle, with the numbers, is in @sec-apbolhas.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
> The programming bubble popped before there were LLMs that could program. Whoever blames AI for the 2022 layoffs is looking at the wrong culprit.

<!-- src: 2022/11/22/akitando-132-rant-a-bolha-de-startups-estourou -->
In the same episode I got the calendar wrong. I said that was only the first leg of the crash, as in 2001, and that 2023 would be "a lost year for tech companies." The second leg never came: layoffs continued in 2023, but the tech stock market rose, pulled up by the AI wave. I warned about the bubble and missed the date, and I distrust anyone who claims to get both right.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Since I started programming, I've seen this happen several times: the end of the microcomputer bubble in the early 90s; the internet bubble, which popped between 2000 and 2002, and the programming recession that followed; the 2008 financial crisis, whose cheap money fed the programming bubble I mentioned above. Now I'm watching the AI bubble. It's always like this, and this is just one more round.

<!-- src: 2023/02/14/akitando-138-meu-comeco-de-carreira-durante-a-bolha-1997-a-2002-bug-do-milenio-crash -->
The internet bubble I lived through from the inside. At the end of 2001 I was working at a sports dot-com that closed in the crash, and none of the companies I'd passed through since 1996 survived [@akitando138]. I spent a week unemployed, until a mentor called me to a SAP consultancy that needed someone who knew the web. I didn't present myself as a programmer of any particular tool, and that's what let me switch fields that same week.

### The Bootcamp Engineers

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In January 2026, at the World Economic Forum in Davos, Anthropic's CEO, Dario Amodei, said we might be six to twelve months away from models doing everything a software engineer does, end to end [@haider2026amodei]. He sells the product, so take it with the appropriate grain of salt (@sec-ch14 deals with these pronouncements).

Even so, I agreed with him on one thing: the "software engineer" minted by a two-month bootcamp is going to become obsolete. Nobody becomes an engineer in a month, and the snake-oil sellers who promised that have vanished. That's my opinion, from someone who spent five years recording videos against that promise.

<!-- src: 2020/02/19/akitando-72-rant-programacao-nao-e-facil -->
The math is simple. From zero to a first junior level is about 1,400 hours of study and practice, and an 80-to-100-hour workshop covers about 5% of that, as I calculated in the same 2020 episode [@akitando072]. It's a back-of-the-envelope estimate, but the order of magnitude is enough: the two-month promise never added up.

### Volume Without Value

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
For years I gave talks titled "Constraint Equals Innovation." The idea is that innovation isn't born when there's money to burn. In the programming bubble there was plenty: why automate anything when you could hire more and more people to do simpler and simpler grunt work?

In the internet bubble, Yahoo paid people to surf the new web and catalog every site that appeared by hand [@brooks2014yahoo]. In the early years of Brazilian e-commerce, someone hand-edited the homepage code of Submarino, one of the country's first big online stores, every day to swap in the new deals.

It was high-volume, low-value work. We automated all of it, and LLMs are just one more step in the same direction. What always stops existing are the professions that are all volume and no added value.

## What's Left When the Typing Goes Away

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
"So we don't need computer science anymore?" Quite the opposite. Now you need it more.

Picture a beginner with no foundation asking the agent for a database query. The agent writes the laziest request possible, "bring me every row in the table." On the beginner's machine, with 300 test records, it works.

In production, the same query pulls a million rows, blows the memory, slows everything down, backs up the task queue and congests the connections. And the person has no clue why "it worked on my machine."

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
The agent doesn't know your system the way an experienced engineer does. It doesn't know which tables are going to grow tenfold next quarter, or which page needs to answer in 80 milliseconds and which one can take 2 seconds. It doesn't know which operation has to happen entirely or not at all, which one can't run twice, which one can never leak sensitive data.

It may well get the syntax right. But syntax was never the hard part.

<!-- src: 2022/06/13/akitando-120-discutindo-gestao -->
The programmer was never the assembly-line worker. In an episode about management, in June 2022, I put it this way: "Repetitive, manual work is already totally automated. The one filling that role is the compiler or interpreter" [@akitando120]. The agent pushes one more layer of typing onto the machine, and the programmer stays in the role that was always theirs: drawing the blueprint.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
I didn't get into computing to become an editor operator. I have no romantic attachment to formatting HTML, fighting CSS or writing the same old CRUD screen for the hundredth time. Let the machine do it. When that layer disappears, what's left is exactly the part that separates amateurs from professionals:

- understanding the business domain and modeling the problem;
- choosing the architecture and weighing the trade-offs of each decision;
- predicting performance and scale before they turn into fires;
- taking care of security and operating cost;
- keeping the system readable and easy to change two years from now;
- deciding what the product should and shouldn't do.

All of that still exists, is still hard and still depends on judgment.

### Exoskeleton or Crutch

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
People who understand operating systems, databases, networking, data structures, compilers, concurrency and security will use the agent as an exoskeleton. People who understand none of that will use it as a crutch.

An exoskeleton amplifies the strength you already have. A crutch only tries to hide weakness.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
In February I gave companies some advice, and I stand by it in October. No LLM writes code on its own: it needs a senior to spec it out. Every piece of generated code needs review, and a senior to review it.

Part of it will need rewriting, and part of it will have [bugs]{.gls key=bug} the model missed; knowing which is senior work. Once you know which, then you can have the model fix them.

### Strong Opinions, Labeled

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
That February piece had harsher opinions, and it's worth separating fact from my opinion.

On February 5, Anthropic published an engineering write-up in which a team of Claude agents running in parallel wrote a C compiler capable of compiling the Linux kernel. To check each result, the team used GCC, the most traditional C compiler in free software, as the reference for the right answer [@carlini2026compiler]. That's fact.

My reading is that it wasn't as impressive as the buzz made it look. Writing a C compiler is college coursework, C is a small language, and if you copy GCC, you arrive at another GCC.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Another opinion: I still think these models are [stochastic parrots]{.gls key=papagaio-estocastico}, the phrase @sec-ch01 explained, and that they recombine what already exists. Geoffrey Hinton, one of the fathers of modern neural networks, publicly argues the opposite: in a 2023 interview, he said these models definitely understand what they're saying [@pelley2023hinton]. He won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics and I didn't [@nobel2024physics].

Still, a résumé is not an argument, and I'm suspicious of anyone who ends a discussion by appealing to someone's authority. In @sec-ch20 I come back to this disagreement with what the measurement showed.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
And the most speculative one: in February I wrote that quality training data had run out. Everything good on the web had already been used, and the sources were drying up. The most visible example was Stack Overflow, the biggest programming Q&A forum. The number of new questions per month, already in decline, collapsed after ChatGPT and fell back to the level of 2009, the year after the site was born [@orosz2025stackoverflow].

Even a dumb question is training data. If nobody asks in public anymore, the next model learns less about new subjects. I treat this as my opinion, a trend I can see with no proof that it will last.

### What About Other Professions?

<!-- src: original -->
I'm a programmer, not a lawyer, accountant or journalist. What follows is my own extrapolation, and only as far as this chapter's reasoning will carry it.

Almost every job has a part that is data entry and a part that is judgment. The first is your punch card: filling out the form, formatting the new report from the old one, translating the same screen into Spanish, drafting the first version of a standard contract. For that kind of nail, as @sec-ch01 already said, the LLM is an excellent hammer.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
The second part is interpreting the law in a specific case, deciding the exception to the rule, relaxing a rule when it doesn't make sense, answering for the outcome. The model may have read every law in the country and still not know when to apply each one, let alone in the exceptions.

<!-- src: original -->
If your job is almost all the first part, I'd be worried, the way the punch card operators had reason to be, and I'd work on climbing a layer while there's time. If it's mostly the second, the tool will take the tedium off your hands, and what's left becomes worth more. A simple test: ask yourself whether you can explain why the right answer is the right one. If you can, the machine does the typing for you.

<!-- src: original -->
Two examples, still in extrapolation territory, from people who don't program. At an accounting firm, keying in entries, sorting the month's invoices and putting together the report in the usual format is the punch card. Deciding how to treat the expense that fits no account, explaining to the client why the tax went up, noticing that a strange number is hiding a problem: that's the upper layer. Climbing a layer, for the accountant or the analyst, means spending less time posting entries and more time being the person the client calls when the books don't balance.

In management, the punch card is the meeting minutes, the tracking spreadsheet, the consolidated report for the board. The upper layer is choosing the priority, saying no, explaining the decision to the team and answering for the result. The manager whose day is mostly pasting together other people's numbers has more reason to move than the one who decides with them.

## How Anyone Becomes a Senior

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
There's a serious objection to all of this. If you'll always need a senior instructing and reviewing, and LLMs do the grunt work that used to go to interns and juniors, how does anyone grow into a senior?

It's the billion-dollar question, and I don't know the full answer. Nobody does.

<!-- src: 2019/10/16/akitando-64-comecando-na-carreira-de-ti-faculdade-niveis-de-experiencia -->
It helps to remember what separates one level from another. Junior, mid-level and senior solve the same problem with similar quality, and what changes is time: the junior tries twenty paths, the senior already knows eighteen of them don't work. The senior's value starts with the ability to decide and answer for the decision. The agent tests the twenty paths faster than any junior; cutting the eighteen and signing off on the choice is still human work.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
Part of the answer I do know: the question starts from a false premise. The LLM doesn't do everything; it does what it was asked. A well-specified request comes out like a dish from a Michelin-starred restaurant; a lame request from someone inexperienced comes out as instant noodles. And the kicker is that every generation has already asked this same question:

- in the 60s: how is anyone going to be a programmer if they can't punch a card?
- in the 80s: how, if they can't save a program to a cassette tape?
- in the 90s: how, if they can't use an editor like Emacs and need a graphical environment to program?
- in the early 2000s: how, if they can't code for the web?
- in the 2010s: how, if they can't build a mobile app?
- in the 2020s: how, if there are LLMs?

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
None of those questions had a definitive answer. In 2026 I know things about programming I never dreamed of when I entered college in 1995. Whoever learned how to learn adapted, and used what they already knew to learn the new way.

<!-- src: original -->
But the people telling this story are the ones who made it across, and I'm one of them. Not everyone whose task disappeared stayed in the profession, like the punch card operators at the start of this chapter. The ones who stayed were, in general, the ones who climbed a layer in time. The precedent guarantees nothing for any one person; it shows which side of the transition is worth trying to be on.

### The Hiding Place Is Gone

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
Juniors are not going to stop learning. They're going to have to learn something else.

The junior of 2015 could spend years hiding their own ignorance behind grunt work: tweaking a screen, adjusting styles, assembling a dumb form, copying code snippets from a forum and looking productive. That hiding place is going away. In my reading, the junior of the agent era shows up sooner, for better and for worse: whoever studies levels up faster, and whoever only leans on the tool gets exposed faster. Both paths are within reach of someone just starting out.

<!-- src: 2020/04/01/akitando-76-guia-definitivo-de-aprendendo-a-aprender-a-maior-bronca-da-sua-vida-rated-r -->
Whoever learned only "how" to use a tool, and never "why" it exists, gets confused by every new tool; it's an old gripe of mine, told in @sec-apestudar. The agent is the newest tool in line. Whoever doesn't know why the code works doesn't notice when it's wrong.

<!-- src: original -->
For the good path, two habits already help: for every change the agent makes, ask why and check whether you could explain it too; and pick a genuinely hard problem and don't let go until you understand why the solution worked. The full roadmap, with the story that taught me this, is in @sec-ch08.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
If they use the agent and actually study fundamentals, they test hypotheses faster, read more code, compare more solutions, make mistakes earlier and fix them earlier. They learn more in less time.

If they use the agent without fundamentals, all they do is outsource their own ignorance. They become a reviewer who can't review, who approves changes they don't understand and confuses "passed the tests on my machine" with "ready for production." That professional is more dangerous than the old junior, who was at least held back by their own slowness.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
In the four days hunting a video bug in a PlayStation 4 emulator (@sec-ch08), I learned again that a senior is whoever becomes a junior again every time they step into a new topic.

### Keep Hiring Juniors

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->
For companies, my February message still stands: keep hiring juniors. Your seniors aren't immortal. Eventually they retire, and long before that they change jobs.

<!-- src: 2021/09/28/akitando-104-rant-empreendendo-com-software-do-jeito-errado -->
In 2021 I was saying the same thing to people starting software companies: put seniors mentoring juniors, because today's junior is tomorrow's senior [@akitando104]. AI doesn't change that math. In my reading, the company that stops training juniors now will be competing for seniors on the market in a few years, along with every other company that did the same.

<!-- src: 2026/02/08/rant-ia-acabou-com-programadores -->

The second most important task of a senior is training their own replacement. A senior who can't do that becomes a liability for the company. Mentoring is still what it always was, training with follow-up and constant feedback, only now with one more LLM in the toolbox.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
> AI reflects who you are. If you're good, it accelerates good code. If you're bad, it accelerates [technical debt]{.gls key=divida-tecnica} at industrial speed.

It's the lesson of the marathon in @sec-ch06 and @sec-ch07, now with an engine: AI never turned a bad programmer into a good one.

## Which Language to Learn

<!-- src: 2026/02/09/ai-agents-qual-seria-a-melhor-linguagem-de-programacao-para-llms -->
That leaves the question every beginner asks me right after the one about the job: which programming language should I learn now? I asked a version of it myself on February 9, 2026, one day after that piece about the bubble, and it took me the whole year to get a measured answer.

My February hunch was that the best language for the machine would be the one humans find most tedious to use: rigid, [strongly typed]{.gls key=tipagem-forte}, bureaucratic, along the lines of Rust, OCaml or Lean. Claude and GPT Codex, when I asked, agreed with me. But asking the model is evidence of nothing, and I wrote that down at the time.

<!-- src: 2026/09/19/nova-linguagem-voltada-pra-ia-bend-2 -->
In September, the Brazilian researcher Victor Taelin released Bend 2, a language where the AI writes proofs that the code obeys the rules the human defined, and a checker blocks whatever doesn't close [@taelin2026bend2]. I tested it on launch day. The checker caught an error that the sample tests let through; the rest of the language was still toy-grade.

<!-- src: 2026/10/11/existem-linguagens-melhores-pra-programar-com-llms -->
In October I went and measured. I built a reproducible [benchmark]{.gls} [@akita2026vibebench]: one hard accounting task, seven languages (from Rust and Haskell to Python and Ruby), four models, each combination with and without a test suite in hand.

The two [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira} got every valid attempt right, in every language. Notice what that means, and what it doesn't: when everyone gets the top grade, the test ranks nothing. It doesn't say which language is better for those models, only that none got in the way on this task.

<!-- src: 2026/10/11/existem-linguagens-melhores-pra-programar-com-llms -->
Where there was a difference to see, in the weaker models, what changed the result was the test suite: 24 correct deliveries with it, against 16 without it. And where the models erred, they erred on business rules, which no compiler catches, in typed or dynamic languages. It was a single task, with two repetitions per combination; if yours doesn't look like it, run your own test.

<!-- src: original -->
Putting the threads together, the February question got a better answer than my hypothesis. The rigidity of the language didn't show up in the result, and the presence of tests did. What worked in Bend was the checker, and the test suite that saved the weaker models played the same role: both are an [oracle]{.gls key=oraculo} in the sense of @sec-ch08, someone on the outside who says whether the answer is right.

> Tests helped more than the choice of language. What improved the result was giving the agent a way to know it got something wrong, and that is the discipline of whoever is at the controls.

<!-- src: 2026/10/11/existem-linguagens-melhores-pra-programar-com-llms -->
So which language should you learn? The one that solves your problem, with the biggest ecosystem around it. Every error from my agents became a new rule, a new test, a new [skill]{.gls}. None of them became a language switch.

<!-- src: original -->
For someone just starting out, what's worth studying is what sits underneath any language: how the computer works, how data is stored, how to know whether the program is right. That cuts across every language and every interface, from the punch card to the agent.

<!-- src: 2023/02/14/akitando-138-meu-comeco-de-carreira-durante-a-bolha-1997-a-2002-bug-do-milenio-crash -->
It's the lesson of that week in 2001. Between 1996 and the end of 2001 I went through about ten languages and tools, from Delphi to ASP, PHP and Perl, and I wasn't "a programmer of" any of them [@akitando138].

<!-- src: 2019/01/30/akitando-37-a-dimensao-do-tempo-para-iniciantes-em-programacao-serie-comecando-aos-40 -->
In 1972, Gordon Bell formulated a law I liked to remind beginners of: roughly every decade a new class of computer is born, with a new platform and a new interface [@wikipedia2026belllaw]. The dominant technology of one decade is usually in decline in the next. Picking the right language forever was never an option; learning the next one always was.

<!-- src: original -->
The full Bend 2 story and the numbers from the language test are in @sec-aptecnico-bend and @sec-aptecnico-linguagens.

## Who Decides

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
So back to the question you brought with you: will AI replace programmers?

It will replace the typist, the way the card punch was replaced. The work of deciding stays. At the end of the day, someone has to look at the result and answer:

- can this go to production?
- can this handle the load?
- is this secure?
- is this easy to maintain?
- does this fit the rest of the system?
- does this solve the right problem?

If the answer is no, someone has to know why it's no. And, more importantly, they have to know how to fix it.

<!-- src: 2026/04/11/vs-code-e-o-novo-cartao-perfurado -->
That's why, in the age of agents, foundational knowledge became more valuable: being wrong without it got more expensive. VS Code is the new punch card because, finally, typing code by hand has stopped being the center of the profession. About time. The agent can write; you're still the one who has to know whether what it wrote is any good.

<!-- src: 2026/06/24/por-que-llms-vao-falhar-na-sua-empresa -->
Except that conclusion hides a trap. If what counts is whoever decides, the companies that bought AI by the ton should be reaping the results, and many aren't. As I read it, the trap lives in the way a lot of companies trained their people: precisely not to decide.
