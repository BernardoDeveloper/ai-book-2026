# How to Talk to the Machine {#sec-ch09}

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
In April 2026, every online thread about AI I got pulled into had some version of the same line. "I canceled my Claude plan, it just doesn't perform as well as GPT for me." "GPT did a way better job, I'm canceling my sub to the other one." "Kimi does the job for me just fine, I'm not paying for anything."

It sounded off to me, for a simple reason. By then I had over 500 hours in Claude Code and OpenAI's Codex on real projects, at a pace that reached 16 hours a day, over two and a half months. Something in the neighborhood of 400,000 lines of generated code.

And in all that time, on tasks I knew how to describe properly, when the model couldn't do something, it often told me so upfront instead of inventing. Both of them delivered. So whenever someone told me "Claude blew it," I'd throw back the question that closed the previous chapter.

In the cases where the person showed me what they had asked for, often the defect started right there.

<!-- src: original -->
That describes my experience, with well-specified tasks. Models make things up, and you saw that in @sec-ch04. Models also differ from one another: in @sec-ch11 the same test gives very different grades to different models, and in @sec-ch12 some models cheat on the test and write a success report for work that doesn't function, no matter how good the request was. This chapter is about the part that depends on you.

## What's in Your Head

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
In 2013 I wrote a piece called "Programmers are terrible communicators." The problem I described there is exactly what blew up once everyone started talking to an [LLM]{.gls} at work. The technology got far more powerful. The people stayed the same people.

Here's how it works. You've got a pile of information in your head: the project's history, the constraints, your preferences, what went sideways before, what got decided in a meeting two months back. Then you walk into a conversation, with a coworker or a machine, and fire off your request as if all of that were sitting on the other side too.

You write "do it the way I'm telling you." Except what you're telling them is really "do it the way I'm thinking." And you don't even notice the difference.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
Developers are bad communicators, managers too, and the default corporate answer is more of the same: more meetings, more spreadsheets. If your communication was bad at volume 1, it stays bad at volume 5.

With AI, the industry answered the same way, with more layers: methodologies with proper names, like Spec Driven Development, 15-section prompt templates, tools to force the model to ask questions before it starts. I respect the intent. But it treats the symptom.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
No future version of Claude, GPT or Gemini is going to guess what's in your head. Context doesn't arrive by osmosis. If the information isn't in the material the model sees or in your request, it simply doesn't exist for the model.

The most useful way I've found to think about an LLM is as a contractor. A magician would solve the problem without you saying anything. A contractor solves exactly what you asked for, with the information you provided, and anybody who has hired outside help knows where a vague request ends up. The LLM is the same thing, just faster and more patient.

> The quality of what you get back is directly proportional to the effort you put into asking.

## Four Things to Say

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
A real example from that April. I have about 12 terabytes of old games, the cartridge and CD files that emulators run, piled up over more than ten years on my file server. That's more than 400,000 files in two folders that came from different collections, with inconsistent names and duplicates everywhere.

I wanted to consolidate all of it into a new folder, organized by platform, following the standardized names the preservation community uses. That was the goal, and I stated it up front.

But I didn't stop there. I also said what I didn't want:

- never treat two files as identical just because they share a name, because filenames lie far too much in this world; check by content;
- keep one copy of each regional version of the same game, because to a collector the Japanese and the American versions are different games;
- keep apart the variants of the same game made for different emulators, which are mutually incompatible.

If I leave that out, the model has no way to know. That knowledge was in my head. Without it, the model assumes its most reasonable default, which might be the exact opposite of what I need.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
Then came the method. I asked for the work to be split into separate, numbered steps, each one safe to run again without wrecking anything, so I could resume halfway through if something crashed. Progress would be recorded in a small database, to survive a crash. That's aligning the way we work, without micromanaging.

And even after seeing its plan, I kept thinking about what could go wrong. No file in the original folders would be deleted: whatever didn't get promoted to the new folder simply stayed where it was. The step that actually moved files could only run after I approved the planning step by hand.

It's a locked gate between planning and doing, against my mistakes, its mistakes, or both at once.

### The Structure Behind It

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
Notice the pattern. I never say just "solve this problem." I say "solve this problem, this way, without doing that, and when you're done, check this." That's four things:

1. **What I want.** The end goal, in plain language.
2. **How I want it done.** In broad strokes, leaving room for the model to suggest something better, because it usually has a suggestion.
3. **What I don't want.** The part people usually skip and the most important one, because that's where every unspoken assumption lives, the ones that turn into bugs later.
4. **How we know it worked.** What the expected result is, what the test is, what the "done" signal looks like.

![The four parts of a good request: what I want, how, what I don't want, and how to know it worked.](assets/figures/en/ch09-quatro-partes.png){#fig-ch09-quatro-partes fig-alt="A request card split into four bands." fig-desc="A request card split into four bands. The first says 'what I want', the second 'how', the third, highlighted, 'what I don't want', and the fourth 'how to know it worked'. One arrow goes from the card to an agent and another comes back carrying the question 'and if it can't?'."}

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
That fourth part is a killer. Most of the clients I worked with over the past twenty years couldn't tell me what the expected result was. Wanting something is easy. Knowing how to measure whether it showed up is hard.

Without a success metric, expectations break by definition, because there was never a concrete expectation to begin with. That goes for consulting, and it goes for an AI [agent]{.gls key=agente}.

When I hand over all four parts, on tasks I know, the model rarely fails. And when it can't do the thing, because the task is impossible under my constraints or because I forgot to pass along some piece of information, it often says something like "given these constraints, I can't proceed because of this." Then I adjust, loosen a constraint, or realize I didn't actually know what I wanted.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
All of this is far less formal than it sounds. I don't write ten-page specs or fill out templates. It's just how I talk to anyone who has to deliver something to me, a habit I picked up running projects, managing contractors and getting knocked around every time my communication was bad.

And the effort scales with what's at stake. For a weekend experiment, something disposable, I cut the request back a lot, knowing my expectations might break, and that's fine. If the result matters, I invest the time to explain.

### Outside Programming

<!-- src: original -->
I use this to program, but nothing in the four parts is specific to code. It works for any request you make to a chatbot, including for people who will never open a terminal in their lives.

Picture someone asking for help writing a letter disputing a wrongful charge from their health insurer. The typical request is one line: "write a letter disputing this charge." What comes back is a generic letter, polite and useless, because the model knows nothing about your case.

With the four parts, the same request looks like this:

- **what I want:** a letter to the insurer's ombudsman asking for a refund of one specific charge, with date and amount;
- **how:** firm, formal tone, one page at most, citing the contract number and the ticket number from my previous call;
- **what I don't want:** no threat of a lawsuit in this first letter, no statute you aren't sure exists, no personal detail I didn't give you;
- **how to know it worked:** after writing it, list every factual claim the letter makes, so I can check them one by one.

The third item protects you from [hallucinations]{.gls key=alucinacao}, those plausible inventions we saw in @sec-ch04. The fourth hands the checking back to you, the person who knows what's true in your case.

Nobody needs to memorize a form to do this. Before you hit Enter, just ask: what do I know about this that the machine has no way of knowing?

## The Conversation Keeps Going

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
Back to the game folders. When the model got going, I didn't leave the room. I kept asking for status.

I noticed the time estimate for the step that computed each file's identity was much longer than the problem warranted. I interrupted: we can do this in parallel, there's spare CPU, the network can handle it; bump up the parallelism, test, and check that it stays stable. That kind of intervention is real [pair programming]{.gls key=pair-programming}, the same as in @sec-ch06, except one of the pair is a machine.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
I call this way of working Agile Vibe Coding: the techniques of [XP]{.gls key=xp}, the Extreme Programming that shows up in @sec-ch06, applied on top of ordinary prompting. Short cycles, fast feedback, continuous correction.

Nobody sits down with a colleague, drops a 15-line task, stands up and walks away expecting magic. You stay there, watch the work take shape, catch the error while it's still cheap to fix, add the context you just remembered.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
The blog post where I told this story was, by the way, written by Claude, off a request of mine with goal, context, references and constraints. And that first request was only the first. While it wrote, I kept sending corrections:

- I added points I'd forgotten to put in the first request;
- I flagged an out-of-date reference to an OpenAI product and had it fixed;
- I told it to read the documentation we'd already written about organizing the games, to sharpen the example;
- I adjusted the tone wherever it drifted from mine.

When I had already authorized the English translation and the push, I still interrupted at the last second over an English word dropped into the Portuguese for no reason. The initial [prompt]{.gls} is the starting point of the conversation. There is no final contract.

### Ask Instead of Prescribe

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
There's a shift I always make after the first few detailed requests. In the opening turns I pour in a ton: goal, constraints, method, validation, everything the model needs to understand the terrain. Once that ground is solid, I stop prescribing the solution and start asking for suggestions.

Instead of "implement it this way, with that library, following this pattern," I switch to asking: "given everything we've covered, what's the best approach here? Research if you need to, compare the options and come back with the one you think fits best."

The model has much more vocabulary than I do across a pile of topics. It was trained on a huge slice of the internet; I read whatever I have time for. If I prescribe line by line, I throw that edge away. If I set the context and then ask, it comes back with options I hadn't considered, sometimes better than my original idea, and I get to pick.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
The order matters. A dry question with no ground gets back a generic answer, or the first idea that seemed to fit. A question resting on context you already laid comes back with a real proposal, comparing paths.

It's how I treat any human developer, except the LLM gives it back in seconds when a person would take days.

<!-- src: original -->
For people using a chatbot outside programming, the shift is the same. Once you've explained your financial situation, your deadline and what you won't accept, instead of asking "build me a spreadsheet like this," ask "what options do I have, and what are the pros and cons of each?" The decision is still yours, but now you're deciding with more cards on the table.

### Stark and Jarvis

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
The image I use for this comes from the movies. Tony Stark, in the Marvel films, has Jarvis, probably the most advanced fictional AI pop culture has produced in the last two decades: it understands voice and context, plans, runs several things at once, even has a personality.

And even so, Jarvis on his own doesn't build a single suit. Jarvis executes; Stark sets the course.

There's a detail almost nobody remembers. Not even Stark, with all his genius and Jarvis riding shotgun, nails the suit on the first try. The first one, in the 2008 film, was bolted together out of scrap in a cave [@favreau2008ironman]. The second could already fly, but iced over at altitude.

Each version fixes a flaw in the last, movie after movie, all the way to the suit called the Mark 85, his last, in *Avengers: Endgame*, from 2019 [@russo2019endgame]. The character's whole arc is a ten-year campaign of iterative development.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
That's the mental model I think is right for working with AI. You, in the Stark chair, use your Jarvis to speed up every lap of the cycle: propose, test, see what broke, fix it, propose again.

> AI speeds up every lap of the cycle. No AI eliminates the laps.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
A note on the period, because tools age fast: in mid-April 2026, with the models tied, I preferred Claude Code over Codex because of the [harness]{.gls}, and that comparison is in @sec-ch03.

I wrote at the time that Codex would probably catch up within months, and that I'd laugh rereading the comparison. The comparison was from April. The advice on how to ask holds for either of them and for whatever comes next.

## A Reader in a Hurry

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
So far I've talked about how to ask. There's a second half to the conversation, less obvious: the material you hand the agent to read. For people who program, that material is the code itself.

In 2008, Robert C. Martin, Uncle Bob, published *Clean Code*, one of the most influential books in software engineering [@martin2008clean]. The premise was that code is written once and read dozens of times, so it has to be written in a way another person can understand, modify and not break. The reader he had in mind was another human being sitting at an editor.

In 2026, the main reader of the code in a project like mine is an agent. And that reader has very different limitations from ours.

### How the Agent Reads

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
Some of those limitations are mechanical, and worth knowing:

- **it reads in slices.** In April 2026, Claude Code read 2,000 lines of a file at a time by default. A giant file doesn't fit in one go; the agent asks for it piece by piece and assembles a fragmented picture;
- **attention drops as the desk fills up.** The [context window]{.gls key=janela-de-contexto} (@sec-ch02) splits its space among your code, the instructions, the conversation history, test output and error messages, and precision on details drops well before the advertised limit;
- **it would rather search than read.** Instead of opening a whole file, the agent uses [grep]{.gls key=grep}, an exact-word search across every file in the project. It's faster and cheaper;
- **every step costs.** Every read, edit or command burns [tokens]{.gls key=token}, which turn into money and seconds of waiting.

![A giant file read in slices and a small file read in one go.](assets/figures/en/ch09-fatias.png){#fig-ch09-fatias fig-alt="A file is sliced up; only 2,000 lines enter the agent's field of view." fig-desc="On the left, a very long file cut into several slices; only one slice, of 2,000 lines, fits in the agent's field of view, and the rest stays out. On the right, a short file that fits entirely in the agent's field of view."}

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
From there, several *Clean Code* rules that used to be a matter of taste became technical requirements. The ones that weigh most:

- **small files and functions.** A unit of meaning that fits in a single read gets understood with full attention;
- **unique, searchable names.** A generic name like "data" or "process" returns fifty search hits and forces the agent to read each one. A specific name returns three;
- **comments that say why.** The 2008 book treated too many comments as a sign of bad code. For the agent it's the reverse: it reads code perfectly, but it doesn't know why you chose a strange path, which bug motivated that line, which business constraint forces that order;
- **tests the agent can run on its own.** One command, no manual setup, predictable output.

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
That last item is what separates a useful agent from one that's guessing. It writes the code, runs the [automated test]{.gls key=teste-automatizado}, reads the result, adjusts, runs it again. If the test won't run without a human in the middle, the agent goes blind.

On the newsletter project I described in @sec-ch06, the automatic check that runs on every change, the [CI]{.gls key=ci}, caught real bugs more than 50 times. Bugs that would have gone straight to production if I had trusted the agent with my eyes closed.

The XP practices, which plenty of people already considered old-fashioned, turned out to be the right way to work with AI.

### The Rules File

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
There's a detail that only becomes clear after hundreds of hours: no LLM does any of this on its own. Ask it to "implement feature X" and it implements it the average way, whatever showed up most in training. Huge functions, duplicated logic because it's faster, 2,000-line files because "everything's in one place."

And there were safeguards I had to ask for. Retrying when an outside service fails, giving up after a timeout, degrading gracefully when something goes down: the agent implements all of that nicely when you ask. Without those instructions, it could stick to the happy path, because it didn't know where my system's weak points were.

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
The fix is to write the rules down somewhere the agent always reads. That place is the [project instruction file]{.gls key=agents-md}: a text file at the root of the project, called AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md depending on the tool, which the agent loads at the start of every session, before touching anything.

That's where the house conventions go: how the architecture is organized, which command runs the tests, what never to do, what kind of safeguards the project requires. It works like the [system prompt]{.gls key=system-prompt} from @sec-ch03, except you write it, and it's specific to that project.

<!-- src: 2026/04/20/clean-code-para-agentes-de-ia -->
Writing this file is a new skill. The text has to be short, direct, imperative. No philosophical prose: a list of what the agent needs to know so it doesn't mess up. Since it rereads the file on every request, each line takes up room on the desk, and density matters.

A typical excerpt looks like this:

```markdown
- Files under 500 lines. Split by responsibility.
- Names specific and unique. Avoid "data", "handler", "Manager".
- Keep your own comments. Don't strip them on refactor.
- Every bug fix gets a test that reproduces the bug.
- Tests run with a single command: <project-specific>.
```

That "keep your own comments" looks silly, but it makes a difference. Without it, the agent deletes its own comments when it [refactors]{.gls key=refatoracao}, because default training taught it that comments are noise. That's precisely the context it will want to read next time.

<!-- src: 2026/02/01/vibe-code-fiz-um-editor-de-markdown-do-zero-com-claude-code-frankmd-parte-2 -->
In @sec-ch05 I told how Claude, without any instruction from me, crammed FrankMD's logic into two or three generic files, and in @sec-ch06 how a single file of it swelled to almost 5,000 lines. The right move would have been to write this file on day one, saying how I wanted the architecture. I didn't. I paid for it in hours of refactoring.

### The "Don't" That Holds Forever

<!-- src: 2026/05/30/boas-praticas-projetos-codigo-aberto-llm-o-minimo -->
The requests I repeat most also follow the four-part rule. When an outsider sends a contribution to one of my projects, a [pull request]{.gls key=pull-request}, the review request starts like this:

> *"Look at the open PR on GitHub. Don't trust the author's description. Audit the code thoroughly to see if it actually makes sense."*

A PR description tells you what the person thought they did, and that doesn't always match what they actually did. LLMs are excellent reviewers, more consistent than I am on my fifth review of the day, but I'm the one who decides to accept, fix or reject.

<!-- src: 2026/05/30/boas-praticas-projetos-codigo-aberto-llm-o-minimo -->
And since all my projects follow the same pattern, with the same deploy script and the same way of cutting a release, I can say "run the deploy" and the agent can do it. Nothing has to be guessed. Standardizing means writing the manual once.

<!-- src: original -->
People who don't program have a household version of the rules file. Most chatbots have a field for permanent instructions, which applies to every conversation, or "projects" with instructions of their own [@openai2023custominstructions]. That's where you put what you're tired of repeating: your profession, the tone you prefer, what never to make up, how to flag what it isn't sure about.

Keep it short, in the imperative, and add a line whenever you notice you've repeated the same correction for the third time.

## Prompts with Scars

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
In September, several readers asked me about the "skills" I mentioned when I talked about my workflow. The name suggests some sophisticated technology. There's no magic involved.

A [skill]{.gls} is a folder with a text file inside. The file has a header with a name and a description, and a body that is, essentially, a well-written prompt about how to carry out a task. Sometimes it ships with small helper programs, so the model doesn't have to recreate them every time.

> Skills are just prompts you versioned, refined and kept one command away.

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
More skills don't make the agent better. Every installed one competes for its attention when it decides what to use; Anthropic itself wrote, in a guide on tools for agents, that more tools don't always lead to better outcomes [@anthropic2025writingtools].

My version is blunter: an idle skill is debt. You change your process, the skill ages, and one day the agent executes a rule that no longer makes sense. How the tools load skills without filling the desk is in @sec-aptecnico-skills.

### Don't Install Other People's Skills

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
My skills are published in an open repository [@akita2026myskills], with a warning in the description: not tailored for general usage. They're there for anyone who wants to read the reasoning and build their own.

Copying someone else's skill and running it blind is like signing a contract without reading it. Worse: you're installing another person's instructions to run with your permissions, on your files, with your accounts. It's a [prompt injection]{.gls key=prompt-injection}, orders hidden in text the machine will read, except you installed it voluntarily. The full mechanics of that attack are in @sec-ch16.

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
A single skill from outside survived my scrutiny, and it runs on every piece of text I publish. The Wikipedia community maintains a public catalog of the signs of AI-generated text, the constructions that give the machine away [@wikipedia2025signsai]. A skill called humanizer turns that catalog into a procedure: mark the tells, rewrite without changing what was said, check again.

Even with that one, I read the source material before trusting it, and merged a version with my own adjustments into my own repository. That's the pattern.

### Polished the Hard Way

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
The skills I use most handle the contributions that arrive in my open source projects. The audit skill, for example, starts from the premise that nothing a contributor wrote is true until it's been checked. It's the same "don't trust the author's description," written once and applied every time.

None of them was born ready. Each one was polished the hard way, month after month, and no rule in there came from somebody else's opinion. Each one came from me face-planting into the exact error the rule now prevents. The routine and the numbers of that maintenance are in @sec-aptecnico-skills.

> Every line in a skill is a scar from a mistake you already made.

### Knowledge Outweighs Skills

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
Now the counterpoint. I depend on skills very little. Skills are for procedures that repeat the same way. What actually carries my workflow is something else:

- **each project's instruction file,** with the rules specific to that code;
- **a persistent memory per project,** with the decisions and the gotchas only that code has. Without it, every session starts from zero, like an intern with amnesia (agent memory is the subject of @sec-ch02);
- **pointing at a living example.** When I wanted to automate publishing a package for a new project, I didn't write any skill. I asked: "do it the same way we did in that other project." The agent read what worked and reproduced it.

A living example has one advantage over a skill: it's always up to date, because when it breaks, I fix it.

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
If you take a single practice from this part, take this one: have the agent write down everything that didn't become code. The research it did, the decision you made and why, the alternative you threw out. Without that, the knowledge evaporates at the end of the session.

In my memory project for agents, every big decision was preceded by research on the competitors, and each piece of research became a document in the project itself. Every hour of research an agent did for me became a permanent asset, which I reuse in any new project. It's compound interest for knowledge.

<!-- src: original -->
This works outside programming too. If you used a chatbot to understand your options for a loan, ask at the end for a summary of the conclusions, of what was ruled out and why, and save it in a file of your own. In the next conversation, start by pasting that summary. That way the memory stays with you, outside the vendor's account.

### Nobody Knows What You Need

<!-- src: 2026/09/17/falando-um-pouco-sobre-minhas-skills-de-ia -->
My audit skill was molded around my projects, my languages, my risks, my level of paranoia about security. Your project has a different mix of all that, and you may need checks I don't, and vice versa. Experience only transfers to people who have lived under the same constraints.

In 2019 I made a whole video about this on Akitando, my channel, called "Não terceirize suas decisões" (Don't outsource your decisions) [@akitando063]: nobody but you knows what you need. AI didn't change that rule. It just made everything else cheaper. Research that took a week now takes an afternoon, and the cost of building your own tool, or your own prompt, fell through the floor.

## The Answer Is 42

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
My company is called Codeminer 42. The number comes from Douglas Adams's *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* [@adams1979guia].

In the story, a civilization builds a supercomputer to calculate the Answer to the Ultimate Question of Life, the Universe, and Everything. After millions of years of processing, it announces the result: 42. Then comes an awkward silence, because nobody really knows what the question was.

42 is a technically correct answer to a question nobody managed to formulate. Which is why it means nothing.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
That's the sharpest lesson about engineering I've ever read in fiction. When an expectation breaks, the question was often wrong, even when the answer was well executed.

My work with a client, human or machine, starts right there: getting the person to see that what they think they want is probably wrong, rethink the assumptions they walked in with, and only then execute. Skip that step and anything I deliver is going to be a 42.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
The skill that matters is still the one held by whoever asks: knowing what you want, what you won't accept and how to measure. With it, AI makes the professional more productive.

<!-- src: original -->
And that skill can be learned. I learned it by running projects and getting beaten up when I communicated badly, and you don't have to repeat the whole path: the four parts of this chapter are the method, and you can practice it on the next request you make, to a machine or to a person. If you're starting your career, @sec-ch08 shows how I learn what I don't know, and @sec-ch18 covers what changes for people entering the field now.

<!-- src: original -->
Next time you read someone saying they canceled an AI because it "doesn't deliver," it may be that the model really did fail: they do, and they differ from one another. But before concluding that, take a good look at the question the person was asking. Many times, the answer they got back was 42.

<!-- src: original -->
Knowing how to ask, though, is only half the job. The other half is taking a cold look at what came out of everything I asked for. In May, after months of asking agents for things almost all day long, it was time for that reckoning.
