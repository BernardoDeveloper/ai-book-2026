# My First Failure {#sec-ch08}

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
On Sunday, March 1, 2026, I tagged version 0.1 of Frank Yomik. Six days of work, 90 commits, and a 551-line file with a single mission: look at a manga page and say where the speech balloons are.

It got things wrong. Not always, but often enough. Every new manga I opened revealed some case that broke the detector, and the most common mistake was always the same: the program looked at a character's face and decided it was a balloon.

In practice, the app erased the face of the girl with blue hair and wrote the translation over it. In a reading tool, that's the kind of defect that makes everything else useless.

When I told you where the Frank projects get their name (@sec-ch05), I called Frank Yomik my first real failure. Time to tell the story. And it's instructive precisely because the AI did everything I asked, fast, without complaining. The problem was in the plan.

## A Face Where the Balloon Should Be

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
I have a huge collection of manga bought from Amazon Japan, which I read through Kindle in the browser. Manga for teenagers usually comes with furigana, those tiny characters next to the kanji that tell you how to read them. Furigana helps me a lot.

Manga for adult readers usually comes without. It's just the kanji, and my reading crawls.

For years I'd wanted a tool for this. The idea was simple:

1. find the speech balloons on the page;
2. extract the text with [OCR]{.gls key=ocr}, the automatic recognition of letters in an image;
3. add the furigana, or translate straight into English;
4. draw the result back inside the balloon.

It sounds easy, and I never had the patience to do it precisely because I knew it only sounded that way. I know how to build the first 80% of almost any project. What scares me is the last 20%, the phase of experimenting, tweaking and handling every weird case, which usually eats more time than everything else combined.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
Then came [vibe coding]{.gls key=vibe-coding}, the way of programming by asking an [agent]{.gls key=agente} in plain language to write the code. I thought: maybe now that 20% becomes doable. I started on February 24, 2026, at 11:10 p.m., with Claude writing and me steering.

The name combines "yomi", reading in Japanese, and "ik-da", to read in Korean, because the app was also going to read webtoons, the Korean digital comics.

### The Plan I'd Carried for Years

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
My original plan used OpenCV, an old and famous computer vision library [@opencv]. The reasoning was that of someone who has programmed a lot: a manga balloon is a white area with a black outline. So all you have to do is look for white regions, find the outlines, filter by size and shape, and you're done.

That kind of solution is called a [heuristic]{.gls key=heuristica}: a set of handwritten rules describing how we imagine the thing usually is.

Within 24 hours I had a proof of concept that did everything, including translating with a language model running on my own machine. The next day I was already extending it all to webtoons, 27 commits in a single day. It seemed to be flowing.

And then the hell began.

### The Thing That Looks Most Like a Balloon

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
Manga isn't a standardized document. Every artist has a different line, scan quality changes from one printing era to another, and color pages call for completely different parameters than black-and-white ones.

And the thing that looks most like a white balloon on a manga page is a face: a light, more or less rounded area with a dark outline.

So I kept stacking filters. One measured how many edges there were inside the region, because a balloon has few strokes (just the letters) and a face has hair, eyes, a nose. Another checked whether the region was really white, another threw out shapes that were too irregular. At the peak, there were seven layers of filters, each one created in response to a specific mistake.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
The worst part was that the filters interacted with each other in unpredictable ways. On February 26 I loosened two numeric thresholds, touching only the second decimal place, to recover balloons that were getting lost. Faces and body parts started passing as balloons in one of the manga I was using for testing, and I had to undo it all.

That pattern repeated for days. Recovering a lost balloon opened the door to a face, and blocking the face lost a legitimate balloon. It was whack-a-mole: you hit one and another pops up right next to it.

When the filters weren't enough, the band-aids came. A second pass boosted the image's contrast to find grayer balloons, except it also boosted the characters' skin and required an entire function to check the candidates against the original image. Then came a third pass, based on edges. Every band-aid added 50 to 100 lines and one more layer of complexity, with its own special cases.

![Handwritten rules on one side; a single model trained on thousands of pages on the other.](assets/figures/en/ch08-regras-vs-modelo.png){#fig-ch08-regras-vs-modelo fig-alt="Two sides next to each other. On the left, a manga page going through a stack of seven sieves, each with a patch, and a character's face slipping through the last one, marked as if it were a balloon, with the label 'balloon?'. On the right, the same page going into a single box labeled 'model trained on 11 thousand images' and coming out with the balloons marked correctly and the face intact."}

### The Tally at Version 0.1

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
When I tagged version 0.1, the detector had:

- 551 lines in a single file;
- seven layers of filters against false positives;
- three different detection passes;
- separate profiles for color and black-and-white pages;
- more than 20 magic numbers, thresholds tuned by eye against a small sample of pages;
- automated tests pinning every mistake I'd already seen: the face of the girl with blue hair, a window frame, a thin horizontal strip, a concrete floor.

Even with all that, it wasn't reliable. It was an extremely brittle monolith.

> When you're patching a fix that fixed another fix that was fixing yet another fix, and touching one piece accidentally breaks another, the code has become a house of cards. That's the moment to stop and rethink.

<!-- src: original -->
Notice that at no point did Claude fail in the sense people imagine. I asked for a new filter, it wrote a new filter, correct, with a test. I asked for a third pass, it wrote it. The machine flawlessly executed a bad plan, and executed it so fast that I went much further in the wrong direction than I would have alone.

## The Day-One Question

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
At version 0.1 I finally stopped and asked the question I should have asked at the start: has anyone already trained a model to do exactly this?

I asked Claude to research it. The most promising alternative was an open source detector published by a developer who goes by ogkalu, trained on about 11,000 images of manga, webtoons and Western comics [@ogkalu2025bubble]. It uses an object detection architecture called RT-DETR [@lyu2024rtdetr] and tells three things apart: the balloon, the text inside the balloon and the loose text on the page, like narration and sound effects.

That's a [pretrained model]{.gls key=modelo-pre-treinado}. Somebody gathered thousands of labeled examples and let the machine learn on its own the difference between a balloon and a face. Nobody wrote the rules: they're baked into the model's [parameters]{.gls key=parametros}, adjusted during training.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
On March 4 I swapped everything. The commit deleted 1,112 lines and added 732. The 551-line detector became one of 262, and most of those lines aren't even detection, they're cutting out the balloon's shape from the box the model returns. Another entire file, 387 lines long, which looked for loose text on the page, vanished along with it.

Gone were the magic numbers, the color and black-and-white profiles, the seven filters. A model that has seen 11,000 different pages already knows how to tell a balloon from a face better than my pile of "if this, then that".

I didn't even need to train the model on my own data, the [fine-tuning]{.gls key=fine-tuning} I'd had planned. As it came, it handled more than 99% of the cases.

### Why 80% Is Too Little

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
People who aren't used to statistics have a hard time seeing this difference. With my rules I was already catching about 80% of the balloons, maybe more. Sounds good. But if on every page some face gets a balloon slapped on top of it, the app is terrible.

Even if I got to 95%, with a lot of effort and another five hundred rules, it still wouldn't be enough. Reaching 80% is easy. The last 20% costs exponentially more, and the last 1% can be impossible in many cases.

### Prompting Doesn't Replace Thinking

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
I spent about five days polishing a detector, with more than 20 fine-tuning commits, that in the end was replaced by about 50 lines wrapped around a ready-made model. If I had done that research on day one, I'd have gotten to the same place in about two days. The difference between weeks of work and a weekend was a search on Hugging Face, the site where the community publishes trained models.

> Prompting doesn't replace thinking. No prompt in the world turns seven layers of brittle rules into a robust solution.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
Claude generated code very fast, but my problem was never typing speed. It was the approach. AI speeds up whatever direction you choose, including the wrong one, and with the same efficiency.

Researching before implementing seems obvious in hindsight. In the heat of the moment, the temptation to solve it by hand is strong, and with an agent at your side it gets even stronger, because it stopped hurting.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
There's the other side, and it's what keeps me optimistic: vibe coding made failure cheap.

Before AI, those five days of OpenCV would have been maybe two or three weeks. At that cost, throwing it away gets hard, because we get attached to what we paid dearly for. With the agent, I discarded the five days without pain, because I knew I could rebuild fast. And I did: the switch to the new model and the reorganization of the whole project fit into a single day, in 15 commits.

<!-- src: 2026/03/05/meu-primeiro-fracasso-com-vibe-code-e-como-consertei-frank-yomik -->
Frank Yomik got back on its feet. In nine days of work it added up to 111 commits, close to 30,000 lines and almost 400 automated tests, and I open-sourced the code. The server needs a [GPU]{.gls key=gpu}, the graphics card, to run the detector, the OCR and the translation.

One problem remained that I don't think I'll solve. It translates each balloon in isolation, without knowing what came before. In Japanese, the sentence often doesn't say whether the person being talked about is a man or a woman, so the captain talks about Nami and the translated balloon comes out "he". A pun that showed up volumes ago and comes back later doesn't stand a chance.

To get this right, the translation would need to carry the previous text as [context]{.gls key=contexto}, and the cost would grow with every chapter. Hold on to this idea of translating without seeing the rest. It comes back at the end of the chapter.

## Four Days in the Dark

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
In April 2026 I was putting together, on Linux, a collection of racing games from old consoles, running on [emulators]{.gls key=emulador}, programs that pretend to be a game console inside the computer. The final boss was Driveclub, for PlayStation 4, running on shadPS4, an open source emulator that doesn't have a stable release yet [@shadps4].

The game ran, but in the night races, in Canada or in Munnar, India, the screen dimmed right after the start until it went black. Only the on-screen overlays survived. Track, car and headlights vanished until the game recovered on its own, about a minute and a half into the race.

I had written this off as an "accepted limitation", but a black screen in the middle of a race is a [bug]{.gls}, a software defect, the kind that makes a game unplayable. I decided to fix it myself.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
I started on a Sunday night and finished Thursday morning. It took 31 phases of investigation, 44 commits on my copy of the emulator and more than 15,000 lines of diagnostic code.

The fix was changing one integer in a config file.

### Information That Exists and Doesn't Help

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
The first thing anyone does is search the internet. Here's what turns up:

- guides from 2025 saying to always turn on a certain sync option;
- guides from a few months later saying to turn off that same option, because it kills performance;
- videos with modified versions of the emulator that nobody documented;
- a thread on the project's forum with three mutually incompatible explanations for the dimming;
- my own notes, written a few days earlier, recommending the opposite of what I found out later.

The information exists, but it's scattered, dated and contradictory.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
Now imagine asking an AI agent to "fix this black screen in Driveclub". It hands you back exactly that material, organized and confident: it suggests a mod that makes the game run in slow motion, it suggests messing with brightness and color correction, which treats the symptom and hides the cause. Its source is the same forums, and the forums were wrong.

### What Was Going On

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
The final explanation, without jargon, is this. Driveclub adjusts the scene's brightness the way the human eye adapts to the dark. The graphics card measures how bright the image is, and the main processor reads that measurement on the next frame to decide the exposure, like a photographer opening or closing the aperture.

The emulator, in its default configuration, didn't synchronize that read. The processor read a stale measurement, zeroed out, and concluded the scene was dark. It opened the aperture, and more, and more, until the image saturated in a way that turned everything black.

There's a stricter sync mode that fixes it. It's tucked away among the advanced options because it's expensive and, on some graphics cards, it tanks performance. Nobody had publicly connected that mode to Driveclub's dimming.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
None of this was knowledge I had on Sunday night. I didn't know the game ran that measure-and-correct loop, or that the option had three modes, or why the middle mode wasn't enough. If I had simply "turned on the option" the forums mentioned, I'd have picked the wrong mode and given up thinking it didn't work.

### Taking Turns

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
I used Claude Code and Codex, and neither of them knew more than I did. They had the same dated forum text anyone can find.

What they did well was aggregate: they read the emulator's code in parallel with me, cross-referenced, compiled, ran tests and parsed logs of dozens of megabytes much faster than I could have alone.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
Neither of them, alone, cracked the problem. Several times Claude Code went in circles, repeating the same hypotheses without bringing anything new. When that happened, I'd spend a few hours in Codex to clear the air, with other ideas and another angle on reading the code, and then go back. The final fix came out of Claude Code, but the journey switched models several times.

There was also something I'd never seen in any agent: Codex started repeating the same "solution" it had just tried, over and over, without noticing what it was doing. I had to kill the session and start a fresh one from scratch.

That says a lot about long sessions. The more the conversation grows, the more the [context window]{.gls key=janela-de-contexto} gets cluttered with earlier attempts (@sec-ch02). The complexity and length of that investigation took down both models, each in its own way.

### The AI Wanted to Stop

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
Over the four days, from eight in the morning to midnight, the AI suggested stopping some four or five times.

The first time, I had a patch that made the game playable, with the darkness reduced to a dim gloom. The agent proposed: *"the game is playable, this is a decent mitigation, we should document and accept"*. I answered that it was playable but it was wrong, and that we were going to keep going.

On Wednesday night, with a much better patch, the suggestion to wrap up and send the contribution to the project came again. There was still darkness on other tracks. We kept going.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
It was the right behavior for a sensible tool. When you have something that works reasonably well, stopping and documenting is often the right call. Codex itself, in a document it wrote about the fix as it stood at that point, called it a mitigation, without pretending it had found the cause.

The difference was that I had a stubborn hunch that something was still wrong. The patches worked, but they required a different tweak for each track and didn't generalize. When the cost of a solution is out of proportion to how much you understand it, something deeper is wrong. That hunch isn't always right, but it deserves to be heard, and the one listening is you.

> AI speeds up the search. Deciding that it isn't over yet, and understanding why, is still a human's job.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
On Thursday morning, tired, I was looking at a list of seven suspects the investigation had turned up. One of them bugged me because it was the only one tied to *when* the information was read, while the others were about *whether* it was written correctly. I switched the sync mode and ran the night race in Canada. The start opened bright, the exposure adjusted as it should, and night fell naturally.

## How You Learn Now

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
A question I hear all the time: "with AI doing everything, how is a junior going to learn?"

The premise is wrong. AI does what you ask it to. It has no initiative about the domain, and it won't tell you "this looks like a broken measurement loop" unless you know that kind of thing exists. If you ask it for the answer directly, it hands you back the forum summary, with a good chance of sending you down the wrong path.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
This is where a lot of people misread me. "Oh, but Akita is a senior, he has more than 25 years of experience, of course he knows." I had never programmed for the PlayStation 4. I'd never looked at that emulator's code, and I didn't know the console's graphics architecture. On Sunday night, I was an absolute junior on the subject.

A senior professional is someone who has been a junior in every topic they master, and who keeps becoming a junior every time they step into a new domain.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
Junior, though, doesn't mean totally green. Years earlier, on my channel, I had made videos explaining how old consoles work under the hood, starting with the NES processor, and how emulators work in general [@akita2020akitando81]. I wasn't going to trip over the basic vocabulary. That old curiosity paid dividends.

### Shoot Everything to Learn the Terrain

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
Through most of the 31 phases, without knowing I was doing it, I used a technique that has a name: shotgun.

During the race, the game used more than a thousand textures, the images that cover the objects in the scene. If the suspicion was "the dimming comes from some texture" and each one took twenty minutes to examine, I'd spend days on a single branch of the tree. And maybe the answer wasn't even there.

Instead, you shoot at all of them at once. You alter every texture in one go, run the game and, in thirty seconds, you know whether any of them affects the dimming. If none did, you've just ruled out the entire category, and you move on to the next.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
I built 32 of these test mechanisms over the course of the investigation, and only one was actually used to find the bug. Even so, every round ruled out an entire family of hypotheses. And meanwhile, I was learning the system: every test that crashed the game told me where something critical lived, every strange color on screen told me which piece fed which.

When you don't know the system, shotgun probes are a map. You don't use them to find the answer. You use them to learn the terrain.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
There's a detail that almost slips by and that matters for the rest of the chapter. Before the first phase, I made sure the defect showed up in 100% of attempts, always with the same timing pattern. If you can't reproduce the problem on demand, you're chasing ghosts, because every experiment needs a before and an after you can measure.

### One Integer and Seven Thousand Lines of Notes

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
The final tally amuses me: more than 50 note documents, almost 7,000 lines of text describing hypotheses and rejections, against one integer in the config. Almost none of the 15,000 lines of code is any use as a contribution to the emulator, because it's instrumentation built for Driveclub under those conditions.

Even so, it wasn't waste. On Sunday I was a complete stranger to that system. By Thursday I wasn't an expert in any of it, but I had become someone reasonably oriented, and that jump was the most valuable thing of the four days.

<!-- src: 2026/04/23/driveclub-shadps4-e-ia-como-aprender -->
That's the difference between knowing the answer and knowing the terrain well enough to recognize the answer when it shows up. The fix had been sitting in the emulator's code for seven months. On Sunday, I had no technical authority whatsoever to say "flip this on and it's fixed".

To the junior who asks me how to learn in the AI era, this is my answer:

- don't just keep asking, dive into the problem;
- use AI to speed up the search, read code alongside you, compile, dig through logs and old forums;
- insist on understanding the why of everything that worked;
- when the AI suggests "let's accept and document", and it will, ask yourself whether you really understood the whole chain.

You'll probably spend four days on something that gets solved with one number. At the end, you'll know a domain you didn't know before.

## Translating in the Dark

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
The third failure is the most stubborn one, and the only one that changed its nature halfway through.

Let's go back to the stubborn project that showed up in passing in @sec-ch03.

For many years I've had a fixed idea: converting NES games to run on the Master System, Sega's console. I always thought the Master System was technically superior, with a much richer color palette. But back then, Nintendo's contracts with game makers required platform exclusivity [@wikipedia2026nes], and the Master System went hungry. It never got a Castlevania, a Mega Man, a Final Fantasy.

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
In theory, it could be done. Any general-purpose computer can run another's program, as long as someone translates the instructions of one processor into the other's. The problem was always the cost. In forums of people who program for these consoles, the long-standing consensus is that automatically converting that code makes the game slower [@nesdev2018sms], slow enough to never pay off.

And the reason is counterintuitive. The Master System's processor has double the clock speed of the NES processor, which looks like headroom. But each of its instructions takes, on average, about 13 cycles, against about 4 on the NES. To do the same general-purpose work, the "faster" processor ends up being the slower of the two.

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
But now there are [frontier models]{.gls key=modelo-de-fronteira}, and I got curious: could brute force, with a good model in the middle, get a better result than the usual naive translation? In late May 2026 I started a project to find out.

The first result was exactly what the theory predicted. I managed to convert the entire Super Mario Bros, with levels, characters, everything. But to make it minimally playable, I had to make the Master System emulator pretend to have a processor five times faster than the original.

I banged my head against Claude, GPT and other models through June and July, and got stuck. In August, I shelved the project.

### The Hand-Made Port

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
While my project sat on the shelf, another developer, who goes by lackoftrack27, published a Super Mario Bros port for the Master System that's on another level [@lackoftrack27smb]. It runs at full speed, with improved graphics that take advantage of the console's palette. It proved my thesis in practice: the Master System can handle it.

But it's a hand reimplementation, optimized for that game, using as reference the original's code as disassembled by the community. And Super Mario, at about 40 kilobytes, is one of the simplest games on the NES.

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
In early September I went back to the project. I loaded lackoftrack27's code into Claude Fable and then into the newly released GPT Astra, and put the two of them to study what he had done that I wasn't doing.

But the change that really unblocked the project was something else.

## The Oracle

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
Blind translation doesn't work. Without a way to measure, the model generated the translated code without knowing whether it was right, or where it was slow. It needed real feedback.

The heart of the project became what I call the [oracle]{.gls key=oraculo}: infrastructure that runs the emulators and measures reality, regardless of what the model thinks. The translator became the easy part.

<!-- src: original -->
The term comes from software testing. An oracle is anything that can tell, from the outside, whether an answer is right: a test, a measurement, a comparison against a trustworthy reference. The model can argue all it wants, it can be convinced, it can swear it fixed things. The oracle doesn't negotiate: either the numbers match, or they don't.

![Without an oracle, the model translates and guesses; with an oracle, the original and the translation run side by side and every difference goes back to the model.](assets/figures/en/ch08-oraculo.png){#fig-ch08-oraculo fig-alt="Two diagrams. On top, 'blind translation': the original game goes into the model, which produces a translation, and an arrow with a question mark points to 'looks right?'. Below, 'translation with an oracle': the original game and the translation run in two emulators side by side, receiving the same button presses; a comparator checks memory on every frame and measures the time spent; the differences go back along an arrow to the model, closing a loop."}

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
In my project, the oracle works in two layers.

In the first, each piece of the game is tested in isolation. The system picks random starting situations, runs the original piece in a reference simulator, runs the translated version and compares the results. Any divergence raises a flag.

In the second, the whole game runs in both versions, side by side, with the same script of button presses, and the memory of the two is compared byte by byte, frame by frame. Later came comparing the image on screen itself. On top of all that, the system measures how much processor time each part consumes.

### What the Measurement Found

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
On Super Mario, every optimization started being measured with the emulator actually running. The May translation used about six times the time the console has to draw each frame. With each step validated by the oracle, that dropped to under two times. It still needs a boost from the emulator, but a much smaller one than at the start.

On Castlevania, the biggest gain came from something nobody would find by reading the code. A single piece, which sat waiting for a signal from the screen and gave up on timeout every single time, was eating almost a quarter of the entire processor. Only the measurement revealed it, and with it the fix was a targeted one.

> A model that generates code in the dark makes big mistakes. A model that can run the target, measure reality and react to what it measured plays in another league.

<!-- src: original -->
It's the same thread as the other two failures. On Frank Yomik, my rules were checked against a small sample of pages I had picked myself, and every new manga was a surprise. On Driveclub, the first thing I did was make sure the defect always showed up, so that every experiment had a before and an after. Where there was an independent measure the agent delivered, and where there wasn't it produced volume.

The automated tests with real data from @sec-ch06 follow the same logic. A test is a small oracle.

### What the Oracle Can't Reach

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
With the oracle in place, the models dissected lackoftrack27's port and answered the doubt that had me stuck. Super Mario fits on the Master System, and my six-times overrun was entirely the cost of translation, with no blame on the game or the hardware.

His biggest gain was in how the data was laid out in memory. He rearranged the information about the objects on screen in a way that makes each access about five times cheaper. But that only works because the programmer knows, from memory, that a certain number in the code is always the index of one of those objects, between zero and six.

An automatic translator has no way to discover that safely by looking at the code alone. It's the most powerful optimization of all, and precisely the one the machine can't prove on its own. It draws the ceiling of what can be automated.

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
Then comes the next wall: the bigger NES games carried extra chips inside the cartridge, which kept swapping which part of the game was visible to the console, because the processor alone couldn't reach all of it. There are several kinds of these chips, and the project implements only two. Castlevania, which uses one of them, already draws the first level and responds to the controller, but still runs slowly. Whether a Super Mario Bros 3 is feasible, I can't say.

<!-- src: 2026/09/07/desafio-pra-ia-converter-roms-de-nes-pra-master-system-sms -->
The path that excites me is for automatic translation to become a baseline: a version that already runs, correct, if slow, which someone then optimizes by hand, game by game, the way lackoftrack27 did. The oracle guarantees the baseline is right. The human hand comes in to do what the machine can't infer.

## When the Machine Can't

<!-- src: original -->
So back to the question that opened the chapter: what happens when the AI can't do it?

In all three cases, the machine did what I asked, and fast. On Frank Yomik, it did a great job at something that shouldn't have been done. On Driveclub, it was a great research assistant that wanted to stop too early. On the Master System, it translated blind until it got a way to measure, and even then it ran into what existed only in a programmer's head.

What was missing in each case came from this side of the table:

- knowing when to abandon an approach and look for what already exists;
- refusing a solution that works without you understanding why;
- giving the agent a way to measure reality, instead of letting it guess;
- bringing the domain knowledge no code carries written down.

The good news is that being wrong got cheap. I threw away five days of work that used to be two or three weeks, and rebuilt everything in one day.

<!-- src: 2026/04/15/como-falar-com-o-claude-code-efetivamente -->
In these three stories, at least, I knew exactly what I was asking for. The most common failure of all starts before that. Every week someone tells me they canceled their subscription to a model because it didn't perform, or that some other model is way better. Anecdote after anecdote of "works for me" against "doesn't work for me".

My first question is always the same: what did you ask it for, exactly?
