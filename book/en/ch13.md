# AI at Home {#sec-ch13}

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
In March 2026, a new mini-PC landed on the shelf of my rack, under the file server. It was a Minisforum MS-S1 Max, with the AMD Ryzen AI Max+ 395 chip, and I bought it for one specific reason: to run AI inside my own house, on my own server, without asking any company for anything.

The opponent was in my home office. My desktop has an RTX 5090, NVIDIA's top consumer graphics card, with 32 GB of memory. I ran the same models on both machines, and the card won by a mile: on the models that fit in it, it was six to seven times faster. A small model the RTX wrote at 155 tokens per second, the mini-PC wrote at 23.

Then I switched models. A 122-billion-[parameter]{.gls key=parametros} one, with 81 GB of weights, ran on the mini-PC at about 19 tokens per second, without sending a single word outside the house. On the RTX 5090, that same model doesn't fit: it would have to leave pieces outside the card and would drop to 2 or 3 tokens per second, which in practice is unusable.

<!-- src: original -->
That comparison sums up almost everything this chapter has to tell. The two questions people ask me most about AI at home are these: can I run a good AI on my own computer? And are the free models as good as the paid ones? Both answers run through something almost nobody explains, which is memory.

## The Bookcase and the Hallway

<!-- src: original -->
Start with the [GPU]{.gls key=gpu}, the graphics card. It was born to draw games: millions of little dots on the screen, each one computed at the same time as all the others. That knack for doing a huge amount of simple math in parallel is exactly what an [LLM]{.gls} needs, and that's why all of AI runs on graphics cards, from OpenAI's server rooms to my home office.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
The card has its own memory, soldered onto it and separate from the computer's, called [VRAM]{.gls key=vram}. That's where the model has to live while it works. The [weights]{.gls key=pesos}, those billions of frozen numbers that hold everything the model learned (@sec-ch01), have to sit entirely inside that memory.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
The difference between the two machines fits in one picture, and it's the most useful thing I learned about AI at home.

To write each token, the model has to consult all of its weights. All of them, for every token. Picture a writer who, to write each word, has to walk to the bookcase and skim the entire encyclopedia before going back to the desk.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
Two measurements decide this writer's life. The first is the size of the bookcase: if the encyclopedia doesn't fit, there's no work at all. The second is the width of the hallway between the bookcase and the desk.

His speed barely depends on how fast he thinks. It depends on how wide the hallway is, and that's called [memory bandwidth]{.gls key=largura-de-banda}: how many gigabytes per second can travel from memory to whatever does the math.

The RTX 5090 has a small bookcase and a very wide hallway, about seven times wider than the mini-PC's, and the speed difference I measured was almost exactly that. The mini-PC is the opposite. Its chip, which the industry nicknamed Strix Halo, uses [unified memory]{.gls key=memoria-unificada}: instead of one memory for the processor and another for the graphics, a single block, 128 GB, that you divide however you like [@amd2025ryzenaimax].

<!-- src: ref:private-notes/server-notes.md -->
On mine, the graphics side got 64 GB, double the card, because the rest of the server needs memory to breathe too.

![Bandwidth decides speed; capacity decides what fits.](assets/figures/en/ch13-banda-e-capacidade.png){#fig-ch13-banda-e-capacidade fig-alt="Two reservoirs, each with a pipe filling a glass of tokens per second. On the left, a small reservoir, RTX 5090: 32 GB, with a wide 1,792 GB/s pipe and the glass nearly full; the 81 GB model block sits outside, because it doesn't fit: fast, but small. On the right, a large reservoir, Strix Halo: up to 128 GB, with the 81 GB model inside and a narrow 256 GB/s pipe; the glass holds only a little at the bottom: everything fits, but slowly."}

> Bandwidth decides speed. Memory size decides what fits. A good machine at home needs both, and today almost none has both at a price a normal person can pay.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
That's why the 122-billion model from the start of the chapter runs on the mini-PC and crawls on the RTX 5090. On the card, part of the encyclopedia would have to sit in a warehouse outside the bookcase, and the back-and-forth to get there, through an even narrower hallway, drags the speed down to a few tokens per second. Too slow to hold a conversation.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
You can shrink the encyclopedia. [Quantization]{.gls key=quantizacao} stores each number with less precision: instead of 16 bits, 8 or 4. It's like rounding every price in a supermarket to the nearest dollar: the list gets much shorter, and the total at the register barely changes.

<!-- src: 2026/04/05/testando-llms-open-source-e-comerciais-quem-consegue-bater-o-claude-opus -->
The rounding has limits. Below 3 bits the model starts talking real nonsense, and for programming I never go below 4.

And the model's weights are only half the bill. So it doesn't have to reread the whole conversation for every word, the model keeps a notebook of notes on everything it has already read, and that notebook also takes up the bookcase, growing along with the conversation. A coding agent, which reads files and command output all the time, fills the notebook fast.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
On a 32 GB card, the choice comes down to this: either the model and the notebook fit whole, at the cost of precision or conversation memory, with the agent summarizing and forgetting all the time (the [compaction]{.gls key=compactacao} from @sec-ch02), or pieces spill out of the bookcase and every response crawls. Neither option is good. On the mini-PC, the notebook has room to spare; what's missing is speed.

<!-- src: 2026/03/31/review-minisforum-ms-s1-max-amd-ai-max-395 -->
Some big models run fast even in the narrow hallway. Those are the [MoE]{.gls key=moe}, short for mixture of experts: a small router picks, for each token, just a few blocks of weights to work, and the writer consults just a few volumes. But the whole encyclopedia still has to fit in the bookcase.

Whoever wants both things, a big bookcase and a wide hallway, ends up looking at a Mac Studio, which by my estimate in March went past 110,000 reais in Brazil in the maximum configuration. The mini-PC cost somewhere between 12,000 and 15,000.

The 19 tokens per second of the 122-billion model are a long way from the experience of using Claude or ChatGPT. But it's local, it's private, and the whole machine draws under 100 watts on my shelf, less than an old incandescent lightbulb.

<!-- src: original -->
The math behind these pictures, from bytes per parameter to the experts that sleep, is in @sec-aptecnico-memoria-local.

## The Price That Doesn't Show Up in the Column

<!-- src: original -->
Before the second question, a distinction the advertising blurs on purpose. [Open model]{.gls key=modelo-aberto} means the weights were published: anyone can download them and run them without asking permission. That's different from running at home, and it's different from being free.

The training data of an open model is almost never public. And a lot of people who use an open model use it through the cloud, paying per token to some company that runs the weights on professional cards.

<!-- src: 2026/06/14/llm-benchmark-kimi-v2-7-code-glm-5-2-minimax-m3-local -->
In the cloud, the answer to the second question changed over the course of the year. My programming [benchmark]{.gls}, which @sec-ch11 takes apart from the inside, measured the Chinese open models climbing generation after generation. In June, GLM 5.2 jumped from 46 to 87 points from one version to the next, the biggest turnaround I had ever measured within a single family.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
In September, on the harder version of the test, GLM 5.3 reached 94 on a flat-rate subscription plan and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash scored 92.5 for a little over a dollar (@sec-ch11). They landed a few points below the seven most expensive models, which tied at the top.

For everyday programming, with nobody sabotaging your code on purpose, the gap between that pack and the most expensive models on the market got small. That's in September 2026, on one specific task. But it got small.

### Twelve Hours of Machine Time

<!-- src: ref:llm-coding-benchmark/docs/success_report.v4.combined.md@HEAD -->
At home, it's another story. In that same September round, two models ran on my hardware.

GLM 4.7 Flash, the 30-billion-parameter one, running on the RTX 5090 in a [quantized]{.gls key=quantizacao} version that fit on the card, was the first local model to complete all seven stages of the test, in 29 minutes. It scored 24 out of 100: it built the requested features and caught almost none of the flaws planted in the code.

Qwen 3.8 27B, a dense model with 27 billion parameters, stalled at the third stage on the RTX 5090. Its reasoning lost coherence after two stages, with nothing to do with running out of memory. On the mini-PC, with more memory and infinite patience, it completed everything and scored 80 points, the level of a mid-table cloud model.

<!-- src: 2026/09/15/novo-llm-benchmark-v4-retestando-todos-llms-parte-2 -->
It took 706 minutes. Nearly twelve hours, for a test that almost every other model on the table finished in the cloud in 80 to 300 minutes.

In the table's cost column, the local Qwen shows up at zero dollars. Anyone who looks only at that column concludes they've found the bargain of the year.

<!-- src: 2026/10/10/qual-modelo-de-decisao-classificador-de-ia-tipo-jev-e-melhor -->
The real cost is something else. It's the machine, which cost thousands of reais, and the electricity. It's the hours I spent configuring drivers and containers to get each new model running, and the work of keeping all of it standing.

When I ran the classifier benchmark in October, I decided not to write "zero" in the column for the local models. I wrote "not measured," because zero would be a lie.

> In the price column, running at home shows up as zero. In real life there's the machine, the electricity and your hours; nobody added them up.

<!-- src: 2026/06/14/llm-benchmark-kimi-v2-7-code-glm-5-2-minimax-m3-local -->
One more reason local lags the cloud: why don't home machines improve at the same pace as the models? Because fast memory and cutting-edge chips go to whoever pays the most, and in 2026 the ones paying the most are the companies buying cards by the container-load to fill data centers. In June, my guess was that good, cheap local hardware, with the bandwidth of a Mac Studio at the price of a mini-PC, only gets here around 2030, when that race slows down.

### Claude at Home

<!-- src: 2026/04/05/testando-llms-open-source-e-comerciais-quem-consegue-bater-o-claude-opus -->
There's a shortcut that shows up on social media every week. Take a small open model, train it on the answers of a big model, and presto: "Claude at home." The technique exists and has a name, [distillation]{.gls key=destilacao}: a smaller model, the student, learns by imitating what a bigger one, the teacher, answers [@hinton2015distilling].

In April I tested one of those, a 27-billion-parameter Qwen 3.5 trained on reasoning traces from Claude Opus 4.6. The code it produced looked like Claude's: the same class organization, the same comments at the top of each file, the same order of things. And it invented library functions that don't exist. In practice, it failed like any plain Qwen, just with prettier handwriting.

<!-- src: 2026/04/05/testando-llms-open-source-e-comerciais-quem-consegue-bater-o-claude-opus -->
That April benchmark was re-audited later, and several errors I had cataloged turned into hits (@sec-ch11 tells that story). The lesson about distillation survived, because it makes sense once you think about how the student learns. It only absorbs what the teacher repeats, and Claude doesn't go around reciting the exact name of every function in every library while it reasons.

The way of writing carries over to the student; the factual memory stays behind. Distillation transfers style. Knowledge, not always.

## The Killer Who Didn't Kill Anyone

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
In January 2026, when Alibaba released Qwen3 TTS, the enthusiast internet had no doubts. It was the "ElevenLabs killer," the one that would dethrone ElevenLabs, the company that dominated synthetic voice. One article from those days said the open model's 3-second voice cloning beat the paid service's [@byteiota2026qwen3tts].

You've already met this model in @sec-ch06: it's the one I picked to voice the newsletter's weekly podcast, mine and M.Arvin's. Unlike a lot of people who shared the demo video, I put the model to work every Monday.

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
Between February 15 and March 30, I made dozens of adjustments to the [TTS]{.gls key=tts}. Silence at the start of the voice sample, because M.Arvin's was clipping the first syllable. Volume, generation order, the word "podcast," which came out "pódcast." Each adjustment was a session of hours listening, regenerating, tweaking a parameter.

The result came out acceptable, in the sense that every episode went on the air without me re-recording anything by hand. But the intonation was kind of dead, the rhythm uniform. Over long stretches you could feel it was a machine talking.

The worst part was English. The podcast covers tech, so "open source," "MCP," "Claude Opus" show up in every conversation, and Qwen3 pronounced all of it with a thick Brazilian accent, an "oh-pen-ee-sohrss-eh" nobody could understand. The fix was telling the LLM that writes the script to swap anglicisms for Portuguese whenever possible. I mutilated the show's vocabulary so the voice could read it.

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
Then there was the operation. Qwen3 is open, but it didn't run in my house: it ran on a rented graphics card in the cloud, which took 5 to 15 minutes to spin up before each generation. Docker, server, weights, GPU account, all of it was mine to maintain.

### One Night in April

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
On the afternoon of April 8, I opened an ElevenLabs account and signed up for the $99-a-month plan. I tested their v3 model, which had come out of its testing phase in February [@elevenlabs2025v3].

Half an hour later I had a proof of concept. About two hours later, the whole podcast had migrated, and that night I shut Qwen3 down.

The comparison looked like this:

- time to generate a 28-minute episode: about 25 to 30 minutes on Qwen3, about 4 on ElevenLabs;
- emotion tags in the middle of the script, like a sigh or sarcasm: Qwen3 doesn't support them, ElevenLabs accepts them and they work in Portuguese;
- operation: on Qwen3, a rented machine to spin up, wait for and watch over; on ElevenLabs, an access key in a configuration variable;
- cost per episode: about 8 cents of GPU on Qwen3, about $2.70 in credits on ElevenLabs, not counting the $99-a-month subscription.

Look at the last item. ElevenLabs costs more than thirty times as much per episode. And it's worth every penny, because the rest of the list was hours of my week.

The 8 cents are April's bill, with the workflow already tuned. In February, when I set it all up (@sec-ch06), I had estimated a lot more. Even that estimate came in under ElevenLabs' $2.70.

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
By my comparison, Qwen3 didn't even beat ElevenLabs' previous model, v2. The "killer" came out of a 5-second video. Production is a 30-minute episode that has to air every Monday, with English technical terms in the middle, and nobody babysitting it.

> A 5-second demo on Twitter is one thing. Production every Monday is a completely different thing, and the headline only tested the first.

### The Paid Service Hallucinates Too

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
The switch didn't solve everything. That same month, I decided to dub into English, with my cloned voice, the 146 episodes of my YouTube channel, about 96 hours of video.

The first discovery was that voice models make things up too. I sent a block of about 1,500 characters, which should have become a minute and a half of speech, and got back nine minutes of audio. The model decided to keep talking on its own, inventing content, the same [hallucination]{.gls key=alucinacao} from @sec-ch04 in audio form. ElevenLabs' own documentation recommends short blocks; I cut them at 700 characters, always at the end of a sentence, and started rejecting any audio that came out much longer than expected.

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
The second discovery was that "done" is a dangerous word. The first pass closed out all 146 episodes, I uploaded everything, and only after listening to the final files did the problems show up:

- opening jingles that vanished or came in twice;
- artificial pauses in the middle of sentences, because I had used the subtitle breaks instead of the paragraphs of the original script;
- clips from other people's videos, inserted in the middle of episodes, that the automatic captions had transcribed wrong.

Each fix meant regenerating almost everything. In the end, I had burned through more than 91% of the 11 million credits on ElevenLabs' business plan.

Even so, the ElevenLabs math still holds. A studio with an actor, direction and review would cost another order of magnitude. But no demo video shows the fourth round of fixes.

<!-- src: 2026/04/09/como-a-elevenlabs-nao-foi-morta-pelo-qwen3-tts -->
The open model has its place. When the data is sensitive and can't leave the house, or when you have an idle graphics card and little recurring budget, it makes sense. For synthetic voice in a serious product, in April 2026, ElevenLabs was still miles ahead. Qwen3 didn't kill anyone.

## The Small Specialist

<!-- src: 2026/10/10/qual-modelo-de-decisao-classificador-de-ia-tipo-jev-e-melhor -->
So far, the tally for AI at home looks bad. The game changes when the task gets small and closed.

In September 2026 a product called Jev, from TypeSafe, became all the rage, sold as a revolution (@sec-ch14 takes the ad apart). Under the marketing, Jev is a [classifier]{.gls key=classificador}, something that has existed for decades. You give it a text, some rules and a list of options, and it picks one option. It's the person in the mailroom sorting envelopes into three bins.

<!-- src: ref:jev-docs/README.md -->
In October, I built my own test for this kind of model: support cases, refunds, moderation and routing, half in English and half in Portuguese, each with the right answer frozen before any model ran. I compared five paid cloud services with eighteen open models running on the mini-PC on the shelf. At the top, on October 12, were two 27-billion-parameter open models, with 99.4% accuracy; Jev itself came right behind, with 97.6%.

<!-- src: 2026/10/10/qual-modelo-de-decisao-classificador-de-ia-tipo-jev-e-melhor -->
The result that interested me most was another one. The best open models with 4 billion parameters, which fit on an ordinary graphics card, tied with OpenAI's decision service, which debuted at 90.5%.

For a closed decision, with a written rule and three or four options, a small, specialized model gets as far as the general-purpose giants.

<!-- src: ref:private-notes/decision-models.md -->
"At home" with an asterisk, of course. The two champions took up almost all of the mini-PC's video memory, had to run alone and took close to a second and a half per decision. The cloud services answered, almost all of them, in under half a second.

<!-- src: 2026/10/10/qual-modelo-de-decisao-classificador-de-ia-tipo-jev-e-melhor -->
The test also showed the limit of small. The smallest model on the list gave 71 guesses with more than 90% confidence, and 48 were wrong. Whoever sets up a system to "only act when the model is sure" is trusting precisely the number that lies the most.

<!-- src: ref:jev-docs/docs/business-summary.md -->
The practical conclusion works for any company being talked into putting AI in the middle of a process. A rule you can write down, like a numeric limit, severity from structured facts or access permission, stays in code, in an "if this, then that" that gets it right 100% of the time and answers instantly. The model comes in only to turn free text into facts: read the customer's messy email and say "data loss, one customer, confirmed." The final decision is made by the code you wrote.

> A decision model that returns an answer in the right format has only proved it can fill out the form. Whether the answer is right is for you to check, with your own cases.

<!-- src: 2026/10/10/qual-modelo-de-decisao-classificador-de-ia-tipo-jev-e-melhor -->
And the price? In the cloud, the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive paid services came to cents per 10 thousand decisions. A million decisions on Jev cost less than 100 reais, at October's exchange rate. Next to the cost of a critical ticket sent to the routine queue, that difference vanishes.

The local model only pays for itself when the machine already exists for some other reason, or when the data can't leave the house. Buying a computer that costs thousands of reais to save about 100 reais per million decisions is buying yourself a problem.

<!-- src: original -->
The full table, the model that got things right like a coin toss and the critical ticket that a big model got wrong are in @sec-aptecnico-classificadores.

## What Fits on the Shelf

<!-- src: original -->
Back to the two questions from the start, with the date alongside, like everything in this book.

Can you run a good AI at home? In October 2026, yes, if the task is small and closed: classifying texts, organizing photos without sending anything outside (that's what Frank Sherlock from @sec-ch07 did), translating the texts the commercial services refused to translate, as in the interlude about twenty years of blogging (@sec-interlude1). For a coding agent working hours on end with a large context, not yet. Either it fits and runs too slowly, or it doesn't fit.

Are the free models as good as the paid ones? The best open models, running in the cloud, pulled up alongside the paid ones on several tasks I measured. At home, on the hardware a normal person can buy, they fall behind, and what holds them back is memory, hallway and the hours of whoever does the configuring.

<!-- src: 2026/06/14/llm-benchmark-kimi-v2-7-code-glm-5-2-minimax-m3-local -->
And that memory shortage has an owner. The same chips that never reach my shelf are going to data centers that already can't keep up with demand, with construction delayed for lack of transformers and years-long waiting lists for electrical components.

<!-- src: 2026/06/11/llm-benchmark-fable-5-e-a-novela-da-anthropic -->
It was in the middle of that squeeze that, in June 2026, one week after confidentially filing to go public, Anthropic put on sale the first model from the family it had itself announced months earlier as too dangerous to release. When I ran that model through my benchmark, it tied with the previous one. The question left hanging was why the story around it was so much bigger than the result.
